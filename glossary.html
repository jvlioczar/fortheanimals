3Rs

The principle of Replacement, Reduction and Refinement in animal experimentation: replacing animals with other methods whenever possible, using the smallest number of animals, and reducing as far as possible the pain and stress of those used.

It was proposed by the British scientists William Russell and Rex Burch in the book The Principles of Humane Experimental Technique (1959). Today it is written into laws such as the 2010 European directive, which states full replacement of animals as its ultimate goal, and Brazil's Arouca Law. Critics say that, in practice, refinement and reduction get more attention than replacement. See Replacement methods, Arouca Law and Animal model.

A life worth living

The idea that welfare should not only avoid suffering but ensure that the animal's life, overall, has more good experiences than bad ones; used by the UK's farm animal welfare council since 2009.

The concept forces us to ask whether an animal's life has a positive balance, not only whether they were spared some pain. Many intensively farmed animals would hardly pass this test. At the same time, sentientists point out that a good life doesn't justify taking it early: death is also a harm. See Welfare range, Harm of death and Five Domains.

Abolitionism

A movement to abolish an institution considered unjust; in the historical sense, the movement to end the enslavement of people, above all Black people, in the 18th and 19th centuries; in animal ethics, the current that advocates ending the use of animals as property, rather than reforms that merely regulate that use.

The original meaning is the fight against slavery. In Britain, campaigners such as Olaudah Equiano, who had been enslaved, and William Wilberforce helped end the slave trade in 1807 and slavery in most of the empire in 1833; in the United States, Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman were among its best-known figures, and slavery was abolished in 1865. In Brazil, the movement included Luiz Gama, who had been enslaved and, as a lawyer, helped free hundreds of people, and led to the Golden Law of 1888, which made Brazil the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery. Many abolitionists, especially Black abolitionists, also fought racism, which is why abolitionism is seen as part of the history of anti-racist struggle; but not all of them did, and the end of slavery came without reparation or integration, which helps explain inequalities that persist. Today the word also names prison abolitionism, which advocates replacing prisons with other responses. In animal ethics, the term was adopted deliberately, especially by the legal scholar Gary Francione from the 1990s, to argue for ending the property status of animals and to criticize welfarism. This analogy with human slavery is controversial, and using it calls for respect for the word's origin. See Abolitionist veganism, Animal slavery, Welfarism and Reformism.

Absent referent

A concept from the author Carol J. Adams: the animal who became food is made absent from the plate, by words and packaging that hide that there was a living individual and a death.

Words like "steak" and "chicken" separate the food from the animal. Rebuilding the link between what we eat and who was killed is a common awareness strategy. See The Sexual Politics of Meat (1990).

Activist burnout

Physical, emotional and mental exhaustion in people devoted to a cause, leading to loss of energy, cynicism and often to leaving activism.

A study of social justice and human rights activists (Chen and Gorski, 2015) pointed to common causes such as neglecting self-care, the emotional weight of the cause and conflict among activists. In animal advocacy, frequent exposure to images of suffering and the feeling that change is slow add to this. The World Health Organization treats burnout as a work-related phenomenon, not as a disease. Looking after yourself is part of sustaining activism over the long term. See Activist fatigue, Vicarious trauma and Learned helplessness.

Activist fatigue

Accumulated tiredness in people who take part in campaigns, debates and actions for a long time, with falling motivation and energy.

It is milder and more common than burnout, but can lead to it without rest. A related concept is compassion fatigue, described among professionals who care for people in distress: the capacity to care diminishes after long exposure to others' pain. Breaks, support from others and realistic goals help prevent it. See Activist burnout.

Activism and the Movement

Adoption

The act of taking on the responsible, permanent care of an animal, usually a rescued or abandoned one, instead of buying it.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Affirmative action

Measures, usually temporary, that seek to correct historical inequalities affecting discriminated groups, giving them more access to opportunities.

The best-known example in Brazil is quotas in public universities, which the Supreme Federal Court ruled constitutional in 2012 and which became mandatory in federal universities by law that same year. The underlying idea is that treating equally those who start from very unequal situations can keep the inequality in place. This connects with the principle of equal consideration of interests: equal consideration doesn't mean identical treatment, but giving the same weight to what each one needs. See Intersectionality, Representation and Equal consideration.

Agribusiness

The set of economic activities tied to large-scale agriculture and livestock farming, from production to processing and marketing.

It brings together distinct activities: veganism opposes livestock farming and fishing, which are part of agribusiness, but not agriculture, which is also part of it.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Agriculture

The cultivation of plants, such as grains, legumes, fruit and fibers, for human food, animal feed or other uses.

Veganism does not oppose agriculture, but the exploitation of animals. Part of agriculture serves to feed animals raised for consumption, and crop production also has impacts that deserve attention.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Agropecuária (agriculture and livestock)

The combination of crop farming and livestock farming, that is, growing plants and raising animals.

The term joins two distinct activities, and veganism treats each differently: it opposes raising and using animals, but not growing plants.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Alienation

A state of psychological or social distance from the real consequences of one's own actions.

In the consumption of animal products, this alienation is made easier by the physical and informational distance between the consumer and the production process: the less visible the suffering, the easier it is to sustain practices that, if confronted directly, would be morally rejected.

Biases, Fallacies and Rhetoric

Altruism

A disposition to act for the benefit of others, even when it brings no advantage to the one who acts, or has some cost.

The term was coined in the 19th century by the philosopher Auguste Comte, as the opposite of egoism. In biology, it also describes animal behavior that helps others, such as warning the group about a predator. In animal ethics, altruism shows up when someone changes habits or devotes time and resources to beings who will never repay them. But the defense of animals doesn't depend on altruism: not causing avoidable harm is a duty, not a generous gesture. See Effective altruism, Egoism (egocentrism), Charity and Compassion.

Normative Ethical Theories

Analogy

An argumentative resource that compares distinct situations because they share relevant characteristics.

Comparisons between animal exploitation and human oppressions, such as slavery, are used by antispeciesists to highlight common structures of domination, but they are also criticized for potentially minimizing the specificity and gravity of the historical human suffering involved.

Anarchism

A political current that rejects hierarchies and unjustified forms of domination, including the State, and proposes organizing society through voluntary association and self-management.

Part of anarchism includes animals: Élisée Reclus defended vegetarianism, and veganarchism, a term coined by Brian A. Dominick (1995), unites veganism and anarchism.

Anesthesia

A medical procedure to suppress the sensation of pain during interventions; its use (or absence) in livestock procedures is a central topic in welfare debates.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal

A multicellular living being of the kingdom Animalia, capable of movement and, in most cases, possessing a nervous system; a biological term that includes humans, although it is popularly used to refer only to nonhumans.

This everyday separation between "humans" and "animals" reinforces, in common language, a moral hierarchy between species that biology itself does not support.

Sentience, Consciousness and Cognition

Animal advocacy

Legal and political work aimed at defending the interests and rights of animals before courts, legislators and public bodies.

Part of this advocacy seeks not only to improve conditions of exploitation, but to recognize animals as subjects of rights of their own, and not merely as objects of indirect protection.

Animal Law and Legislation

Animal behavior

The scientific study (ethology) of animals' actions and reactions, used as indirect evidence of their mental states.

In practice, it is the main source of empirical evidence for inferring suffering and welfare in species whose subjectivity cannot be directly verified.

Sentience, Consciousness and Cognition

Animal cloning

Producing an animal genetically identical to another, from the genetic material of a body cell of the original animal.

The first mammal cloned from an adult cell was Dolly the sheep, born in 1996 at the Roslin Institute in Scotland after hundreds of attempts. The technique still has a low success rate: many embryos fail to develop, and many animals are born with health problems or die young. It is used in research, in animal farming, in conservation programs and, commercially, to "clone" pet dogs and cats, which requires surrogate mothers and many animals undergoing procedures. See Artificial reproduction and Animal model.

Applied Ethics, the Future and Technology

Animal cognition

The study of the mental capacities of non-human animals, such as memory, planning and problem solving.

Recent discoveries in this area have revealed, in several species, capacities once attributed exclusively to humans, which weakens criteria for moral exclusion based on supposed cognitive inferiority.

Sentience, Consciousness and Cognition

Animal confinement

A farming system in enclosed, restricted spaces (e.g., CAFO, Concentrated Animal Feeding Operation).

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal consciousness

An extension of the concept of consciousness applied specifically to non-humans; it discusses whether, and to what degree, different species have their own subjective experience and how this can be scientifically inferred.

Given scientific uncertainty on this topic, a sentientist perspective tends to adopt the precautionary principle: granting moral consideration whenever there is a reasonable probability of consciousness, rather than demanding absolute certainty before acting.

Sentience, Consciousness and Cognition

Animal cruelty

The act of inflicting unnecessary suffering on an animal; a legal and moral category central to the first protection laws of the 19th century, predating the very concept of 'animal rights'.

The criterion of 'necessity' used to define cruelty tends, in practice, to exclude the suffering caused by legalized industries such as livestock farming, which reveals the limits of this concept when not accompanied by a broader reflection on the moral status of animals.

Animal Law and Legislation

Animal emotions

Affective states, such as fear, anger, joy, grief or the urge to play, experienced by nonhuman animals and studied by ethology and affective neuroscience.

For a long time science avoided talking about emotions in animals, partly because of the legacy of Descartes, who saw animals as machines. Charles Darwin had already devoted a book to the expression of emotions in humans and animals, in 1872. The neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp, who coined the term affective neuroscience, identified basic emotional systems in ancient brain regions that mammals share. Knowing that an animal can feel fear or joy changes the moral question: avoiding physical pain is not enough. See Animal-machine, Positive experiences and Suffering.

Animal entertainment

The use of animals in circuses, zoos, water parks and similar venues.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal ethics

The philosophical field devoted to the moral status of nonhuman animals and to how they ought to be treated.

Its development has been central to exposing speciesism as a moral bias as arbitrary as racism or sexism.

Technical Fields Related to Animals

Animal experimentation

The use of animals in scientific research, product safety testing, teaching and the production of biological substances, such as vaccines and antibodies.

An estimate published in 2019 calculated that about 190 million animals were used in laboratories worldwide in 2015, mostly mice, rats, fish and birds. Experimentation is regulated by laws that require justification and pain control, but allow procedures that cause severe suffering. Supporters point to contributions to medicine; critics question how well the results transfer to humans and advocate animal-free methods. See 3Rs, Animal model, Arouca Law and Replacement methods.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal exploitation

An umbrella term for any instrumental use of animals for human benefit.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal fighting

Forced confrontation between animals (roosters, dogs) for entertainment and betting, usually illegal.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal habeas corpus

A legal instrument used in test cases to challenge the captivity of animals (e.g., great apes), without going into the names of specific cases.

Its use reflects an attempt to break with the logic that only humans can be subjects of a legal instrument historically reserved for the protection of individual liberty.

Animal Law and Legislation

Animal Holocaust

An expression used by part of the animal movement to describe the industrial-scale killing of animals, compared with the Holocaust.

The comparison appears in the work of the Jewish writer Isaac Bashevis Singer, who called the life of animals "an eternal Treblinka", and in Charles Patterson's book Eternal Treblinka (2002). It is highly controversial: Jewish organizations and part of the animal movement itself reject it, considering that it trivializes the genocide of people. PETA's "Holocaust on your plate" campaign was banned in Germany, and the European Court of Human Rights upheld the ban in 2012. The guide discusses this controversy in the argument "Comparing animal farming to slavery or the Holocaust is offensive". See Animal slavery.

Animal language

Forms of communication used by nonhuman animals, such as vocalisations, gestures and chemical signals, studied to understand the cognition of different species.

Findings in this area have progressively narrowed the gap that was once assumed to exist between human communication and that of other species.

Sentience, Consciousness and Cognition

Animal law

The branch of law that studies rules about non-human animals: their legal status, their protection and disputes over their use.

It is a field of study and practice, like environmental or consumer law, not a moral position: one can study animal law without holding that animals have rights. An important part of the field, however, argues for going beyond protection against cruelty and recognizing animals as subjects of rights, breaking with their status as property. Not to be confused with animal rights, which are the rights animals themselves would have. See Animal rights and Animal protection legislation.

Animal legal personhood

A debate about recognising animals as subjects of rights, and not merely property. It is defended by antispeciesist jurists as a necessary paradigm shift to break with the historical reduction of animals to the status of property.

Animal liberation

Both a philosophical concept and the tactic of directly rescuing animals from exploitation.

Activism and the Movement

Animal model

An animal used in research to represent a biological process or a human disease, such as mice genetically altered to develop a type of cancer.

The choice of species often follows the Krogh principle, from the Danish physiologist August Krogh: for each problem there is an animal in which it can be studied most conveniently. Being convenient doesn't guarantee that the result holds for humans. Most drugs that pass preclinical testing fail in human trials, through lack of efficacy or safety problems, and differences between species are one of the causes discussed. Supporters point out that animal models have contributed to medical advances; critics advocate methods based on human biology. In 2022, the United States stopped requiring animal tests for approving new drugs. See Animal experimentation, Replacement methods and 3Rs.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal nutrition

The field that studies the nutritional needs of non-human animals and how to meet them through feeding.

In animal farming, it focuses on making animals grow and produce more at the lowest cost, measuring feed conversion, how much feed becomes meat, milk or eggs. This conversion reveals an inefficiency: the animal spends most of what it eats on living, and only part becomes food for people. The same field also serves welfare, when it looks after the health of companion, rescued and sanctuary animals, and it is the field that studies whether foods without animal ingredients meet the needs of dogs and cats. See Nutrition, Feed, Vegan pet food and Zootechnics (animal science).

Technical Fields Related to Animals

Animal population control

A set of methods for reducing the number of animals in a population, ranging from killing (culling, hunting, poisoning) to non-lethal methods such as neutering and contraception.

For stray dogs and cats, trap-neuter-return (TNR) is an alternative to capture and killing. In wild animals, contraceptive vaccines are already used, for example in wild horses in the United States. From a sentientist point of view, non-lethal methods are preferable when they work, and the question is not only how many animals there are, but how they live and die. Killing is often defended as cheaper or faster, but in many cases the population grows back. See Castration, Invasive alien species and Pests.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal protection legislation

The body of laws and rules that protect animals against cruelty and regulate their use, in areas such as farming, transport, slaughter, research and entertainment.

Most of this legislation is welfarist: it limits how animals are used, but doesn't ban using them. Some rules go further and recognize animals as sentient beings, such as the Treaty of Lisbon in the European Union. See Animal law, Statute, Sentience in law and Welfarism.

Animal protein

Protein derived from animal tissues or secretions, distinct from plant protein in amino acid composition.

Animal psychology

The study of the mind and behavior of non-human animals, such as perception, learning, memory, emotions and social relationships; also called comparative psychology.

For a long time the field was shaped by behaviorism, which studied only observable behavior and avoided talk of mind, often with experiments that caused suffering, such as the learned helplessness experiments on dogs. Today, together with ethology and the science of animal cognition, it provides much of the evidence that many animals feel fear, pleasure and stress, learn, and have complex social lives. There is also an applied side, which deals with behavior problems in companion and captive animals. Not to be confused with the psychology that studies humans' relationship with animals, such as the meat paradox. See Animal cognition, Ethology, Animal emotions and Morgan's canon.

Technical Fields Related to Animals

Animal rescuers (Brazil's "protetores de animais")

In Brazil, people who rescue, treat, neuter and rehome abandoned or at-risk animals, above all dogs and cats, often on their own; called independent rescuers when they work without an organization.

The Portuguese term, literally "animal protectors", can be confusing, because "protecting animals" could include all of them, but in Brazilian usage it usually refers to companion and street animals. The tradition goes back a long way: the União Internacional Protetora dos Animais (UIPA) was founded in São Paulo in 1895. Today much of the work is done by individuals, mostly women, who take animals into their homes, pay for vet care and food out of their own pockets and organize adoption fairs, at a high financial and emotional cost. The closest English terms are animal rescuer or independent rescuer, and someone who takes animals in temporarily is a foster carer; "animal protection" in English usually covers all animals, including those raised for food. Being a rescuer doesn't imply being vegan, and many rescuers eat meat; the reverse also holds, because veganism doesn't replace caring for the animals who already need help. Both kinds of work start from the same concern, and each can learn from the other. See Rescue, Adoption, Castration, Pet and Activism.

Activism and the Movement

Animal rights

Rights that non-human animals would have, in the moral or legal sense, such as not being killed, confined or used as property.

The theory of animal rights took shape with the philosopher Tom Regan, in The Case for Animal Rights (1983), for whom animals who are subjects-of-a-life have intrinsic value and cannot be treated as mere resources. It differs from welfarism, which accepts using animals as long as suffering is reduced. Not to be confused with animal law, the branch of law that studies rules about animals: one thing is the field of study, another is the thesis that animals have rights. See Animal law, Rights, Intrinsic value and Abolitionism.

Animal slavery

An expression used by part of the animal movement to describe the condition of animals treated as property: bought, sold, confined, forced to work or to breed, and killed when they stop being profitable, compared with human slavery.

The comparison was developed in Marjorie Spiegel's book The Dreaded Comparison (1988), and is discussed critically by Black authors such as Aph Ko and Syl Ko. Those who defend it compare structures, such as ownership of bodies and the denial of interests, and don't claim the suffering is equal. Those who criticize it point out that it can erase the history and the consequences of the enslavement of Black people, which are still present. The guide discusses this controversy in the argument "Comparing animal farming to slavery or the Holocaust is offensive" and in "Stop saying". See Animal Holocaust and Animals as property.

Animal theory of mind

The capacity to attribute mental states to oneself and to others; studied in primates, corvids and cetaceans as an indication of cognitive complexity. Evidence of theory of mind in many species reinforces the idea that cognitive complexity is not exclusively human, undermining one of the traditional arguments of speciesism.

Animal traction

The use of an animal's physical strength to pull loads or vehicles.

Animal trafficking

The illegal trade in wild or domestic animals, often involving capture, transport and cruel conditions.

Animal training

The process of training an animal to perform specific behaviors through conditioning; coercive methods are debated ethically.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal Use Ethics Committee (CEUA)

A committee that reviews and approves, or rejects, an institution's research and teaching projects involving animals; in Brazil, mandatory under the Arouca Law, with equivalents in other countries, such as IACUCs in the United States.

The committee must check whether the use of animals is justified, whether the number of animals is the minimum needed and whether pain will be controlled. Studies of committees of this kind point to limits: they are generally made up mostly of researchers from the institution itself, and they rarely reject projects. Including outside members, such as representatives of animal protection societies, is one way of balancing this review. See Arouca Law, 3Rs and Animal experimentation.

Animal Law and Legislation

Animal welfare

The state of an animal as regards their health, what they feel and their ability to live according to their nature; also the scientific and ethical field that assesses and seeks to improve that state, especially in animals under human care.

The scientist David Fraser described three ways of thinking about welfare: biological functioning (health, growth), affective states (what the animal feels) and natural living (being able to express their behaviors). The three don't always go together: a broiler chicken can grow fast and be in pain. For a sentientist ethics, what the animal feels is central. Animal welfare as a science is not the same as welfarism, the political position that accepts the use of animals as long as they are treated well. See Five Domains, Coping and Welfarism.

Animal welfare science

An applied subfield of biology that investigates physiological and behavioral indicators to measure animal welfare.

Its indicators are often used to justify reforms within systems of exploitation, which creates tension between welfarists, who seek to optimize them, and abolitionists, who question the legitimacy of using the animal at all.

Technical Fields Related to Animals

Animal-assisted therapy

The use of animals, such as dogs, horses and cats, in health treatment, rehabilitation or education, under professional supervision.

There is evidence of benefits for some people, such as reduced anxiety, although many studies are small. The ethical question is the animal's side: they need rest, the ability to refuse contact and not to be exposed to constant stress. Practices such as therapy with captive dolphins are criticized because they depend on capturing or confining animals who suffer in those conditions. See Companion animal, Animal welfare and Animal entertainment.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal-drawn cart (carroça, Brazilian usage)

A vehicle pulled by animals, used to carry goods or people.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Animal-machine

A view associated with the philosopher René Descartes, in the 17th century, that animals are mindless automata whose cries and movements are merely mechanical reactions, without feeling.

This idea helped justify experiments on living animals with no concern for pain, and shaped Western science for centuries. Historians debate how far Descartes himself denied all sensation to animals, but the doctrine associated with him was used in exactly that way. Today it is rejected by biology and neuroscience. See Evolutionary continuity, Animal emotions and Vivisection.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Animalism (in the sense of animal advocacy)

A generic term for the set of ideas, movements and practices aimed at defending animals, ranging from welfarism to abolitionism.

Because it covers positions as different as welfarism and abolitionism, the term is criticized by stricter antispeciesists for blurring relevant strategic and ethical differences between these currents.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Animals as persons

A legal and philosophical proposal to recognize certain animals as nonhuman persons, subjects of basic rights, and not mere objects.

Restricting this proposal only to species with high cognitive complexity is criticized by sentientists, who argue that the capacity to suffer, not intelligence, should be the relevant criterion for recognizing basic rights.

Animal Law and Legislation

Animals as property

The predominant legal status of animals in most legal systems, which classifies them as movable goods that can be possessed, bought and sold.

It is the central target of the abolitionist critique, which argues that, as long as animals are legally property, their fundamental interests can always be legally subordinated to the economic interests of their owners.

Anthropocentric environmentalism

An environmentalist view that protects the environment because it matters to human beings, which gives it instrumental value.

It already recognizes that individuals matter, people, so it has to explain why it would leave out the other animals who also feel.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Anthropocentric speciesism

A variant of speciesism that specifically privileges the human species as the supreme criterion of value, distinct from broader forms of species-based discrimination.

It is the most common form of speciesism, present in most cultural and institutional practices that treat human interests as automatically superior to those of any other species.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Anthropocentrism

The view that places the human being as the center and measure of moral value.

It is the worldview that antispeciesism and sentientism most directly contest, because it assigns moral value on the basis of species, and not of the capacity to suffer.

Anthropomorphism

The attribution of human characteristics, emotions or intentions to nonhuman animals; it can be a useful tool of empathy or a source of interpretive error, depending on the scientific context.

Categorically rejecting any attribution of mental states to animals, out of fear of anthropomorphism, can fall into the opposite bias: systematically underestimating the mental life of other species.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Anti-ableist veganism

A current that links animal liberation to that of people with disabilities and criticizes the use of cognitive ability as a measure of worth.

Sunaura Taylor, in Beasts of Burden (2017), is a reference.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Anti-vegan

A person who opposes veganism, often actively; also the adjective for what goes against veganism, such as a speech, a campaign, a law or a joke.

Not to be confused with non-vegan: someone who isn't vegan isn't anti-vegan for that reason. An anti-vegan is someone who rejects veganism, mocks vegans or works against the cause, for example with campaigns to restrict vegan products. The hostility is measurable: in a 2017 study, the psychologists Cara MacInnis and Gordon Hodson found that vegans were rated as negatively as, or more negatively than, several other social groups. This guide treats anti-vegan arguments in their strongest version, with what they get right and where they fail, because answering those who disagree well is more useful than dismissing them. See Anti-veganism, Non-vegan and Carnist.

Activism and the Movement

Anti-veganism

The set of positions, discourses, campaigns and measures that oppose veganism, as a philosophy or as a practice.

It ranges from jokes and stereotypes to industry campaigns and rules that hinder vegan products, such as the 2017 ruling of the Court of Justice of the European Union that reserved names like "milk" and "cheese" for animal products. The most common anti-vegan arguments are nutritional, cultural and economic. The nutritional ones have lost ground: bodies such as the US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics consider well-planned vegan diets appropriate at all stages of life, though some call for more caution in childhood. See Anti-vegan, Non-vegan and Carnism.

Antienvironmentalism

Organized opposition to environmental protection policies and movements, often in the name of economic growth, which includes denying or downplaying problems such as climate change.

Not every criticism of an environmental measure is antienvironmentalism: someone starting from sentientism, for example, questions measures that harm animals without denying environmental problems.

Activism and the Movement

Antimicrobial resistance

The ability of bacteria and other microorganisms to survive drugs that used to kill them, which makes infections harder to treat.

One of the causes is the mass use of antibiotics in animal farming, often to prevent diseases favored by confinement or to speed up growth. Estimates published in the journal *Science* in 2019 indicate that about three quarters of the antimicrobials sold worldwide are used in farmed animals. The World Health Organization considers resistance one of the greatest threats to global health, and the European Union banned the use of antibiotics as growth promoters in 2006. It is a public health argument that adds to the ethical one. See Industrial livestock farming, Factory farm and Zoonosis.

Livestock, Exploitation and Related Practices

Antispeciesism

A position and movement that actively opposes speciesism, defending the equal consideration of interests across species.

Not every antispeciesist necessarily defends the end of all use of animals, a position more associated with abolitionism; in the strict sense, antispeciesism is the rejection of moral discrimination based on species.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Antivivisectionism

The movement that opposes vivisection and, more broadly, experimentation on animals; organized mainly from the 19th century in the United Kingdom.

The Irish writer Frances Power Cobbe founded one of the first antivivisection societies in 1875, and in 1876 the United Kingdom passed the first law regulating experiments on animals. The movement brought together many women and had links with women's suffrage. Today part of it calls for an end to all experimentation and part for gradual replacement by animal-free methods. See Vivisection, Animal experimentation and Replacement methods.

Activism and the Movement

Apathy

Emotional or motivational indifference in the face of another's suffering.

Carnism

The belief system that makes eating certain animals seem normal, natural and inevitable, and that hides the fact that it is a choice.

The term was coined by the social psychologist Melanie Joy in 2001 and spread through her book Why We Love Dogs, Eat Pigs, and Wear Cows (2009). Domestication

The process, over many generations, by which animal populations came to live under human control and were modified by artificial selection; the dog was first, more than 15,000 years ago, and cattle, sheep, goats and pigs were domesticated around 10,000 years ago.

Domestication changed bodies and behavior: hens that lay around 300 eggs a year, broiler chickens that grow so fast that many have leg problems, cows with enormous udders. A common argument says these animals exist thanks to us and so we may use them. But having brought someone into existence doesn't give the right to make them suffer. For veganism, ending exploitation doesn't mean releasing domesticated animals, but no longer breeding them for use and caring for those who already exist. Double standard

Applying different criteria to similar cases, as suits whoever is judging. The Portuguese expression is "dois pesos e duas medidas" (two weights and two measures).

It is a form of inconsistency and the opposite of moral universality. In the treatment of animals, it is very common: the same act is a crime against a dog and routine against a pig, other peoples' hunting is barbaric and ours is tradition, the suffering of a cute animal moves us and that of a fish goes unnoticed. Edge cases

Extreme or rare situations at the boundary of a rule, such as the desert island with no other food or the choice between saving a dog and a child.

Used well, they test whether a principle holds in every case or needs adjusting, and show where a rule's limits lie. The argument from marginal cases, for example, uses humans with limited capacities to test whether rationality is really the criterion for moral consideration. Used badly, they turn the exception into the rule: from the fact that someone would eat an animal on a desert island it doesn't follow that they may buy one at the supermarket, where there is no necessity at all. The useful question about an edge case is: what does it show about the ordinary case? See Hypothetical scenarios and Argument from marginal cases.

Empirical knowledge

Knowledge gained through experience and observation, which can be tested against evidence; in Brazilian schooling, also everyday practical knowledge without systematic method, as opposed to scientific knowledge.

In philosophy, it is contrasted with a priori knowledge, which doesn't depend on experience, like that of logic and mathematics. Euthanasia

The controlled induction of an animal's death, generally to spare it from suffering; distinct from slaughter, which aims at consumption or production.

It is truly euthanasia only when death is in the animal's own interest, as in untreatable suffering. Evidence

Information obtained by observation, measurement, experiment or record that makes a claim more or less likely.

Not all evidence weighs the same. In general, personal accounts count less than observational studies, which count less than controlled experiments, and reviews that pool many studies count more than a single study; the consensus of scientific bodies shows where the evidence converges. Having little evidence is not the same as having evidence against. And the demand applies to both sides: animal advocates also need to back their claims about health, the environment and data with evidence. Evidence-based practice

A way of deciding that uses the best available evidence about what works, rather than relying only on intuition, tradition or preference.

The idea took hold in medicine in the 1990s, with evidence-based medicine, and spread to other fields such as education and public policy. In animal advocacy, research organizations study which campaigns, messages and strategies reduce suffering the most. It requires humility: evidence about strategies is limited and changes over time. And it doesn't replace ethics: evidence helps choose the means, but the reason to defend animals comes from sentience. See Evidence, Rationalist (or evidence-based) veganism and Effective altruism.

Evolutionary continuity

The idea, set out by Charles Darwin, that the mental differences between humans and other animals are differences of degree, not of kind, because all species descend from common ancestors.

If the human body and brain come from a long history shared with other animals, it would be odd for the capacity to feel to have appeared out of nowhere in our species alone. That is why evolutionary continuity is one of the pillars for attributing pain and emotion to other vertebrates, and increasingly to some invertebrates. It doesn't say that all animals feel the same, only that the question has to be answered with evidence, species by species, and not by a line drawn in advance. See Sentience, Animal consciousness and Animal cognition.

Externality

A cost or benefit that an activity causes to other people, or other beings, and that doesn't show up in the product's price.

In the production of meat, milk and eggs, the most cited externalities are water and air pollution, greenhouse gas emissions, deforestation, antibiotic resistance and the risk of new diseases from animals. Extrinsic harm

Harm that is not bad in itself, but because of what it prevents or causes; the main case is deprivation, when something good someone would have had is taken away.

Guerrilla marketing

An unconventional, low-cost form of promotion that uses creativity, surprise and public spaces to draw attention, instead of paid advertising.

The term was popularized by Jay Conrad Levinson in his book Guerrilla Marketing (1984), written for small businesses. Social movements adopted the same logic. In the animal cause, a well-known example is the Cube of Truth, by the group Anonymous for the Voiceless, founded in Australia in 2016: masked activists, standing in a cube formation, hold screens showing footage from farms and slaughterhouses while others talk to passers-by who stop. Projections, stickers, art interventions and online actions also fall into this category. Creativity doesn't excuse dishonesty: footage must be genuine and in context, and the effect of each action should be evaluated, not assumed. See Guerrilla tactics, Manipulation, Street activism and Vegan witness (bearing witness).

Activism and the Movement

Guerrilla tactics

In activism, quick, cheap and surprising actions carried out by small groups to draw attention where and when it isn't expected, such as projections on buildings, banners, street interventions and flash demonstrations.

The term comes from irregular warfare: "guerrilla" means "little war" in Spanish and became popular during the resistance to Napoleon's occupation of Spain in the early 19th century, with small, mobile groups that avoided direct confrontation with a larger army. In activism, the idea is to make up for a lack of resources with creativity and surprise. There is an important difference between legal, peaceful actions and actions that damage property or intimidate people. And the effect isn't always what was intended: a 2020 study by Matthew Feinberg, Robb Willer and Chloe Kovacheff found that protest tactics seen as extreme tend to reduce public support for the cause, even when they draw more attention. See Guerrilla marketing, Direct action, Protest and Vigil.

Guinea pig

The domestic guinea pig (Cavia porcellus), a small rodent domesticated in the Andes thousands of years ago; by extension, any animal used in experiments and, figuratively, any person subjected to a test.

The guinea pig was one of the first laboratory animals: in the late 18th century, Lavoisier and Laplace used one to measure the heat produced by the body, and in the early 20th century it was central to research on scurvy, because, like humans, it can't make its own vitamin C. Today mice and rats are used far more, but the name stuck as a synonym for an experimental animal. Guinea pigs are also kept as companion animals and, in the Andes, raised as food. The figurative sense, as in "being used as a guinea pig", follows the same pattern as other speciesist terms: using someone in tests without their consent is seen as abuse when it is a person, but as normal when it is the animal who gave the expression its name. In Portuguese the word is "cobaia". See Animal experimentation, Animal model, 3Rs and Speciesist term.

Harm

What leaves a being worse off than they would otherwise be; in the moral sense, a setback to someone's interests.

There are two ways of causing harm: bringing about something bad, such as pain, fear or disease, which is intrinsic harm, or taking away something good, such as freedom, companionship or the rest of one's life, which is extrinsic harm, or harm by deprivation. In the moral sense, only those who have interests, that is, sentient beings, can be harmed: a car can be damaged, but there is no one for whom that is bad. In law, the word has its own uses, as in material and moral damages. See Intrinsic harm, Extrinsic harm, Harm of death, Maleficence and Benefit.

Criteria and Moral Status

Harm of death

The harm death does to the one who dies, even when it is quick and painless.

The most widely accepted explanation is deprivation, defended by the philosopher Thomas Nagel in 1970: death is an extrinsic harm, because it takes from the one who dies all the good experiences they would still have had. Epicurus disagreed: for him, death "is nothing to us", because when it comes there is no one left to suffer it. Among those who accept deprivation, there is disagreement about the size of the harm: for Jeff McMahan, it depends on how psychologically connected a being is to their own future, which would lead some to attribute a smaller harm to animals; others reply that the life lost is the same, with or without plans. In any version, killing a healthy animal, even painlessly, takes the rest of their life from them, and in farming most are killed very young: broiler chickens at a few weeks old. See Harm, Extrinsic harm, Intrinsic harm and Interest.

Healthy diet

A diet that provides the nutrients and energy the body needs and helps prevent disease, based on varied and minimally processed foods.

The World Health Organization recommends basing one's diet on fruit, vegetables, legumes, nuts and whole grains, with at least 400 g of fruit and vegetables a day, and limiting free sugars, saturated fats, trans fats and salt. Health and ethics are different questions: the moral question is whether it's necessary to exploit animals to eat well, and the answer of the main nutrition bodies is that it isn't. Human being

A member of the species Homo sapiens, a primate that emerged in Africa about 300,000 years ago and is the only living species of the genus Homo; also called a human.

Biologically, a human being is an animal, and the opposition "humans and animals" is a convention of language. The word "humane" took on a moral sense of kindness and compassion, and "inhumane" became a synonym for cruel, as if mistreatment were typical of other animals. Defending animals doesn't diminish human rights; it extends the same logic. See Animal, Nonhuman animal, Anthropocentrism and Speciesism.

Hypothetical scenarios

Imagined situations used to test ideas and principles; when built for this purpose in philosophy, they are called thought experiments.

They are used on both sides of the debate, and can help or hinder. They help when they isolate what matters morally: vegans ask, for example, whether extraterrestrials more intelligent than us could breed and eat us, to test whether intelligence justifies exploiting those who are less intelligent; or whether an AI that felt pain would deserve consideration, to test whether the criterion is sentience rather than species or the material a being is made of. They hinder when the scenario is impossible or poorly described and hides premises, when an intuition about an artificial case is treated as proof, or when it serves to dodge the real decision, as in "what if they discover plants feel?" faced with a choice made today. This also applies to animal advocates: an analogy with extraterrestrials that ignores relevant differences is unconvincing. Impartiality

The principle of giving equal weight to similar interests, without favoring anyone for reasons that don't matter morally, such as who they are, where they are or what group they belong to.

It takes several forms in philosophy: in the dictum attributed to Bentham that each is to count for one and none for more than one, in Henry Sidgwick's "point of view of the universe" and in John Rawls's veil of ignorance, which asks us to choose rules without knowing what place we would occupy in society. Peter Singer extended the idea to animals with equal consideration of interests: species, like race or sex, doesn't change the weight of a pain. Impartiality doesn't forbid all special care for those close to us, and there is a long debate about how much partiality it allows. See Partiality, Equal consideration, Temporal impartiality and Moral universality.

Criteria and Moral Status

Instrumentalization

Treating someone merely as a means to others' ends, ignoring their own interests; also using a cause or a person for ends foreign to it.

The idea comes from Immanuel Kant, for whom people must always be treated also as ends, and never merely as means. Using someone as a means isn't always wrong: when we buy bread, the baker serves us, but consents and is paid. The problem is using someone merely as a means, without consent and against their interests. Kant didn't extend this principle to animals, but philosophers such as Tom Regan argued that every subject-of-a-life has value of their own and can't be treated as a mere resource. That is what happens in animal farming, where the animal's whole life is organized around what they produce. In the political sense, people also speak of instrumentalizing a cause, for example when animal protection is used only to attack the practices of minority groups, while those of the majority are left out. See Instrumental value, Intrinsic value, Objectification and Subjects-of-a-life.

Interest

What matters to a being, because it affects their life and well-being for better or worse; having interests, such as avoiding pain and staying alive, is the minimum criterion for deserving moral consideration.

Philosophers distinguish "taking an interest in" something, which is wanting it, from something "being in someone's interest", which is being good for them: an animal may not want a vaccine, and it can still be in their interest. In the moral sense, only sentient beings have interests, because only to them can anything matter. A plant needs water, but there is no one for whom lacking it is bad. Under equal consideration, similar interests count equally, whatever the species. See Equal consideration, Sentience and Animal welfare.

Intrinsic harm

Harm that is bad in itself for the one who suffers it, regardless of its consequences, such as pain, fear, distress and boredom.

It is the easiest kind of harm to recognize, because it is felt: an animal mutilated without anaesthesia or confined in a space where they can barely move suffers an intrinsic harm. Invasive alien species

A species taken by humans outside its native range that spreads and causes harm to ecosystems, other species or the economy, such as the European wild boar in Brazil.

These species are usually fought by large-scale killing, which creates a conflict between the conservationist view, which values species and ecosystems, and the sentientist view, which looks at each individual. Learned helplessness

A state in which a person stops trying to change a situation because they have come to believe that nothing they do will make a difference.

The concept comes from experiments by Martin Seligman and Steven Maier in the 1960s with dogs given electric shocks, an example of the very animal suffering the guide discusses. Logic

The branch of philosophy that studies correct reasoning: when a conclusion follows from the premises.

An argument is valid when, if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true too; and it is sound when, besides being valid, its premises are actually true. Every chapter of the guide runs this test: it reconstructs the strongest version of the objection and shows whether it fails on validity or on a premise. Logic alone doesn't settle facts or values: it shows what follows from what one accepts, so it needs premises supported by evidence. See Argument, Fallacies, Logical consistency and Evidence.

Logical consistency

The absence of contradiction between the statements of an argument or of a set of beliefs.

Consistency guarantees internal logic, but not external truth: a set of beliefs can be perfectly consistent and still false, if it starts from a false premise. So, besides consistency, a good argument needs true premises, supported by evidence. In debate, pointing out an inconsistency shows that some belief has to change, but doesn't by itself say which. It differs from moral consistency, which asks us to apply the same principles to similar cases. See Logic, Moral consistency and Evidence.

The evil or harm done to someone; the opposite of benefit.

In bioethics, the principle of non-maleficence, "first, do no harm", is one of the oldest in medical ethics. Applied consistently, it asks us not to cause unnecessary harm to any being who can suffer it, which includes animals. See Harm, Benefit and Bioethics.

Criteria and Moral Status

Manipulation

Influencing someone's beliefs or choices by means that bypass or exploit their capacity to evaluate, such as deceiving, hiding information, exploiting biases and emotions or applying pressure, instead of offering good reasons.

The difference from persuasion lies in the means, not the goal: those who persuade present reasons and evidence the other person can examine and reject; those who manipulate count on them not noticing what is happening. Appealing to emotions isn't, in itself, manipulation: showing how animals live on a factory farm is true and relevant, and the emotion it arouses is part of understanding the problem. It becomes manipulation when the image is fake or out of context, when the numbers are exaggerated, or when guilt and shame are used to stop the person from thinking. The line isn't always sharp, and philosophers debate how to draw it. Both sides of the debate can manipulate: the industry, with euphemisms and adverts of happy animals, and animal advocates, with inflated statistics. For a cause that asks for consistency, persuading honestly is also a matter of respect. See Mass manipulation, Euphemism, Post-truth and Autonomy.

Martin's Act

A British law of 1822, proposed by the Irish MP Richard Martin, which prohibited the mistreatment of cattle, horses, sheep and other farm and working animals; it is considered one of the first national animal protection laws.

The law did not protect dogs and cats, and animals remained property, but it paved the way for the first animal protection society, in 1824, which later became the RSPCA. It shows that legal protection of animals began with the idea of cruelty, not of rights. See Animal cruelty, Animals as property and Welfarism.

Mass manipulation

Manipulation aimed at large audiences, through the press, advertising, political propaganda, social media and other means of communication.

One of the pioneers of public relations, Edward Bernays, argued in his book Propaganda (1928) that public opinion could be deliberately shaped, and by his own account helped popularize the bacon-and-eggs breakfast in the United States, in a campaign for a bacon producer backed by doctors' opinions. Twentieth-century totalitarian regimes used propaganda on a large scale, and today social media allow messages tailored to each profile, as the Cambridge Analytica case showed in 2018. In food, industry campaigns helped associate meat and milk with strength, health and tradition, while showing little of how animals live and die. Two cautions apply: not all mass communication is manipulation, and saying that those who disagree "have been manipulated" doesn't answer their arguments; it can also be a way of dodging the debate. See Manipulation, Post-truth, Meat industry and Carnism.

A decision principle that prescribes choosing the course of action in which the well-being of the worst-off is as high as possible, regardless of the total or average sum of well-being.

In contexts of animal exploitation, this principle would favour prioritising the reduction of suffering of the worst-off individuals (often animals in extreme confinement), even when this does not maximise aggregate well-being.

Meat industry

The set of companies and activities that breed, slaughter, process and sell animals as meat, from genetics and feed to meatpacking plants, distribution and advertising.

It is one of the largest industries in the world: according to FAO data, more than 80 billion land animals are slaughtered each year for consumption, not counting fish, which are measured in tonnes. The sector is concentrated in a few companies, and one of them, Brazil's JBS, is the largest meat processor in the world; Brazil is also the largest exporter of beef and chicken. To protect its prices and image, the industry relies on lobbying, subsidies and advertising with happy animals, fights in court over the use of names like "burger" for plant-based products and, in the United States, backed laws that punish those who secretly film farms, several of which courts have struck down. The costs it doesn't pay fall on others: cattle ranching is the main driver of deforestation in the Amazon, and meatpacking workers face high rates of injury and psychological distress. Criticizing the industry isn't accusing each worker or small producer, who often have few alternatives. See Livestock industry, Industrial livestock farming, Lobby, Subsidies and Externality.

Minimalism

A lifestyle that seeks to own and consume less, keeping what is useful or meaningful and letting go of excess.

The name comes from the minimalist art of the 1960s, and the lifestyle became popular in the 2010s, through books, blogs and documentaries such as those by the duo The Minimalists. It has points in common with veganism: buying less, choosing second-hand and repairing instead of replacing reduce environmental impact, and many minimalists question the idea that consuming more brings happiness. But the two aren't the same. Minimalism asks how much is consumed; veganism asks what is consumed and who pays for it. A minimalist can own few things and still eat meat or wear leather, and a vegan can consume a lot. Some also criticize minimalism when it becomes just an aesthetic, with expensive products sold as simplicity. See Conscious consumption, Consumption and Veganism.

Contempt for or aversion to animals and nature, seen as inferior, dirty or cruel; a term coined by the writer Jim Mason in the book An Unnatural Order (1993), by analogy with misogyny.

Moral agent

A being capable of acting morally and of being held responsible for its choices (usually adult humans).

Moral anthropocentrism

The view that only human interests have moral value of their own, and that everything else matters insofar as it serves humans or affects them.

It underpins arguments such as "they're just animals." Sentientism rejects it for treating species, and not the capacity to feel, as the criterion.

Moral circle

An imaginary boundary that marks out who is included in the community of morally considered beings; historically expanded to groups previously excluded, such as enslaved people and women.

For antispeciesism, the logic of this historical expansion points to the full inclusion of animals as its next coherent step.

Moral consideration

The act of including a being's interests in ethical weighing.

Moral consistency

The logical requirement that ethical principles be applied consistently across similar cases, without arbitrary exceptions; the basis of the argument from marginal cases.

By this requirement, defending unequal treatment of humans and animals in morally equivalent situations is one of the main sources of criticism of speciesism.

Criteria and Moral Status

Moral extensionism

The historical and philosophical process of expanding the moral circle to new groups previously excluded, such as women, colonized peoples and animals; a guiding thread running through much of the history of the animal advocacy movement.

For antispeciesism, this process remains incomplete as long as most sentient animals remain excluded from full moral consideration.

Criteria and Moral Status

Moral gradualism

The position that moral status can vary in degree according to a being's cognitive complexity, rather than being all-or-nothing.

Critics point out that degrees of moral status, if used to justify differences in treatment unrelated to the capacity to suffer, can reintroduce by another route the arbitrariness that the principle of equal consideration seeks to eliminate.

Criteria and Moral Status

Moral inclusion

The act of recognizing a being as deserving of consideration within the moral community.

Extending moral inclusion to animals is, for antispeciesism, a necessary correction of a historical error analogous to the moral exclusion of other groups in the past.

Criteria and Moral Status

Moral objectivism

The position that there are moral truths that are valid independently of opinion or culture.

If objective moral truths exist, and if the capacity to suffer is morally relevant, it follows that excluding animals from moral consideration would be objectively unjustified, and not a mere matter of cultural preference.

Criteria and Moral Status

Moral patient

A being that can be morally affected, benefited or harmed, even without being a moral agent.

Nonhuman animals fall into this category: even though they cannot be held responsible for their actions, they have interests whose violation or promotion is morally relevant.

Criteria and Moral Status

Moral philosophy

The branch of philosophy that studies what is right and wrong, good and bad, and how we should act; in practice, another name for ethics.

It is usually divided into three parts: metaethics, which asks what moral judgments are and whether they can be true; normative ethics, which proposes criteria for what we should do, such as utilitarianism, deontology and virtue ethics; and applied ethics, which discusses concrete cases, such as abortion, the death penalty and the use of animals. Animal ethics is part of applied ethics, but depends on the other two. See Ethics, Normative ethics and Animal ethics.

Normative Ethical Theories

Moral pluralism

The position that several ethical theories capture partial truths, with none of them being sufficient on its own.

A pluralist can recognise, for example, both the relevance of the total sum of well-being and the importance of prioritising the worst-off, without committing exclusively to a single theory.

Normative Ethical Theories

Moral progress

The idea that humanity can expand and refine its ethical judgements over time, as in the historical widening of the moral circle.

For antispeciesism, overcoming speciesism represents the next frontier of this progress, historically comparable to the abolition of slavery and the winning of civil rights.

Criteria and Moral Status

Moral relativism

The position that right and wrong are not universal, but depend on the culture, era or person judging.

Two theses need separating. The first is descriptive and true: cultures and people disagree about what is right. The second is relativism proper: there would be no standard for saying some of these views are better than others. It faces well-known objections, such as those of the philosopher James Rachels: if it were true, we couldn't condemn slavery where it was accepted, nor speak of moral progress, and reformers would always be wrong. Some also invoke relativism selectively, only for the practices they want to protect. Some philosophers, such as Gilbert Harman and David Wong, defend more sophisticated versions, and the debate continues. Even in those versions, an animal's suffering isn't a matter of opinion: they feel pain whatever the culture of the observer. See Cultural relativism, Moral progress, Moral universality and Double standard.

Moral status

The degree to which a being is taken into account for its own sake in the ethical decisions of others.

For sentientism, a being's moral status derives solely from its capacity to feel, not from its species, intelligence or usefulness to others.

Criteria and Moral Status

Moral universality

The principle that valid moral judgments must apply equally to all relevantly similar cases, regardless of who carries them out.

It is the logical basis of the argument from marginal cases and of much of the antispeciesist critique: if a moral criterion holds for humans in a given situation, it must hold equally for animals in a relevantly similar situation.

Criteria and Moral Status

Morgan's canon

A rule of method proposed by the British psychologist Conwy Lloyd Morgan in 1894: an animal's behavior should not be explained by a higher mental capacity if it can be explained by a simpler one.

For much of the 20th century, the canon was used to deny emotions and thought to animals. Today many scientists point out that the simplest explanation is not always the one that denies a mind: if a dog and a human react to injury in the same way and have similar brain structures, it may be simpler to assume that both feel pain. Morgan himself didn't mean to forbid mental explanations when there is evidence for them. See Anthropomorphism, Evolutionary continuity and Critical anthropomorphism.

Necessity

What a being needs to live well; in ethics, the criterion that separates unavoidable harm from avoidable harm.

Negative liberty

Liberty as the absence of interference: to be free is not to be prevented or coerced by others.

The distinction between negative and positive liberty became well known through the philosopher Isaiah Berlin, in the 1958 essay Two Concepts of Liberty. An ecological relationship in which one organism, the parasite, lives in or on another, the host, and feeds on it, harming it, usually without killing it right away.

Partiality

Giving special weight to the interests of some, such as family, friends, compatriots or one's own companion animals, because of the relationship we have with them.

The branch of philosophy that studies the organization of power, justice and rights; relevant for discussing the representation and citizenship of animals (e.g., sentiocracy).

Positive education

An approach to raising and teaching, children and also animals, based on respect, encouragement and reinforcing the desired behaviour, rather than punishment, fear and pain.

With children, the name often refers to positive discipline, proposed by the psychologist Jane Nelsen in the 1980s based on the ideas of Alfred Adler, which aims to be firm and kind at the same time; it is also used for Martin Seligman's positive education, which brings well-being into schools. Research links physical punishment to worse outcomes for children, and in Brazil the 2014 Menino Bernardo Law bans physical punishment and cruel treatment in child rearing. With animals, the equivalent idea is positive reinforcement training, which rewards the desired behaviour; studies find more signs of stress in dogs trained with aversive methods, such as shock collars and leash jerks. See Animal training and Humane education.

Positive liberty

Liberty as the capacity to act: to be free is to have real conditions to make choices and run one's own life.

For Isaiah Berlin, positive liberty was above all self-determination, being one's own master; today the term also covers having the means to act, such as health, resources and a suitable environment. For animals, it helps explain why opening the cages isn't enough: rescued animals need care and a place where they can live well, and wild animals free from human interference can still go hungry, fall ill and suffer. It is one of the reasons part of the movement argues for helping animals in the wild when this is possible without causing more harm. See Freedom, Negative liberty and Wild animal suffering.

Post-truth

A situation in which objective facts are less influential in shaping public opinion than appeals to emotion and personal belief.

The term was chosen as word of the year for 2016 by Oxford Dictionaries, the year of the Brexit referendum and the US presidential election. It isn't the same as fake news: fake news is the content, and post-truth is the climate in which it circulates and convinces, because it confirms what people already want to believe. In the debate about animals, it appears on both sides: in advertisements of happy cows that don't show the reality of farms, in nutrition myths that spread on social media, and also in exaggerations by animal advocates. The antidote is the same for everyone: verifiable sources, attention to what scientific bodies say, including where they disagree, and willingness to admit uncertainty. See Truth, Evidence and Cognitive dissonance.

Sentience in law

The recognition, in laws and treaties, that animals are sentient beings and not mere things.

In the European Union, Article 13 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union, in force since the Treaty of Lisbon (2009), requires paying regard to the welfare of animals as sentient beings, but makes exceptions for religious rites, cultural traditions and regional heritage. France began defining animals in its Civil Code as living beings endowed with sensitivity in 2015, and the United Kingdom passed an animal sentience law in 2022. The recognition is a symbolic step forward, but on its own it bans no practice: animals are still treated as property. See Animals as property, Animal legal personhood and Sentience.

Sentientist

A person who holds sentientism, that is, who takes sentience, rather than species, substrate or intelligence, as the criterion for moral consideration.

For a sentientist, every being able to feel, to have good and bad experiences, deserves to have their interests taken into account, whether a human, another animal or, if one ever exists, a sentient artificial mind. Since the late 2010s, the term has also named a worldview that joins reason and evidence with compassion for all sentient beings. A sentientist isn't necessarily vegan, and a vegan isn't necessarily a sentientist: one starts from a criterion of moral consideration, the other from a commitment not to exploit animals, and the two usually go together. Speciesist term

A word or expression that treats animals as things, uses animal names as insults or makes exploitation seem normal.

In English there are several kinds: calling animals "it", "stock" or "head"; using "pig", "cow", "chicken", "rat", "snake" or "dumb animal" as insults, which assumes those animals are inferior; calling people in experiments "guinea pigs"; and idioms that normalize violence, such as "kill two birds with one stone", "beat a dead horse", "there's more than one way to skin a cat" or "like lambs to the slaughter". Simple alternatives exist, such as "solve two problems at once", "go over old ground" or "there's more than one way to do it". In 2018, PETA proposed playful swaps such as "feed two birds with one scone", and was widely mocked, which shows how easily the subject can backfire. Language reflects and reinforces attitudes, and noticing these terms helps us see how common speciesism is. But changing words doesn't change practices by itself, and constantly correcting other people's speech can push away those who are just starting to think about the subject. See Euphemism, Absent referent, Objectification, Beast of burden and Guinea pig.

Biases, Fallacies and Rhetoric

Speciesist vegan

A vegan who rejects animal exploitation but still gives less weight to the interests of some sentient beings just because of their species.

Veganism starts from the principle of not exploiting animals. Antispeciesism goes further: it asks that similar interests of any sentient being weigh equally, whatever the species, including in situations that don't involve exploitation. A vegan can be speciesist in two ways. The first is discriminating among animals by species without justification, such as caring about dogs, cows and dolphins while dismissing fish, insects and other invertebrates who may also feel, which the glossary calls xenospeciesism. The second is ignoring sentient beings in scenarios beyond exploitation: wild animal suffering, treated as "just nature" even when helping would be possible; animals seen as pests, killed without need or in cruel ways; animals hit by disasters, road traffic or pollution, who get attention only when they belong to favored species. Dismissing possible new minds, such as sentient AIs, in advance is a similar mistake, but it isn't speciesism, because they belong to no species: the name for that bias is substratism, or carbonism when it favors organic bodies. The critique works as self-criticism, to carry the principle all the way through, not to invalidate veganism: pointing out that some vegans are speciesist doesn't show that exploiting animals is acceptable. See Speciesism, Xenospeciesism, Antispeciesism, Wild animal suffering and Substratism.

Stress

The set of bodily and behavioral responses to a threat or challenge, such as the release of hormones like cortisol, a faster heart rate and flight.

Stunning of animals

A procedure that seeks to render an animal unconscious before slaughter, reducing (but not always eliminating) the perception of pain.

The most common methods are the captive bolt gun, electric shock and, for pigs and poultry, gases such as carbon dioxide. At high concentrations, carbon dioxide is aversive to pigs, who show signs of panic and breathlessness before losing consciousness. Failures happen, and some animals reach the bleeding stage conscious. See Humane slaughter, Slaughterhouse and Slaughter.

Subjective experience

What is felt from the point of view of the one living through something: there being "something it is like to be" that being, in the words of the philosopher Thomas Nagel.

It is what distinguishes a sentient being from an object or a system that only reacts to stimuli: a stone has no experience, and a dog does. Sentience is the capacity to have good or bad subjective experiences, which is why it is the guide's moral criterion. The 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness states that there is strong scientific support for conscious experience in mammals and birds, and a realistic possibility in all vertebrates and many invertebrates, such as octopuses, crabs and insects. Subsidies

Public resources given to a sector or product, such as direct payments, cheap credit, insurance or price guarantees, to lower its costs or sustain its production.

According to a 2021 report by FAO, UNDP and UNEP, support to agricultural producers worldwide amounts to about 540 billion dollars a year, and 87% of it distorts prices or causes environmental and social harm. Much of it goes to animal farming and feed grains, which helps keep meat, milk and eggs artificially cheap. The environmentalist critique asks for prices to include costs to the environment and health, but the animal-centered critique goes further: the gravest cost, the suffering and death of animals, isn't counted at all. And a caution: a policy that charged only for environmental impact could make chicken and fish cheaper, since they emit less per kilo, and increase the number of animals killed. Well-justified subsidies would be those supporting plant proteins, fermentation and cultivated meat. See Tax incentive, Agribusiness and Externality.

Transhumanism

An intellectual movement that advocates using science and technology to overcome biological limits, such as ageing, disease, cognitive limitations and suffering.

The term was popularized by the biologist Julian Huxley in 1957, and the movement organized in the late 1990s, when Nick Bostrom and David Pearce founded the World Transhumanist Association, now Humanity+. The Transhumanist Declaration advocates the well-being of all sentience, including humans, non-human animals and possible artificial intelligences. Vegetarian

A person who doesn't eat the meat of any animal, including fish and seafood; in the most common sense, they may consume other animal products, such as milk, eggs and honey.

Diet and Vegetarianism: Variants

Vegetarianism

A diet that excludes the meat of any animal, including fish and seafood; in the strict sense, it also excludes all other animal products, such as milk, eggs and honey.

The word has two senses. In the broad sense (lato sensu), the most common, a vegetarian is someone who doesn't eat meat but may consume milk, eggs or honey; in practice, most are lacto-ovo vegetarians, and that is what most people understand by the term. In the strict sense (stricto sensu), vegetarianism is a diet free of all animal products, which in Brazil is called strict vegetarianism. Those who defend the strict sense often say it was the original meaning, and there is some support for this: some of the first to use the word, in the early 1840s, such as the members of the Alcott House community in England, ate no animal products at all. But the Vegetarian Society, founded in 1847 and responsible for popularizing the term, accepted eggs and dairy from the start, and that usage took hold; historians still debate the exact meaning of the earliest records. It was to name those who consumed no animal products that the word vegan was coined in 1944. Many people think vegetarians eat fish, but they don't, and some products hide ingredients from dead animals, such as gelatin and the rennet in certain cheeses. See Vegetarian, Strict vegetarianism, Lacto-ovo vegetarianism and Veganism.

Vicarious trauma (secondary traumatic stress)

Psychological distress in people who are repeatedly exposed to other people's trauma, even without having lived through it directly.

The concepts were first described among therapists and emergency workers: vicarious trauma by McCann and Pearlman (1990), secondary traumatic stress by Charles Figley. Vigil

In animal advocacy, a peaceful gathering outside slaughterhouses, farms or animal markets to bear witness to the animals' suffering, record it and make it visible.

The format became known through Toronto Pig Save, founded in Canada in 2010 by Anita Krajnc, which grew into the Animal Save Movement, now with groups in many countries. For many participants, a vigil is also an act of mourning and a refusal to look away. See Vegan witness (bearing witness), Protest and Guerrilla tactics.

A philosophical debate over whether and how humans would have an obligation to intervene to reduce suffering in nature.
