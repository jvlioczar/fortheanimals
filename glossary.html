Absent referent

A concept from the author Carol J. Adams: the animal who became food is made absent from the plate, by words and packaging that hide that there was a living individual and a death.

Agribusiness

The set of economic activities tied to large-scale agriculture and livestock farming, from production to processing and marketing.

Agriculture

The cultivation of plants, such as grains, legumes, fruit and fibers, for human food, animal feed or other uses.

Veganism does not oppose agriculture, but the exploitation of animals. Part of agriculture serves to feed animals raised for consumption, and crop production also has impacts that deserve attention.

Agropecuária (agriculture and livestock)

The combination of crop farming and livestock farming, that is, growing plants and raising animals.

The term joins two distinct activities, and veganism treats each differently: it opposes raising and using animals, but not growing plants.

Alienation

A state of psychological or social distance from the real consequences of one's own actions.

In the consumption of animal products, this alienation is made easier by the physical and informational distance between the consumer and the production process: the less visible the suffering, the easier it is to sustain practices that, if confronted directly, would be morally rejected.

Analogy

An argumentative resource that compares distinct situations because they share relevant characteristics.

Comparisons between animal exploitation and human oppressions, such as slavery, are used by antispeciesists to highlight common structures of domination, but they are also criticized for potentially minimizing the specificity and gravity of the historical human suffering involved.

A political current that rejects hierarchies and unjustified forms of domination, including the State, and proposes organizing society through voluntary association and self-management.

Part of anarchism includes animals: Élisée Reclus defended vegetarianism, and veganarchism, a term coined by Brian A. Dominick (1995), unites veganism and anarchism.

Animal

A multicellular living being of the kingdom Animalia, capable of movement and, in most cases, possessing a nervous system; a biological term that includes humans, although it is popularly used to refer only to nonhumans.

Legal and political work aimed at defending the interests and rights of animals before courts, legislators and public bodies.

Animal behavior

The scientific study (ethology) of animals' actions and reactions, used as indirect evidence of their mental states.

Animal cognition

The study of the mental capacities of non-human animals, such as memory, planning and problem solving.

Recent discoveries in this area have revealed, in several species, capacities once attributed exclusively to humans, which weakens criteria for moral exclusion based on supposed cognitive inferiority.

Animal consciousness

An extension of the concept of consciousness applied specifically to non-humans; it discusses whether, and to what degree, different species have their own subjective experience and how this can be scientifically inferred.

Given scientific uncertainty on this topic, a sentientist perspective tends to adopt the precautionary principle: granting moral consideration whenever there is a reasonable probability of consciousness, rather than demanding absolute certainty before acting.

Animal cruelty

The act of inflicting unnecessary suffering on an animal; a legal and moral category central to the first protection laws of the 19th century, predating the very concept of 'animal rights'.

The criterion of 'necessity' used to define cruelty tends, in practice, to exclude the suffering caused by legalized industries such as livestock farming, which reveals the limits of this concept when not accompanied by a broader reflection on the moral status of animals.

Animal habeas corpus

Animal holocaust

A term used by part of the animal liberation movement to describe the massive scale of animal killing in industrial livestock farming, in analogy to the human Holocaust.

The comparison is controversial: it is rejected by some scholars of the human Holocaust and by sectors of the animal movement itself, who consider that it may trivialize the specific human suffering of that historical event, even if the intention is to convey the massive scale of the killing of animals.

Animalism (in the sense of animal advocacy)

A generic term for the set of ideas, movements and practices aimed at defending animals, ranging from welfarism to abolitionism.

Because it covers positions as different as welfarism and abolitionism, the term is criticized by stricter antispeciesists for blurring relevant strategic and ethical differences between these currents.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Animals as persons

A legal and philosophical proposal to recognize certain animals as nonhuman persons, subjects of basic rights, and not mere objects.

Restricting this proposal only to species with high cognitive complexity is criticized by sentientists, who argue that the capacity to suffer, not intelligence, should be the relevant criterion for recognizing basic rights.

Animal Law and Legislation

Animals as property

The predominant legal status of animals in most legal systems, which classifies them as movable goods that can be possessed, bought and sold.

It is the central target of the abolitionist critique, which argues that, as long as animals are legally property, their fundamental interests can always be legally subordinated to the economic interests of their owners.

Animal Law and Legislation

Anthropocentric environmentalism

An environmentalist view that protects the environment because it matters to human beings, which gives it instrumental value.

It already recognizes that individuals matter, people, so it has to explain why it would leave out the other animals who also feel.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Anthropocentric speciesism

A variant of speciesism that specifically privileges the human species as the supreme criterion of value, distinct from broader forms of species-based discrimination.

It is the most common form of speciesism, present in most cultural and institutional practices that treat human interests as automatically superior to those of any other species.

Currents of Animal Ethics

Anthropocentrism

The view that places the human being as the center and measure of moral value.

It is the worldview that antispeciesism and sentientism most directly contest, because it assigns moral value on the basis of species, and not of the capacity to suffer.

The attribution of human characteristics, emotions or intentions to nonhuman animals; it can be a useful tool of empathy or a source of interpretive error, depending on the scientific context.

Organized opposition to environmental protection policies and movements, often in the name of economic growth, which includes denying or downplaying problems such as climate change.

Argument

A structured set of premises that support a conclusion.

Biases, Fallacies and Rhetoric

Argument from marginal cases

The thesis that criteria used to exclude animals from moral consideration, such as rationality or language, would also exclude humans with severe cognitive disabilities, revealing an inconsistency in speciesism.

Because it exposes this inconsistency, many philosophers consider it one of the hardest challenges to answer for those who defend cognitive criteria as the basis for the moral exclusion of animals.

Argument from relevance

The thesis that only morally relevant characteristics, such as the capacity to suffer, justify a difference in treatment; species, in itself, is not one of those characteristics.

It is one of the central arguments used to characterize speciesism as morally unjustified discrimination, analogous to racism and sexism.

The position that there are moral truths that are valid independently of opinion or culture.

If objective moral truths exist, and if the capacity to suffer is morally relevant, it follows that excluding animals from moral consideration would be objectively unjustified, and not a mere matter of cultural preference.

Moral patient

A being that can be morally affected, benefited or harmed, even without being a moral agent.

Nonhuman animals fall into this category: even though they cannot be held responsible for their actions, they have interests whose violation or promotion is morally relevant.

Moral pluralism

The position that several ethical theories capture partial truths, with none of them being sufficient on its own.

A pluralist can recognise, for example, both the relevance of the total sum of well-being and the importance of prioritising the worst-off, without committing exclusively to a single theory.

Moral progress

The idea that humanity can expand and refine its ethical judgements over time, as in the historical widening of the moral circle.

Moral relativism

The position that moral values depend on cultural or individual context.

The degree to which a being is taken into account for its own sake in the ethical decisions of others.

The principle that valid moral judgments must apply equally to all relevantly similar cases, regardless of who carries them out.

A concept from Tom Regan (cited only as a reference) designating beings with a unified psychophysical existence, memory, preferences and a well-being of their own, who deserve basic rights.

This concept underpins the defense of basic rights, to life and to liberty, for various nonhuman species, and not only for humans.

Substratism

Giving different weight to the good of a sentient being according to the substrate its body is made of, biological or digital, for example.

The parallel is direct: just as speciesism uses species as the criterion, and species says nothing about the capacity to suffer, substratism uses the material of the body, which says nothing either. A consequentialist strand that seeks to maximize well-being and minimize the aggregate suffering of those affected by a decision (reference: Bentham, Singer, cited only as examples).

The central principle of utilitarianism, according to which the right action is the one that produces the greatest possible balance of well-being over suffering.
