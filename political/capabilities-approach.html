Home / Political and ethical objections / The capabilities approach

"What matters is for the animal to live according to its species: a farm that lets it graze and be with others, and kills it painlessly, respects that."

The capabilities approach: the principle

Secure for each individual, up to an adequate minimum, the central capabilities for a dignified life: not only what they feel or own, but what they are actually able to be and do. The approach was proposed by the Indian economist Amartya Sen to think about human development and turned into a theory of justice by the American philosopher Martha Nussbaum, who extended it to animals.

Reply

The capabilities approach itself begins with life: for Martha Nussbaum, death harms those whose life unfolds over time, so mammals and birds should not be killed for food. Living according to one's species also includes bonds, health and an intact body, and commercial farming separates mothers and young, mutilates and slaughters young animals. Grazing is not enough to flourish.

Ask a question back

If flourishing as one's own species is a right, does slaughtering a young animal respect that flourishing or cut it short?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Nussbaum's list of central capabilities begins with life. In Justice for Animals (2023), she argues that death harms those whose life unfolds over time, a life they are aware of and value, and concludes that mammals and birds should not be killed for food, only in the animal's own interest or in self-defense. A "good" farm that ends with the slaughter of young animals goes against the first capability on the list (B2, B3).
  • Living according to one's species involves much more than grazing. The list also includes health, bodily integrity, emotions and bonds, play and some control over one's environment. In commercial farming, even the most free-range, calves are separated from their mothers, males are castrated and animals are slaughtered long before the natural end of their lives (B4, B5).
  • The approach was born as a critique of Rawls. In Frontiers of Justice (2006), in a chapter called "Beyond Compassion and Humanity", Nussbaum argues that mistreating animals is not merely a lack of compassion but an injustice, because animals are subjects entitled to flourish (A25).
  • She also points to a harm that pain does not exhaust. A bear dressed up and trained to perform tricks in a circus may not even suffer, but it does not live as a bear: it does not forage, hunt or choose mates. That is why the approach asks for more than the minimum welfare of current laws (I23, B10).

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version comes from Nussbaum herself. In Frontiers of Justice she treated painless killing for food as a difficult case, and in Justice for Animals she grants that killing fish painlessly may be legitimate, if it is true that they live in a perceptual present, with no projects for death to interrupt.

The answer

That concession applies, at most, to animals whose life does not unfold over time, and nothing guarantees that fish are like that: Nussbaum herself recommends caution, and the philosophers Eva Read and Jonathan Birch note that the criterion of "unfolding over time" is vague and little studied. Besides, real fishing does not kill painlessly, because fish die by suffocation, crushing or being gutted alive (A2). And for mammals and birds, the theory itself rules out killing for food.

The criticisms of the approach

Cochrane points to a problem of scope. Nussbaum grounded the approach in the value of the complex forms of life in nature, not in sentience alone. But if what matters is natural flourishing, justice should also reach plants, bacteria and mosquitoes. In Frontiers of Justice, Nussbaum limited herself to sentient beings for practical reasons; in Justice for Animals, she came to treat sentience as the dividing line. Even so, not every natural capacity deserves to be promoted, such as the capacity to kill or to spread disease, and Nussbaum says the approach is not nature worship. But then a criterion is needed to choose which capacities are worth it, and Peter Singer suggested that the obvious criterion would be the effect on well-being, which would bring the approach close to utilitarianism.

Wild animals are another point of debate. In Frontiers of Justice, Nussbaum went as far as speaking of a gradual replacement of the natural by the just, including with regard to predation, and in Justice for Animals she argues that justice is also owed to wild animals. For defenders of wild animals, this is the theory's consistent consequence; for many environmentalists and liberals, it asks too much (F4, N6, N8).

Read and Birch, in a 2023 review, also point to a tension with political liberalism: Nussbaum hopes for a consensus among reasonable doctrines, but many reasonable views, from Kantians to religions and environmentalists, would refuse to give priority to the worst-off animals. They also note that there is much uncertainty about which animals are sentient, such as insects and rays, which makes it uncertain how many are entitled to justice. And they criticize allowing the killing of pests as self-defense, since a rat or a cockroach rarely threatens anyone's life.

The philosopher Anders Schinkel argued in 2008 that the approach is inconsistent in keeping certain human uses of animals as acceptable, because they clash with the respect for flourishing the theory itself asks for. The criticisms differ, but none concludes that animals are outside justice: they debate how far the approach goes (B18).

A neighboring and older idea is that of the American philosopher Bernard Rollin (1943–2021), in Animal Rights and Human Morality (1981): animal rights should protect the telos, the nature proper to each species, what makes a pig a pig. Rollin, however, was a reformer and did not call for an end to animal farming. He saw traditional husbandry as a fair contract between humans and animals and worked for farming and research to respect the telos. It is the same idea as the counter-reply above, and the same answer applies: respecting an animal's nature and killing it young for food are hard to reconcile (B3, B16).

Wild animals and flourishing

Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (2022), highlights a point on which Nussbaum agrees with those who defend helping: in Frontiers of Justice, she separates the predator's blame from the question of preventing harm to the prey. The lion is not to blame for anything, but that does not settle whether we should protect the gazelle, as Steve Sapontzis and Tyler Cowen also observed (F4).

He also points to a tension in the idea of flourishing according to one's own nature, defended in different versions by Thomas Hurka and by Jennifer Everett. If a species' nature makes almost all of its young starve to death soon after birth, leaving them to it is not what is best for them; it is a reason to change those conditions, as we do with diseases that are also natural (N2, N8).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Martha Nussbaum: American philosopher; in Frontiers of Justice (2006), she criticizes Rawls's contract for leaving animals out, and in Justice for Animals (2023), she extends the central capabilities of her theory of justice to them
