"What matters is securing humanity's future; today's animals are a detail."

Longtermism: the principle

Positively influencing the long-term future is a key moral priority of our time. Since the future may last a very long time and hold an immense number of beings, reducing today the risks that would compromise it, such as the risk of extinction, would weigh heavily. The idea was developed within effective altruism by philosophers such as Nick Bostrom, Toby Ord, in The Precipice (2020), and William MacAskill, in What We Owe the Future (2022). In the strong version, by Hilary Greaves and MacAskill, the best options in today's most important decisions are those with the best effects on the far future.

Reply

The argument of longtermism is that the future may hold an immense number of beings, and that each one counts. But most of the sentient beings of the future will probably not be human, and a future that perpetuates factory farming or spreads suffering through space may be worse for those who will feel it. That is why animal advocates propose widening the moral circle, ideally to all sentient beings, as a long-term strategy.

Ask a question back

If what matters is the immense number of future beings, why does the math start by excluding most of them?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • The central argument of longtermism is about numbers: the future may hold far more beings than the present, and each of them counts. But most of the sentient beings of the future will probably not be human, just as most are not today, and they may include digital minds. A longtermism that counts only humans abandons the very reason that supports it: a future harm matters because someone will feel it, and that does not depend on species (N23, N21, A8).
  • The definition matters too. Oscar Horta and Mat Rozas, in "Animals and Longtermism" (World Futures, 2024), argue that longtermism should not be defined as concern for the future of humanity and its descendants, but for what the far future may be like for all sentient beings. They describe three kinds of risk for animals: more exploitation, because population and consumption grow, because farming turns to smaller animals such as shrimp and insects, and because, over centuries, the accumulated total would be immense; worse forms of harm, through new methods, artificial intelligence put to work for farming, and synthetic biology; and more wild animal suffering, through global warming and life taken beyond Earth (N23, N25, N26).
  • The risk is not only extinction. So-called s-risks, risks of suffering on an astronomical scale, are scenarios in which the future exists but holds immense suffering. The Center for Reducing Suffering and the researcher Tobias Baumann, in Avoiding the Worst (2022), point to a narrow moral circle, one that leaves out animals or artificial minds, as one of the factors that make such scenarios more likely (N26).
  • Factory farming and wild animal suffering could be perpetuated or carried further. Longtermism itself warns about value lock-in: the values dominant at a decisive moment may last a very long time. If indifference to animals is among them, intensive farming could be perpetuated, and the colonization of other planets, with terraforming or ecosystems taken off Earth, could multiply wild animal suffering (N25).
  • That is why animal advocates propose moral circle expansion as a long-term strategy. Jacy Reese Anthis and the philosopher Eze Paez, in "Moral circle expansion: A promising strategy to impact the far future" (Futures, 2021), argue that widening moral consideration, ideally to all sentient beings, is a promising way to improve the far future, and cite evidence that concern for some excluded groups tends to increase concern for others. It is the same conclusion as effective altruism's: the math has to include everyone who feels before it begins.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says the priority is avoiding extinction: if humanity disappears, there will be no one to improve the situation of animals in the future, so securing human survival would also be the best way to help them.

The answer

Surviving is not enough: a future that takes intensive farming into space, or spreads ecosystems full of suffering, may be worse for those who will feel it. MacAskill himself distinguishes securing civilization's survival from improving its trajectory, that is, the values it carries forward. The two tasks do not compete, and widening the moral circle is what makes a long future good for everyone, besides helping animals now (N22, N24).

The debate

In What We Owe the Future, MacAskill discusses animals in part of the chapter "Will the Future Be Good or Bad?". He describes factory farming as the society-wide production of a monstrous volume of suffering, judges that the lives of animals raised this way are, on average, worse than not existing, and says he does not know whether wild animals' lives are, on average, good or bad. Critics note that this is little space for the most numerous group of sentient beings. Heather Browning and Walter Veit, in the chapter "Longtermism and Animals" of the book Essays on Longtermism (Oxford University Press, 2025), argue that the longtermist debate has focused almost entirely on humans, and that animals, being far more numerous, should weigh much more in long-term decisions, even if each one counts for less.

Longtermism also has critics outside effective altruism. Émile P. Torres, in "Against longtermism" (Aeon, 2021), and the philosopher Alice Crary, in "The toxic ideology of longtermism" (Radical Philosophy, 2023), argue that it can justify ignoring present suffering in the name of speculative numbers about the future; Crary adds that it diverts the attention effective altruism used to give to the current suffering of humans and animals. Another criticism, discussed by philosophers within the field, is fanaticism: accepting bets with tiny probabilities just because the value at stake is enormous (N24, N26).

For animal advocacy, both readings point the same way. If longtermism is right, future animals count, and a great deal; if it is wrong, today's animals still count. Neither reading supports the claim that animals are a detail. And many actions, such as fighting speciesism, creating institutions that represent those who do not vote and ending factory farming, serve the present and the future at once (N22).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Jacy Reese Anthis: cofounder of the Sentience Institute; with Eze Paez, he argues in "Moral circle expansion" (2021) that widening the moral circle to all sentient beings is a promising strategy for improving the far future
  • Tobias Baumann: researcher at the Center for Reducing Suffering; in Avoiding the Worst (2022), he presents the risks of suffering on an astronomical scale, which may affect humans, animals and artificial minds
  • Brian Tomasik: writer and researcher; in "Risks of Astronomical Future Suffering" (2011), he argues that space colonization would likely increase total suffering, including that of animals, and he writes on wild animal and insect suffering
  • Oscar Horta and Mat Rozas: philosophers; in "Animals and Longtermism" (2024), they argue that longtermism must consider the future of all sentient beings and describe the long-term risks for animals
  • Heather Browning and Walter Veit: philosophers; in "Longtermism and Animals" (2025), they argue that longtermism needs to give animals much more weight than it has
