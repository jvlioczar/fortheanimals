Home / Political objections / Pragmatism

"Politics is the art of the possible: the animal cause is not viable, wins no votes, and there are more practical problems to solve."

Pragmatism: the principle

Judge ideas and policies by their practical consequences, not by loyalty to a doctrine; test, look at the results and correct course.

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • If practical consequences are what count, the first question is: consequences for whom? The suffering and death of billions of animals are real, measurable consequences of current policies and habits. Leaving them out of the count is not pragmatism; it is a choice made before looking at the results.
  • What is "viable" changes, and it changes because someone acts. In a few decades, conventional battery cages were banned in the European Union, the sale of cosmetics tested on animals was banned in dozens of countries and the market for plant-based alternatives grew. Treating what exists today as a fixed limit confuses the possible with the habitual (D2, K8).
  • Philosophical pragmatism, in authors such as William James and John Dewey, asks that moral beliefs be revised by experience. The experience gathered so far, from the science of sentience to the alternatives available, is exactly what calls for revision in the case of animals.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says that resources and political attention are limited, and that a responsible politician or manager should spend them where there is popular support and a sure result.

The answer

That calculation applies to choosing the means, not to deciding who counts. A pragmatist can prefer gradual goals with proven results for animals, and many exist. What a pragmatist cannot do, without abandoning their own method, is ignore the consequences for those who feel. And not eating animals depends on neither votes nor public budgets (J16, K6).

Not to be confused with pragmatic veganism

This page is about pragmatism as a general political and philosophical outlook. It is not the same as so-called pragmatic veganism, a current within the vegan movement that accepts the goal of ending animal exploitation and discusses which strategies get the most results, such as gradual reforms and corporate campaigns. One is an objection from outside; the other is an internal debate about means.

Philosophical pragmatism does not, on its own, lead to a conclusion about animals: it is a method, and what it concludes depends on which consequences are counted. The authors below show what happens when the consequences for animals are counted.

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Erin McKenna and Andrew Light: American philosophers; they edited Animal Pragmatism: Rethinking Human-Nonhuman Relationships (2004), which applies philosophical pragmatism to our relations with animals
  • Henry Spira: activist (1927–1998); known for campaigns with concrete, achievable goals that ended specific animal tests; his story is told by Peter Singer in Ethics into Action (1998)
