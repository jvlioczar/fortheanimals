Home / Political and ethical objections / Communitarianism

"Duties arise from the community and its traditions, and animals are not members of it."

Communitarianism: the principle

Morality is built in the bonds and practices of a community.

Short answer

Domesticated animals have lived inside our communities for thousands of years: they take part in our practices all the time, only as tools. Traditions also change, and many changed precisely to include those who were once outside. A community that defines its members by what suits it repeats the exclusion that the struggles for inclusion fought against.

Ask a question back

If the dog living in your home is not a member of the community, who decides who is?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Communitarianism can even protect animals more than liberalism does. If the state should promote the values a community shares, and many societies say they reject cruelty, the state has grounds to go beyond liberal neutrality, for example by banning intensive farming, even if that limits the liberty of producers and consumers. Cochrane gives the example of the United Kingdom, which passed one of the world's first laws against cruelty to animals in 1822.
  • Domesticated animals have lived inside our communities for thousands of years. Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka propose treating them as members, with adapted citizenship rights.
  • A community's traditions change, and many changed precisely to include those who were outside (D1, D6).
  • A community that defines its members by what suits it repeats the exclusion that struggles for inclusion fought against.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says membership requires taking part in shared practices, which animals do not do.

The answer

Dogs, horses, cows and chickens take part in our practices all the time, just as tools. The question is whether that participation should be exploitation or membership.

The limits of basing protection on a community's values

For the philosopher Michael Walzer, in Spheres of Justice (1983), a society is just when it is faithful to the shared understandings of its members. He uses the example of eating dogs and cats, accepted in some societies and taboo in others. Taken literally, this idea leaves animals protected only where society already cares about them, and makes any local practice, however cruel, immune to criticism, just as it would do for practices that discriminate against women or minorities (D6).

The political theorist Bhikhu Parekh proposes a middle way: there are universal values, but each culture interprets them in its own way. The limit, Cochrane notes, is that not every practice is an interpretation of a value; some simply deny it. On the other hand, almost every society has traditions of compassion to appeal to. In China, for example, Buddhism has a long association with vegetarianism, and bear bile farms shock many Chinese people.

It is also hard to say what a community's "authentic" value is. Before the nineteenth century, the British were seen by their neighbors as not very fond of animals, because of cockfighting and bear- and badger-baiting. And the 2004 ban on hunting with hounds in England and Wales divided the country: both sides claimed to speak for British tradition (D1).

Another problem is partiality. A community can protect the pet rat very well and leave the wild rat to the exterminator. Mary Midgley argues that some partiality is legitimate, including toward one's own species. The philosopher Elisa Aaltola replies that species can change the content of what we owe someone without changing the priority of their interests. And there is a useful distinction: favoring family and friends in personal life is acceptable, but the state's decisions should be impartial (A12).

J. Baird Callicott distinguishes the mixed community of domestic animals from the biotic community of wild ones, and proposes that duties follow closeness. For him, domestication was a kind of contract: animals receive care and may be used and slaughtered, and industrial farming violates that contract. Cochrane doubts any animal would accept such a deal, and the philosopher John Hadley observes that farm and laboratory animals are kept out of sight, without the close relationship the theory assumes. Callicott himself conceded that the strength of an interest can outweigh closeness, which brings him near impartial theories (B16).

Finally, a state is home to several communities, with different practices toward animals. Promoting the values of all of them leads to cultural and religious exemptions, discussed on the page on the progressive left and multiculturalism.

Community and wild animals

The territory criterion, on which domestic animals are "ours" and wild ones belong to another community, has a communitarian basis: J. Baird Callicott's mixed community, which gives domesticated animals the rights of members and denies wild ones even the right to life. Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (2022), notes that the criterion is applied only when the victims are not human: almost no one thinks that malaria should go untreated among peoples who live in the forest because they would belong to another "territory". And the historical debt to domesticated animals would give them, at most, priority, not a reason to exclude wild ones (N26).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Mary Midgley: British philosopher (1919–2018); in Animals and Why They Matter (1983), she describes the "mixed community" humans and animals have formed for millennia
  • Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka: in Zoopolis (2011), they treat domesticated animals as members of the political community; for wild animals, they defend the sovereignty of their communities, which limits help to them, a point contested by other animal advocates
  • Elisa Aaltola: Finnish philosopher; in "Animal Ethics and Interest Conflicts" (2005), she criticizes the idea that belonging to the same species justifies giving priority to someone's interests
  • John Hadley: Australian philosopher; in "Critique of Callicott's Biosocial Moral Theory" (2007), he shows that farmed animals do not live in the close community Callicott's theory assumes; in "The Duty to Aid Nonhuman Animals in Dire Need" (2006), he argues that what we owe free-roaming animals in dire need is essentially no different from what we owe severely cognitively impaired distant strangers
