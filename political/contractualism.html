"Justice holds among those who have a sense of justice and can make agreements."

Contractualism and Rawlsian justice: the principle

Choose the rules of a society impartially, as if we did not know what position we would occupy in it.

Reply

The veil of ignorance exists to discount what is luck, such as race, sex and talent, and species is luck too. Not knowing whether you would be born a human or a chicken, nobody would approve rules that allow chickens to be bred and killed for a meal. And justice already protects babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities, who cannot make agreements either: being protected by a rule never required being able to follow it.

Ask a question back

Behind the veil, not knowing whether you would be born a human or a chicken, what rule would you choose for the farms?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Rawls himself left animals out of justice, but he recognized duties of compassion toward them. Yet duties the state does not enforce give way to each person's liberty, as the liberalism page shows (A25).
  • Rawls gave two reasons for the exclusion. The first is reciprocity: justice regulates social cooperation, and only those who contribute would receive it. But animals on farms, in laboratories, in zoos and in homes contribute a great deal to how society works. The second is that only "moral persons", with a sense of justice and a conception of the good, would be parties to the contract. But Rawls includes all humans in justice, even those who will never have these capacities, so this reason does not separate humans from animals either (A19, A21).
  • The veil of ignorance exists to discount what is luck, like race, sex and talent. Mark Rowlands observed that species is also luck, and so should also go behind the veil (A13).
  • Not knowing whether you would be born human or chicken, no one would approve rules that allow chickens to be bred and killed for a meal.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version is that of the British philosopher Peter Carruthers, in The Animals Issue (1992), who develops an idea of Rawls's own. He grants that not every human can follow the rules, but says excluding those humans would put just institutions at risk: it is hard to tell who is a moral person, and people would resist rules that left babies out. With animals, he says, none of these risks exists.

The answer

Babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities cannot follow the rules either, and justice protects them: being a beneficiary of a rule has never required being able to follow it (A21, A23). And the stability argument proves little. Many stable societies have excluded whole human groups from justice, and many people would also resist rules that said nothing about how animals are treated. Carruthers also assumes that no animal is a person, which is debated in the case of great apes and cetaceans.

Species behind the veil, and the objections to the idea

The proposal of Donald VanDeVeer, Robert Elliot and Mark Rowlands is to apply Rawls's method more fully. If being a moral person or not is a matter of luck, like race and sex, it should also go behind the veil, and so should species. Not knowing whether they would be born human or not, the parties would have reasons to protect animals.

Three objections tend to come up. First: would the parties then choose rules even for stones and plants? No, because it only makes sense to protect those who have interests, and what does not feel cannot be harmed. Second: can one imagine being an animal? As much as one can imagine being a newborn, which Rawls's method already asks. Third: how would we know what is just for a cow? It is hard, as it is for humans, but facts about sentience and each species' needs already let us say that a chicken in intensive farming lives badly and a well-cared-for dog lives well.

The strongest objection comes from a defender of animals, the political theorist Robert Garner. For him, the contract does little work: what decides who is in are the convictions we bring into it, adjusted through reflective equilibrium. Rawls excludes animals because he already thought justice is only for persons, and the defenders of the veil include them because they already thought sentience is enough. So Garner suggests arguing directly that sentience makes one a recipient of justice. The objection does not defeat the case for animals, but it shows where that case really rests.

The exclusion has old roots in contractualism. Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) said that, since no contract can be made with animals, we are in a permanent state of war with them.

Explaining the origin of morality is not justifying it

The idea that morality is born of an agreement among those who can protect themselves from one another is old. In Plato's Republic, Glaucon puts it forward so that Socrates can refute it: people would agree not to commit injustice so as not to suffer it. Echoes of it appear in David Gauthier, in Morals by Agreement (1986), and, according to Peter Singer, also in Rawls, and they have been used to exclude animals, who can neither reciprocate nor restrain themselves. It helps to separate two families. Mutual-advantage contractarianism, that of Hobbes and Gauthier, grounds morality in each party's interest; Rawls grounds justice in the impartiality of the veil of ignorance, so the objections below hit him less.

Singer, in Practical Ethics (2nd ed., 1993), distinguishes explaining the origin of ethics from justifying it. Even if it arose from self-interested agreements, reason, once in use, is not tied to the interest that gave rise to it, just as counting, invented for practical purposes, eventually led to calculus. And the version based on reciprocity alone proves too much. It would remove the duties of slave traders toward those who could not retaliate, and those of rich countries toward poor ones. Above all, it would remove duties toward future generations: those who will live in 2100 can do nothing for us, and it would be enough to store nuclear waste safely for a hundred years, even though some of it stays deadly for thousands of years (N22, N23).

Some contractarians reply that everyone who has, or will have, the capacity for reciprocity counts. Singer notes that this version has already given up actual reciprocity, and then there is no reason left to pick that capacity as the boundary rather than the capacity to be harmed (A21, A23).

The Canadian philosopher Jan Narveson gave this family its most direct form in the debate about animals. In "Animal Rights" (1977), he rejects weak justifications for using animals, such as the claim that they feel no pain, but suggests that a morality grounded in rational self-interest, if it can account for our moral intuitions, defeats the claim that animals have rights as strong as those of humans. In the strongest version of the argument, which he developed later in replies to Tom Regan, morality is an agreement among rational agents who gain from cooperating: each accepts limits because the others do too. Animals cannot make this agreement or keep it, so we would have no direct duties to them; what protects them is how much people care about them. It is a theory consistent with its own starting point, and it deserves to be discussed on those terms.

Regan replied in the same issue of the journal and, in "The Case for Animal Rights" (1985), summed up the objection to this kind of contract: if only those who can sign the agreement have rights, young children and people with profound cognitive disabilities are also left without direct duties, and their protection comes to depend on someone caring about them. Few accept that conclusion for humans. The philosopher Nathan Nobis, in Animals & Ethics 101 (2016), suggests starting there: judge contractarianism first by what it implies for vulnerable humans, ground on which almost everyone already has firm convictions, and only then by what it implies for animals (A21, A23).

The contract and wild animals

Even without including animals, no rational contractor would approve a total ban on intervening in nature, because intervening is necessary for their own survival. This alone shows that "do not interfere with nature" cannot be a principle of the contract (N9).

Behind Rowlands's veil, which also hides species, the parties do not know whether they would be born human or as one of the animals living outside human control. Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (2022), argues that they would choose to help those in danger regardless of whether the harm has a human or a natural origin, and would reject limiting help to those who can reciprocate. Since wild animals are astronomically more numerous than humans, the chance of being born as one of them would weigh heavily in the choice (N6, N7).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Mark Rowlands: Welsh philosopher; in Animal Rights: A Philosophical Defence (1998), he puts species behind Rawls's veil of ignorance, and he restates the argument for a general audience in Animals Like Us (2002) and Animal Rights: All That Matters (2013)
  • Donald VanDeVeer: American philosopher; he made the same proposal in 1979, in the article "Of Beasts, Persons, and the Original Position"
  • Martha Nussbaum: in Frontiers of Justice (2006), she criticizes Rawls's contract for leaving out animals and people with disabilities
  • Robert Elliot: Australian philosopher; in "Rawlsian Justice and Non-human Animals" (1984), he argues for including animals in Rawls's original position
  • Ruth Abbey: political theorist; in "Rawlsian Resources for Animal Ethics" (2007), she examines what Rawls's work offers animal ethics and argues that the duties of compassion he recognizes can protect animals
