"God gave humans dominion over animals; if He allowed it, there is nothing wrong with eating them."

Religious ethics and divine command: the principle

What is right is what God commands, or what agrees with God's good nature, known through scripture and tradition.

Short answer

Allowing is not commanding: no major tradition requires eating animals, and many condemn needless suffering. In Genesis itself, dominion comes right before the diet given to humans and animals, of plants and fruit, and many theologians read dominion as a duty of care, not a license to exploit. Today, for most people, eating animals is a choice, not a necessity.

Ask a question back

If dominion was given to care for creation, what does a shed with thousands of confined animals say about that care?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Allowing is not commanding. Even in the traditions that allow eating animals, none requires it, and many condemn needless suffering (D12).
  • In Genesis itself, dominion (1:28) comes right before the diet given to humans and animals: plants and fruit (1:29-30). Many theologians read dominion as the responsibility of a caretaker, not as a license to exploit (D10).
  • Almost every major religious tradition has teachings of compassion toward animals, and some, such as Jainism and strands of Hinduism and Buddhism, make non-violence toward them a central principle.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says that eating animals was expressly authorized, for example to Noah in Genesis 9:3, and that no one can call wrong what God authorized.

The answer

An authorization given in one context is not a command for all time, and it does not cancel the duties of mercy the traditions themselves teach. Today, for most people, eating animals is a choice, not a necessity, and the choice that spares suffering is the one closest to the compassion these traditions ask for. Theologians such as Andrew Linzey argue this from within the faith.

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Andrew Linzey: British Anglican theologian; in Animal Theology (1994) he argues that animals have God-given value and that human dominion is a duty of care
