"What matters is saving the planet, ecosystems and species, not each individual animal."

Environmentalism: the principle

Protect the environment and the planet: conserve ecosystems, biodiversity and natural resources, and secure living conditions for present and future generations.

Short answer

Ecosystems, species and biodiversity feel nothing: those who can be harmed are the individuals who live in them. The most common environmentalism is already centered on individuals, only human ones: it protects the climate and forests because people depend on them. If what makes people matter is that they feel, other beings who feel count too. And industrial farming and fishing are among the main causes of deforestation and habitat loss.

Ask a question back

If only the ecosystem matters, and not the individual, would you accept sacrificing humans to save a forest?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Ecosystems, species and biodiversity are abstractions: they feel nothing. Those who can be harmed or benefited are the individuals who live in them. When environmentalism defends only these abstractions, animals are left out of the center of the debate, and their interests can be sacrificed in their name (A10, N3). Pure environmentalism, holistic or ecocentric, is consistent on this point: if value lies in the whole, the individual can be sacrificed, even a human, as in a land ethic like the one J. Baird Callicott developed from Aldo Leopold. Tom Regan called this consequence "environmental fascism". The one that needs to be questioned is anthropocentric environmentalism, the most common kind: it already recognizes that individuals matter, people, and values the environment only as a means of preserving them, that is, instrumentally. But if what makes people matter is that they feel and can be harmed, there is no reason to leave out the other individuals who also feel.
  • And for other animals the sacrifice already happens in practice: environmental programs kill animals deemed invasive and defend hunting as management, "sustainable" meat and insect farming (H16, H13, H15). These measures reduce an environmental impact while keeping or increasing the number of victims.
  • The most common environmentalism is already centered on individuals, only human ones: it protects the climate, water and forests because people depend on them. That is anthropocentric environmentalism. The same logic can be extended to everyone who feels: protecting the environment because sentient beings, human or not, depend on it. That is sentiocentric environmentalism.
  • The two movements have much in common. Industrial animal farming and fishing are among the largest causes of deforestation, emissions and habitat loss, and environmental destruction directly harms the animals who live in those places.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version, from Aldo Leopold's land ethic and Arne Naess's deep ecology, says the whole has value of its own: an action is right when it preserves the integrity and stability of the biotic community, and individuals matter as parts of it.

The answer

If the whole had priority over individuals, the same would hold for humans, the species with the greatest environmental impact. Almost no one accepts that conclusion: when the victims would be human, environmentalists themselves limit their goals by the rights of individuals. Consistency asks for the same limit for the other beings who feel. And a stable ecosystem is not, in itself, a good place for those who live in it (N3).

Environmentalism and veganism are not the same thing

Environmentalism is about the use of natural resources and the preservation of the environment. Veganism is about the exploitation of animals. A person can stop eating meat for the climate and still accept uses of animals with a low environmental impact. That is why environmental arguments are treated in this guide as indirect arguments: they help with the facts, but they do not answer the question of what we owe animals.

The most consistent relation between the two is the one that puts individuals at the center. Those who defend humans or animals have good reasons to defend the environment, insofar as environmental destruction harms them. And when an environmental goal conflicts with the interests of sentient beings, what weighs is what happens to those who can feel.

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • J. Baird Callicott: American philosopher; in "Animal Liberation: A Triangular Affair" (1980), he contrasts the holistic land ethic, in which value lies in the whole, with the defense of animals as individuals, and later revises part of that position in "Back Together Again" (1988)
  • Dale Jamieson: American philosopher; in the article "Animal Liberation is an Environmental Ethic" (1998), he argues that animal liberation is an environmental ethic
  • Oscar Horta: Spanish philosopher; he shows where animal ethics and environmentalism diverge and argues for deciding by the interests of sentient beings
  • Catia Faria and Eze Paez: philosophers; in the article "It’s Splitsville" (2019), they explain why the criteria of animal ethics and environmental ethics are incompatible when they conflict
  • Jeff Sebo: American philosopher; in Saving Animals, Saving Ourselves (2022), he ties the protection of animals to public health and the climate crisis
