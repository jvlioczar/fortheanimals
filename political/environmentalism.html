"What matters is saving the planet, ecosystems and species, not each individual animal."

Environmentalism: the principle

Protect the environment and the planet: conserve ecosystems, biodiversity and natural resources, and secure living conditions for present and future generations.

Reply

Ecosystems, species and biodiversity feel nothing: those who can be harmed are the individuals who live in them. The most common environmentalism is already centered on individuals, only human ones: it protects the climate and forests because people depend on them. If what makes people matter is that they feel, other beings who feel count too: that is a sentientist environmentalism. And industrial farming and fishing are among the main causes of deforestation and habitat loss.

Ask a question back

If only the ecosystem matters, and not the individual, would you accept sacrificing humans to save a forest?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Ecosystems, species and biodiversity are abstractions: they feel nothing. Those who can be harmed or benefited are the individuals who live in them. When environmentalism defends only these abstractions, animals are left out of the center of the debate, and their interests can be sacrificed in their name (A10, N3). Pure environmentalism, holistic or ecocentric, is consistent on this point: if value lies in the whole, the individual can be sacrificed, even a human, as in a land ethic like the one J. Baird Callicott developed from Aldo Leopold. Tom Regan called this consequence "environmental fascism". The one that needs to be questioned is anthropocentric environmentalism, the most common kind: it already recognizes that individuals matter, people, and values the environment only as a means of preserving them, that is, instrumentally. But if what makes people matter is that they feel and can be harmed, there is no reason to leave out the other individuals who also feel.
  • And for other animals the sacrifice already happens in practice: environmental programs kill animals deemed invasive and defend hunting as management, "sustainable" meat and insect farming (H19, H15, H18). These measures reduce an environmental impact while keeping or increasing the number of victims.
  • The most common environmentalism is already centered on individuals, only human ones: it protects the climate, water and forests because people depend on them. That is anthropocentric environmentalism, and treating the environment as an instrument for humans is the most common way of defending it. There are good reasons to extend this logic to everyone who feels: what makes environmental destruction bad for people, hunger, disease, pain and the loss of shelter, is the same as what it causes other animals. Protecting the environment because sentient beings, human or not, depend on it is a sentientist environmentalism, or simply sentientism with its proposals for the environment.
  • The two movements have much in common. Industrial animal farming and fishing are among the largest causes of deforestation, emissions and habitat loss, and environmental destruction directly harms the animals who live in those places.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version, from Aldo Leopold's land ethic and Arne Naess's deep ecology, says the whole has value of its own: an action is right when it preserves the integrity and stability of the biotic community, and individuals matter as parts of it.

The answer

If the whole had priority over individuals, the same would hold for humans, the species with the greatest environmental impact. Almost no one accepts that conclusion: when the victims would be human, environmentalists themselves limit their goals by the rights of individuals. Consistency asks for the same limit for the other beings who feel. And a stable ecosystem is not, in itself, a good place for those who live in it (N3).

A sentientist environmentalism

In a sentientist environmentalism, the environment matters because of those who live in it. Climate, water, forests and oceans are still protected, but the reason is what happens to the beings who feel, human and nonhuman. And when an environmental goal conflicts with them, the criterion is the effect on those beings, not the number of species or how a landscape looks.

This leads to concrete proposals. Reducing animal farming and fishing, which are among the largest causes of deforestation and habitat loss and directly kill billions of animals. Counting animals in impact assessments and climate policies, as affected beings, not only as a source of emissions (H7, H21). Building roads, buildings and power lines with those who live around them in mind, with wildlife crossings and bird-safe glass. Fighting pollution by plastics, pesticides and oil also for the suffering it causes animals. Preferring nonlethal methods, such as contraception, to culling and hunting in managing populations and introduced species (H19). Rescuing animals in fires, floods and spills. And researching the welfare of wild animals before larger interventions (N17).

On many points, these proposals coincide with those of traditional environmentalism. They diverge when the environment is defended at the expense of individuals: killing animals to protect a species or an ecosystem, defending hunting as management or valuing wild nature without asking how the animals in it live (A10, N3, N16).

Environmentalism and veganism are not the same thing

Environmentalism is about the use of natural resources and the preservation of the environment. Veganism is about the exploitation of animals. A person can stop eating meat for the climate and still accept uses of animals with a low environmental impact. That is why environmental arguments are treated in this guide as indirect arguments: they help with the facts, but they do not answer the question of what we owe animals.

The most consistent relation between the two is the one that puts individuals at the center. Those who defend humans or animals have good reasons to defend the environment, insofar as environmental destruction harms them. And when an environmental goal conflicts with the interests of sentient beings, what weighs is what happens to those who can feel.

Theories that place value in the flourishing of every living thing, rather than in sentience, face the same problem as holism. Cochrane notes that, taken seriously, they would ask society to promote the flourishing of bacteria, viruses and mosquitoes too, which almost no one accepts, and they force a choice about which natural capacities are worth promoting. This is one of the criticisms of the capabilities approach and of the Marxist idea of distributing according to needs, discussed on their own pages.

Dorado (2015) gives an example of how the environmental argument can leave animals out. J. Baird Callicott (1994) accepted vegetarianism on grounds of ecological efficiency, since the plants fed to farmed animals would feed more people if eaten directly. It is a reason that says nothing about what is owed to animals, and it is debatable as a general rule: some crops also use a lot of resources, and hunting and fishing obtain animal food without cropland.

Singer's critique of biocentrism and holism

Peter Singer, in Practical Ethics (2nd ed., 1993), examines attempts to extend ethics beyond sentient beings. The best known is Albert Schweitzer's reverence for life, for whom all life is sacred. A more recent philosophical version is Paul W. Taylor's, in Respect for Nature (1986): every living being pursues its own good in its own way, and so deserves the same value we give our own existence. Singer's objection is that both use metaphorical language and then argue as if it were literal. We say a plant "seeks" the light, but we could equally say that a river seeks the sea or that a guided missile seeks its target. Without consciousness, a tree's growth is a physical process, and it is unclear why it should deserve more respect than the formation of a stalactite.

Deep ecology goes beyond the individual. Aldo Leopold proposed a land ethic; Arne Naess and George Sessions gave intrinsic value to the flourishing of all life and to diversity; and Bill Devall and Sessions defended a biocentric egalitarianism, in which all organisms would have equal intrinsic value. Lawrence Johnson and Freya Mathews speak of the interests of species and ecosystems, and Mathews extends this to the whole biosphere, James Lovelock's Gaia. Singer replies that being part of an interconnected whole does not give each part intrinsic value, let alone equal value, and that the idea clashes with firm judgments, such as that a gorilla's life weighs more than a weed's.

The test he proposes is to ask what it would be, for the subject itself, to have an interest frustrated or to fail to flourish. The question has an answer for an opossum drowning when a dam floods the valley: it must be horrible. It has none for the tree, the species or the ecosystem, because there is nothing it is like, for them, to go through it. So the line of sentience would be a firmer moral boundary than the line between living and non-living. This does not weaken the defense of nature: for Singer, the interests of sentient beings, present and future, human and nonhuman, are already enough to show that preserving what is left of the wild is worth more than the economic gain from destroying it. The environmental ethic he proposes includes using less fossil fuel and no longer eating animals (H7).

The test is contested. Taylor and Robin Attfield reply that having a good of one's own is enough to have value, even without experience, and J. Baird Callicott and Holmes Rolston III argue that species and ecosystems have a value that does not reduce to that of individuals. On the side of animal advocates, besides Singer, Tom Regan rejected putting the whole before individuals, which he called environmental fascism.

In practice, humans stay on top

In the thesis El conflicto entre la ética animal y la ética ambiental (2015), Daniel Dorado gathered and annotated hundreds of works from both fields. One of his conclusions is that environmental ethics uses three main criteria, being alive, being an ecosystem or a species, and being human, but that in practice authors combine them and end up subordinating the first two to the third. Human interests come before ecosystems and other living beings.

The examples come from the works themselves. Paul W. Taylor, who gives value to every living being, accepts that even nonbasic human interests, such as building an art museum, prevail over the basic interests of other living beings, and argues for reserving the idea of moral rights for humans. Pentti Linkola, who argues for reducing the human population for the good of ecosystems, supports the extinction of cats and mink for the environmental damage they would cause, but not that of humans, whom he himself sees as the greatest threat to the environment.

For Dorado, an environmental ethic that did not subordinate itself to humans would have to accept sacrificing them for the good of ecosystems, a conclusion almost no one accepts, not even among environmentalists. An ethic centered on sentient beings does not have this problem, because it protects humans for the same reason it protects other animals: they all feel.

The thesis concludes that the two ethics are opposed in their foundations, and that attempts to reconcile them, such as those of J. Baird Callicott ("Back Together Again", 1988) and Dale Jamieson ("Animal Liberation Is an Environmental Ethic", 1998), are unlikely to succeed. The conclusion is disputed: Jamieson and Callicott still argue for bridges between the fields. Both things can be true at once: the movements can agree on many practical measures, such as reducing animal farming, and disagree about what to do when an ecosystem and the animals who live in it come into conflict.

Helping wild animals: where the conflict shows

The case of wild animals is where the difference between environmentalism and animal advocacy becomes clearest. The philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (Reasons to Help, 2022), separates three families of environmentalism by what they value directly: holism, which values wholes such as species and ecosystems (ecocentrism is the version centered on ecosystems); naturocentrism, which values what has a natural, non-human origin; and biocentrism, which values the good of each living organism. He notes that biocentrism, taken seriously, would call for more help for wild animals, not less: if the good of each organism counts, population dynamics, in which almost all die soon after birth, is a problem for it too (N15).

Even for those who give species and ecosystems a value of their own, the conclusion "do not help" does not follow. First, an environmental value would only block all help if it worked as an absolute trump, not merely as a greater weight; and many kinds of help, such as vaccinating against rabies or pulling an animal out of the mud, affect no environmental goal at all. Second, if environmental value adds to the good of sentient beings, reducing suffering also increases the total value. Third, environmentalists themselves already limit their goals by the basic interests of humans: no one proposes leaving malaria untreated in a community that lives in the forest in order to preserve the natural course of things. Without speciesism, the same limit applies to other animals (N3, N9).

Cunha goes further and holds that pluralist environmental views, which claim to give some value to sentient individuals, end up anthropocentric in practice, because they approve of culling animals for environmental goals but never humans. This is his thesis, and pluralists reject it. He also points out that ecology describes what happens in nature and does not say what we should do: those who draw a norm from it are the ones confusing the fields.

The strongest point of conflict is the expansion of natural areas. Cunha, like Oscar Horta and Brian Tomasik, argues against expanding or recreating natural areas, for example through reforestation or rewilding, because this would multiply the births of animals that have thousands of offspring, almost all doomed to die young. He does not propose destroying existing habitats, and he advises against opening the conversation with this point when talking to someone who does not yet know the problem. The thesis is contested. Conservationists and climate policies defend reforestation, including as a way to remove carbon from the atmosphere; Cunha replies that there are other means against climate change, such as energy efficiency and renewable energy. Organizations devoted to wild animals, such as Wild Animal Initiative, currently focus on research into their welfare. And the thesis depends on a balance of welfare in nature that is uncertain: Zach Groff and Yew-Kwang Ng (2019) concluded that it could go either way, and Heather Browning and Walter Veit (2023) argue that it may be positive (N1, N16).

J. Baird Callicott, who developed the land ethic from Aldo Leopold, is on the other side of this debate. In "Animal Liberation: A Triangular Affair" (1980), he set the holistic land ethic against the defense of animals as individuals. In "Animal Liberation and Environmental Ethics: Back Together Again" (1988), he brought the two closer by treating domesticated animals as members of a mixed community with humans, but he went on denying wild animals the right to life and treating them as parts of environmental goals. That is why he does not appear in the list below of those who defend animals from within this theory (A10).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Peter Singer: Australian philosopher; in Practical Ethics (2nd ed., 1993), he defends an environmental ethic centered on sentient beings, present and future, that counts the deaths of wild animals caused by projects such as dams and includes no longer eating animals among the changes needed
  • Albert Schweitzer: Alsatian physician, theologian and philosopher (1875–1965), Nobel Peace Prize for 1952; his ethic of reverence for life includes animals and condemns hunting for sport and unnecessary suffering. With caveats: the ethic applies to every living being, not only to those who feel, a point Singer criticizes, and Schweitzer himself left it to each person to decide whether it requires vegetarianism
  • Dale Jamieson: American philosopher; in the article "Animal Liberation is an Environmental Ethic" (1998), he argues that animal liberation is an environmental ethic
  • Oscar Horta: Spanish philosopher; he shows where animal ethics and environmentalism diverge and argues for deciding by the interests of sentient beings
  • Catia Faria and Eze Paez: philosophers; in the article "It’s Splitsville" (2019), they explain why the criteria of animal ethics and environmental ethics are incompatible when they conflict; Paez, in "Intuitions Gone Astray: between Implausibility and Speciesism" (2015), answers objections to helping wild animals in the face of predation and procreation and argues that the intuitions against such help are implausible or speciesist
  • Daniel Dorado: researcher; he gathered and annotated the literature on the conflict between animal ethics and environmental ethics, in the thesis El conflicto entre la ética animal y la ética ambiental (2015), where he concludes that the two are opposed and that environmental ethics, in practice, is subordinated to anthropocentrism, and on intervention in nature on behalf of animals, in "Ethical Interventions in the Wild: An Annotated Bibliography" (2015)
  • Jeff Sebo: American philosopher; in Saving Animals, Saving Ourselves (2022), he ties the protection of animals to public health and the climate crisis
