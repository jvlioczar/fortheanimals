"In the natural order, animals exist for human use; killing them for food is legitimate."

Natural law and Thomism: the principle

Do good and avoid evil, knowing through reason the basic goods of human nature, such as life, knowledge and community, and never attacking them directly.

Short answer

In Thomas Aquinas's natural law, cruelty to animals is condemned mainly because it may make someone cruel to people, an indirect duty that would make it acceptable to torture an animal in secret. The tradition itself went further: the Catechism says it is contrary to human dignity to cause animals to suffer or die needlessly. Where there are alternatives, breeding and killing them for food is exactly that.

Ask a question back

If causing animals to suffer or die needlessly is contrary to human dignity, what is the need when there are alternatives at the store?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • The idea comes from Thomas Aquinas, in the Summa contra Gentiles (book III, 112): animals are ordered to human use, and cruelty toward them is condemned mainly because it may make someone cruel to people. It is the same scheme of indirect duties that would make it acceptable to torture an animal in secret, a conclusion almost nobody accepts (A14).
  • The tradition itself went further. The Catechism of the Catholic Church (§ 2418) says it is contrary to human dignity to cause animals to suffer or die needlessly, and the encyclical Laudato Si’ (2015) states that other living beings have a value of their own in God's eyes and that the Bible has no place for a tyrannical anthropocentrism.
  • "For human use" does not say which use. Where there are alternatives, breeding and killing animals for food stops being necessary, which is exactly the Catechism's criterion (C10).

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says that natural law is about human goods, and that animals, lacking reason, have no goods of that kind: they may be used, as long as there is no gratuitous cruelty.

The answer

Even granting this, gratuitous cruelty is condemned, and factory farming and slaughter where there are alternatives are needless suffering and death, in the Catechism's own terms. Catholic authors such as Charles Camosy argue from this tradition that we should stop consuming factory-farmed products.

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Charles Camosy: American Catholic theologian; in For Love of Animals (2013) he argues from Christian ethics for no longer consuming factory-farmed products
  • Pope Francis: in the encyclical Laudato Si’ (2015), stated that other living beings have a value of their own in God's eyes and criticized tyrannical anthropocentrism
