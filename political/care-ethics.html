Home / Political and ethical objections / Ethics of care

"Care applies to close relationships, not to unknown animals."

Ethics of care: the principle

Respond to the needs of the vulnerable and the dependent with attention and responsibility.

Reply

Buying the chicken is a relationship: it is the choice that orders its life and death. Domesticated animals depend on us, because we made them that way, and care also guides what we do to those affected by our choices, not only to those we know by name. The feminist care tradition itself has already applied the concept to animals.

Ask a question back

Does anyone depend more on our choices than the animal we ourselves breed to eat?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Domesticated animals are, by definition, dependent on us: we made them that way.
  • The feminist care tradition has already applied the concept to animals, as Josephine Donovan and Carol J. Adams show.
  • Care is not limited to those we know by name: it also guides what we do to those affected by our choices.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says care depends on a relationship, and we have no relationship with the chicken at the supermarket.

The answer

Buying the chicken is a relationship: it is the choice that orders its life and death (K2). An ethics of care that ignores those who depend on our choices loses its own foundation.

Where the ethics of care comes from, and the version that limits duties

The starting point is the book In a Different Voice (1982), by the psychologist Carol Gilligan. She criticized her colleague Lawrence Kohlberg's scale of moral development, on which women rarely reached the higher stages, and argued that the scale ignored another moral voice, one that speaks of relationships and responsibilities rather than abstract rules. Gilligan did not write about animals, but other authors applied that voice to them.

The version that limits duties is that of the philosopher Nel Noddings, in Caring (1984). For her, there is no obligation to be vegetarian, because we have no obligation to all animals beyond sparing them pain when possible. She says she has a responsibility to cats, having lived with them, but not to rats, with whom she has no relationship. Cochrane replies that this kind of partiality can justify prejudice, such as excluding those a community treats as outsiders, and that the state's decisions should be impartial, even if personal life allows preferences (A12, L17).

Josephine Donovan replies from within the tradition that care is not limited to those close to us: we care about distant strangers. And the philosopher Brian Luke argues that compassion for animals is the norm, and that it is stifled by religious stories about human dominion, by false information from industry and by scientific dogmas that deny animal consciousness. Cochrane doubts there is a single natural attitude: some children pet animals and some mistreat them.

Reason and emotion: a debate with rights theorists

Care theorists criticize the theories of Singer and Regan. Reason also errs. Rational arguments hide an emotion, for example when they assume, without justification, that babies must be protected. Activists are moved by compassion, not by calculations of equality. And for the ecofeminist Marti Kheel, rights presuppose antagonism, when it would be better to have a community in which no one needed to claim them.

Cochrane answers each point. That reason errs calls for humility, not for abandoning it, since it is with reason that we notice the error. Singer and Regan give reasons to protect babies and animals: sentience, or being the subject of a life. Activists have varied motives, as in any movement, and many were awakened precisely by these philosophers. And rights do not create conflicts; they limit the conflicts that inevitably exist.

His conclusion is a middle ground. Emotion sets morality in motion, and without it no one would care about anything; being moved is also legitimate in campaigns. But reason should have the last word for three reasons: it corrects prejudices feelings do not notice, it decides when equally compassionate people reach opposite conclusions, and it allows the general rules a large society needs. Defending animals with arguments, and not only with feelings, is exactly what the charge of "emotional appeal" says is missing (L31).

Care and wild animals

There is a version of care ethics that limits duties to those close to us and is used against helping wild animals. Grace Clement, in "The Ethic of Care and the Problem of Wild Animals" (2003), applies care against intervention in nature, and Clare Palmer, in Animal Ethics in Context (2010), defends a "laissez-faire" intuition toward wild animals: there would generally be no duty to assist them, because we do not have with them the relationship we have with domesticated animals (N7).

Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (2022), and Catia Faria, in "Disentangling Obligations of Assistance" (2015), reply that relationships alone do not decide who deserves help. Faria imagines three options: pressing a button that immunizes all humans against cancer, pressing another that immunizes only the people close to us, or pressing neither. Almost no one finds choosing the second acceptable. And those who use the lack of a relationship to avoid helping wild animals lose the argument against exploitation, because we have no relationship with the farmed animals we buy either (K2).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Josephine Donovan and Carol J. Adams: edited The Feminist Care Tradition in Animal Ethics (2007), which gathers the central texts of this approach
  • Lori Gruen: American philosopher; in Entangled Empathy (2015), she proposes an ethic based on empathy and attention to each animal
  • Marti Kheel: American ecofeminist (1948–2011), cofounder of Feminists for Animal Rights; in "The Liberation of Nature: A Circular Affair" (1985), she criticizes the language of rights and defends an ethic based on care
  • Brian Luke: American philosopher; in "Justice, Caring, and Animal Liberation" (1992), he argues that compassion for animals is the norm and that institutions stifle it
