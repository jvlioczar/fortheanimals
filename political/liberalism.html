"Freedom is a right of rational individuals; animals aren't subjects of liberal rights."

Liberalism: the principle

Protect individuals against harm caused by others and ensure each can live their own life. For the English philosopher John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), one of the leading names of liberalism, the only legitimate reason to limit someone's freedom is to prevent harm to others. This is the harm principle, which he sets out in On Liberty (1859).

Short answer

Liberalism already protects those who are not autonomous, such as babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities: rationality was never the condition for protection. And the harm principle works against the exclusion. If animals can be harmed, and the evidence says they can, breeding and killing them is exactly the kind of action that affects others and that liberalism exists to limit.

Ask a question back

If a baby is not a rational individual, does liberalism stop protecting them from harm?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Liberalism already protects individuals who are not autonomous, such as babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities. Rationality is not, in practice, the condition for protection.
  • The harm principle works against the exclusion: if animals can be harmed, and the evidence says they can (A1), using and killing them is exactly the kind of action that affects others.
  • Treating someone who feels as property is what liberalism rejected among humans. Owning a sentient being is not a neutral starting point (A25).

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version, defended by Cochrane himself, grants that many animals may not have an intrinsic interest in liberty, in the sense of choosing their own life the way an adult human does.

The answer

Even granting this, Cochrane concludes that they have strong interests in not suffering and in staying alive, and that a consistent liberalism must protect them. The debate about liberty changes the kind of right, not the fact that there are rights. And intensive farming violates even the most basic freedom of movement.

Negative liberty and positive liberty

In a 1958 lecture, the philosopher Isaiah Berlin (1909–1997) separated two senses of liberty. Negative liberty is freedom from interference by others: no one imprisons, forces or blocks you. Positive liberty is having the real conditions to lead your own life. The distinction helps in thinking about animals.

It is true that many animals may not want liberty for its own sake, the way an adult human wants to choose the course of their life. For most of them, what matters is how their life goes: not feeling pain, fear and hunger, being able to move, to be with others and to stay alive. So being loose does not guarantee a good life. Some animals who are not free live better than free animals: a well cared-for dog or cat, or a rescued animal living in a sanctuary, is usually better off than many animals in the wild, who live under constant threat of hunger, disease, cold and predation (N1).

It does not follow that confinement is acceptable in general. What follows is that each situation has to be assessed case by case, by its effect on the animal's well-being. To have a good life, an animal needs material conditions: space, food, shelter, health, company and safety. Those conditions do not exist on a factory farm, and they do not exist for those who live under constant threat either. A consistent liberalism protects animals from interference that harms them, which is negative liberty, and cares about the conditions they need to live well, which is the idea behind positive liberty.

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • John Stuart Mill: defended laws against cruelty to animals in Principles of Political Economy (1848) and wrote that the interests of animals count, when defending Bentham against William Whewell (1852)
  • Alasdair Cochrane: British political theorist; in Animal Rights Without Liberation (2012), he defends interest-based animal rights within the liberal tradition
  • Martha Nussbaum: American philosopher; in Justice for Animals (2023), she extends the capabilities approach, a liberal theory of justice, to animals
  • Robert Garner: British political theorist; in A Theory of Justice for Animals (2013), he argues that animals are recipients of justice
  • Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka: in Zoopolis (2011), they use liberal citizenship theory to propose political rights for animals
