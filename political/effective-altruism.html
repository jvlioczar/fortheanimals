"Resources for doing good are limited, and should go to humans first."

Effective altruism: the principle

Use evidence and reason to do the most good possible with the resources available.

Reply

The criteria of effective altruism, scale, neglectedness and tractability, favor the animal cause: the number of victims is huge and the resources devoted to it are small. Excluding animals in advance is deciding the result before doing the math, the opposite of what the approach proposes. Uncertainty about the weight of each animal exists, but it cuts both ways, and the scale is large enough to survive heavy discounts.

Ask a question back

If the rule is to do the math before choosing, why are animals left out of the math before it even starts?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Effective altruism's criteria, scale, neglectedness and tractability, favor the animal cause: the number of victims is enormous and the resources devoted to it are small.
  • Excluding animals in advance is deciding the result before doing the math, the opposite of what the approach proposes.
  • Not eating animals takes no resources from any other cause (J20).

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says it is hard to compare well-being across species, and that uncertainty favors the better-known human causes.

The answer

The uncertainty is real, but it cuts both ways, and the scale is so large that the animal cause stays a priority even with heavy discounts on the weight of each animal.

The pond argument, and what it says about animals

The philosophical starting point of effective altruism is an argument by Peter Singer, from "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972), taken up again in Practical Ethics (2nd ed., 1993). Someone walking past a shallow pond who sees a child drowning should wade in, even if it ruins their clothes, because that cost is of no moral importance comparable to the child's life. As premises: if we can prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it; absolute poverty is bad; we can prevent some of it without comparable sacrifice; therefore we ought to prevent it. Singer notes that non-consequentialists can accept the first premise too, because the duty lapses when fulfilling it would require violating rights or doing something of comparable importance.

He answers three common objections. The drop in the ocean: the argument does not require changing the total amount of poverty in the world, only preventing some of it, such as lifting one family out of destitution (K1). Looking after our own first: that is what we tend to do, but distance and membership in a community do not explain why a stranger's life should weigh less (A12). And the claim that aid only makes the population grow: birth rates tend to fall as poverty, child mortality and lack of education decline, and letting millions die now for an uncertain gain in the future would trade a certain evil for a speculation. As a public standard, Singer suggests that those earning the average income of rich countries give at least 10% of it, more than a token gesture and less than a saint's life.

The bridge to animals is in the book itself: much of the grain and soy of rich countries becomes feed, and Singer concludes that hunger is mainly a problem of distribution, not of production (J20). The structure of the argument also applies to animals, with a difference that makes it stronger: swapping an animal product for a plant one rarely costs anything of comparable importance, and in consumption the duty is not to help but to stop paying for the harm. The criticisms apply here too. The demandingness objection says the principle asks too much; the philosopher Leif Wenar, in "Poverty Is No Pond" (2011), shows that real aid is far less simple than pulling a child out of a pond; and Singer himself framed the argument for human poverty, not for animals.

The distant future

Part of effective altruism defends longtermism, the idea that positively influencing the long-term future is a key moral priority. The same logic of scale applies there: most of the sentient beings of the future will probably not be human, and a longtermism that counts only humans repeats the exclusion discussed on this page. The topic has its own page, Longtermism, the next theory on this list (N23, N26).

Wild animals as a priority

By the criteria of scale, neglectedness and tractability, wild animal suffering is an extreme case. Brian Tomasik's estimates, which he himself treats as very uncertain, put the number of terrestrial arthropods alive at any one time somewhere between a hundred quadrillion and ten quintillion, plus tens to hundreds of trillions of fish, and almost no resources are devoted to them. Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (2022), argues that, even giving each human ten times more weight, the scale would require investing more in animals.

Tractability is the most debated point. Cunha examines the efficiency objections: that acting would only be worthwhile on a large scale, that the effect would be short-term, that it would be "mopping up while the tap is running" because the animals breed again, and that it would take resources from other causes. He replies that sterilizing half of a population already prevents half of the births of young who would soon die, that research and outreach prepare larger actions, and that decisions should follow expected impact, not the worst-case scenario. He also warns against the proportion bias: judging a cause by the percentage of the problem that can be solved, rather than by the amount of harm prevented. But he admits that cost-effectiveness comparisons between this cause and others are lacking (N12, N13).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Peter Singer: one of the central references of both effective altruism and antispeciesism; in "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972) and Practical Ethics (1979; 2nd ed., 1993), he defends the duty to prevent suffering when doing so does not require sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance
  • Animal Charity Evaluators: an organization that assesses which charities help the most animals per resource donated
  • Lewis Bollard: has led, since 2015, the farm animal welfare program at Open Philanthropy, one of the largest funders in the area
  • Rethink Priorities: a research center that studies invertebrate sentience and how to compare well-being across species
  • Jeff Sebo: American philosopher; he researches the moral weight of animals, insects and artificial minds
  • Jacy Reese Anthis: cofounder of the Sentience Institute; in The End of Animal Farming (2018), he proposes strategies to end industrial animal farming
