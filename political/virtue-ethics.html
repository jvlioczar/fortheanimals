Home / Political and ethical objections / Virtue ethics

"Virtue is about human character; treating animals well matters only because it makes us better."

Virtue ethics: the principle

Cultivate virtues like compassion, justice and temperance, and avoid vices like cruelty.

Reply

Compassion is directed at those who suffer: if the animal's suffering did not matter in itself, there would be nothing for it to respond to. A just and compassionate person who pays for the avoidable suffering of others acts against their own virtues. And when there is an alternative, causing someone's death for a meal is hard to reconcile with compassion.

Ask a question back

If compassion for the animal only serves to make us better, who exactly are we feeling compassion for?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Compassion is directed at the one who suffers. If the animal's suffering did not matter in itself, there would be nothing for compassion to respond to.
  • A just and compassionate person who pays for the avoidable suffering of others acts against their own virtues.
  • Reducing the animal's value to its effect on our character is the same structure as Kant's indirect duties, which almost no one accepts (A14).

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says a virtuous person can eat meat with moderation and gratitude, without cruelty.

The answer

Moderation applies to what affects only ourselves, not to harm to others (E9). And when there is an alternative, causing someone's death for a meal is hard to reconcile with compassion.

The "cruelty-kindness" view

Many common defenses of animals say only that we should not be cruel to them and should be kind. The philosopher Tom Regan, in "The Case for Animal Rights" (1985), calls this the cruelty-kindness view and shows its limit. Cruelty and kindness describe motives and character, that is, what someone feels when acting, not whether the act is right or wrong. A kind person can do something wrong, and a great deal of harm is done with no cruelty at all, out of indifference, routine or profit, by someone who takes no pleasure in the suffering they cause (B18, A25).

So "just don't be cruel" does not answer the question about use. The counter-reply above, that a virtuous person can eat meat without cruelty, may even be right about that person's character and still leave open the question of what happens to the animal. Regan's criticism hits the versions of virtue ethics that only forbid cruelty. It does not hit authors such as Rosalind Hursthouse, for whom compassion and justice, and not merely the absence of cruelty, already condemn current practices.

Wild animals: Hursthouse's objection

Rosalind Hursthouse, in "Virtue Ethics and the Treatment of Animals" (2011), acknowledges that suffering usually predominates in the lives of wild animals, but rejects intervening to help them: it would destroy the characteristic forms of life of each species, it would come from applying general principles, which virtue ethics distrusts, and it would impose anthropocentric values.

Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (2022), replies that the rule "reject general principles" is itself a general principle, and that Hursthouse's conclusion also rests on one: preserving characteristic forms of life, however bad they are for those who live them. No one applies that principle to humans: we treat diseases that are also part of our species' life. He adds that "never intervene in nature" could not be a virtue, because it would prevent cultivating any other, and that a compassionate person would not stay indifferent to immense suffering just because they did not cause it (N6, N8).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Rosalind Hursthouse: New Zealand philosopher, one of the leading authors of present-day virtue ethics; she applied the theory to our treatment of animals and concluded that current practices are cruel and self-indulgent; in "Virtue Ethics and the Treatment of Animals" (2011), however, she rejects helping wild animals against natural harms, a point discussed above
  • Stephen R. L. Clark: British philosopher; in The Moral Status of Animals (1977), he defends vegetarianism from an ethics of Aristotelian inspiration
