"Animals lack reason, so there is no justice between us and them; the Stoic tends to their own virtue."

Stoicism: the principle

Virtue is the only good: live in accordance with reason and nature, with justice, courage, temperance and wisdom, and accept what does not depend on us.

Short answer

The Stoic exclusion of animals depends on the idea that only rational beings fall under justice, and that idea would also exclude babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities. Stoic ethics is about character, and cruelty and excess are vices whoever the victim is. Not by chance, Musonius Rufus recommended a meatless diet, and Seneca stopped eating animals as a young man.

Ask a question back

If temperance is a virtue, what virtue is there in causing another being to suffer just for the taste of a meal?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • The exclusion rests on a premise the ancient Stoics took as obvious: only rational beings fall under justice. Cicero, in On Ends (book III, 67), records Chrysippus's view that there is no justice between humans and animals. But the same premise would exclude babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities (A15, A18).
  • Stoic ethics is about the character of the one who acts. Cruelty, gluttony and indifference to suffering are vices whoever the victim is, and temperance asks precisely that food not become a whim (E9).
  • Two of the best-known Stoics stopped eating meat. Musonius Rufus recommended a simple, meatless diet, and Seneca tells in Letter 108 that as a young man he stopped eating animals under the influence of his teacher Sotion, and only went back at his father's request.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says that justice, for the Stoics, is a relationship between beings who share reason, and that animals, who do not, stand outside the moral community.

The answer

Even within that view, cruelty and excess remain vices, and the Stoic must avoid them. The Stoic image of widening circles of concern, from Hierocles, can be extended to those who feel, and interpreters of modern Stoicism, such as Massimo Pigliucci, discuss whether a Stoic should be vegetarian. An ethics that tells us to live according to nature cannot ignore that animals feel.

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Musonius Rufus: Roman Stoic philosopher (c. 30–c. 100); recommended a simple diet without meat
  • Seneca: Roman Stoic philosopher (c. 4 BC–65); tells in Letter 108 that he stopped eating meat as a young man, influenced by his teacher Sotion
