Home / Political and ethical objections / Intersectionality

"Intersectionality is about human oppressions; animals are a different cause."

Intersectionality: the principle

Forms of discrimination do not act in isolation: race, gender, class, disability and others intersect and produce experiences of oppression that cannot be explained by any one of them alone. The term was proposed by the legal scholar Kimberlé Crenshaw in 1989, based on the situation of Black women in United States courts, and it has roots in Black feminism, as in the Combahee River Collective statement (1977) and in Patricia Hill Collins's idea of a matrix of domination.

Reply

Intersectionality shows that forms of discrimination intersect. If what unites them is giving less weight to someone because of a trait that doesn't change how much they can suffer, species is one more such trait, and animals fall within the principle. And it works both ways: vegans also need to fight discrimination between people, without that being demanded only of those who defend animals.

Ask a question back

If intersectionality opposes every hierarchy based on traits that don't change the capacity to suffer, why would it stop at species?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • If what unites these struggles is the refusal to give less weight to someone's interests because of a trait that doesn't change how much they can be harmed, species is one more such trait. An intersectionality that excludes animals applies the same logic of hierarchy it claims to fight, only at the species border (A8, A11).
  • The intersections also run through animals. Slaughterhouse workers, often poor and migrants, face some of the most dangerous conditions in industry; the forced reproduction of females is central to animal farming, which feminist authors analyze; and human groups have been compared to animals to justify their exploitation, which shows that racism and speciesism have propped each other up. Authors such as Aph Ko, Syl Ko, A. Breeze Harper and Carol J. Adams discuss these intersections.
  • The principle also applies within the movement: vegans need to fight racism, sexism, ableism and other forms of discrimination, so as not to contradict themselves. All people are sentient beings, and discriminating against them for a biological trait is the same mistake speciesism makes with species (L19).

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says that bringing animals into intersectionality dilutes the agenda, diverts attention from human groups still oppressed and repeats comparisons that have been used to dehumanize people.

The answer

Care with comparisons is fair, and the case for animals stands without analogies (L22). But including animals takes nothing from anyone: moral consideration is not a resource that runs out, and the same principle that protects people who face discrimination protects those who feel in any species. Movements can have different focuses without one denying the other.

Hierarchies and unequal demands

Among vegans, intersectionality sometimes appears only as a demand: those who defend animals are required to debate every human cause before talking about animals, while no such demand is made of non-vegans, or of other movements regarding animals. That inverts intersectionality, which asks us to recognize all oppressions, not to make one struggle conditional on the approval of the others. Phrases such as "Veganism that doesn't discuss racism is just rich kids' cuisine" and "Being vegan requires being anti-capitalist", in Stop saying this, show this pattern, and the catalog answers similar demands (L13, L11).

These demands carry a speciesist hierarchy: feminist and antiracist movements are rarely asked why they leave animals out, while vegans are challenged over any human cause they don't mention. A consistent intersectionality would ask for attention to all victims, human and nonhuman, without demanding it of one side only. The pages Feminism and Anti-racism and anti-ableism deal with each movement, and the page Speciesism among vegans, in Beyond veganism, gathers other examples.

Decoloniality

Decoloniality is a Latin American school of thought that studies how the hierarchies created by colonization, of race, gender and knowledge, persist after the end of the colonies. The sociologist Aníbal Quijano called this the coloniality of power, and the philosopher María Lugones, in "Toward a Decolonial Feminism" (2010), argues that the hierarchical dichotomy between human and non-human was the central dichotomy of colonial modernity: colonized peoples were treated as non-human, almost as animals, to justify their exploitation.

The cause is also used against veganism: it is said to be a European, white, middle-class imposition on Indigenous and traditional peoples (D6, D7, L23). There is a real point here: imposing habits without listening to those who live a different reality repeats the colonial stance, and subsistence hunting and fishing are a different moral situation. But the decolonial critique attacks precisely the division between those who count and those who can be used; applying it only among humans and leaving the species border intact keeps the very logic it denounces. And the industrial farming that grows today in the Global South is not ancestral: cattle arrived in the Americas with the colonizers, and export ranching, built on large estates and deforestation, follows a colonial model.

Indigenous authors and authors from the Global South also link the two causes. The Mi'kmaq researcher Margaret Robinson defends an Indigenous veganism drawing on her people's stories (2013), and Luz Calvo and Catriona Rueda Esquibel, in Decolonize Your Diet (2015), propose recovering the plant-based food of Mexican traditions. The glossary covers the idea under "Food colonialism / vegan decoloniality". The care needed here is the same as for all intersectionality: criticize industrial exploitation and ideas, listen to the peoples affected and don't treat each community as a target, without giving up the point that animals count too.

Communication and accusations

The topic produces many communication failures. Comparisons between animal exploitation and slavery or the Holocaust can be received as offensive, especially coming from people who don't suffer those oppressions (L22). On the other hand, accusations of racism or elitism against those who defend animals sometimes replace the argument with a label (L23), and accusations of speciesism against activists of other causes can sound like contempt for their struggle.

So it's worth being careful: talk about ideas and structures, not people; listen to those who live each oppression before comparing; don't use one group's suffering as a tool for another; and recognize that different causes can have different focuses. The useful question is whether an alliance or a criticism helps the victims, not who wins the argument.

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Kimberlé Crenshaw: American legal scholar; proposed the term intersectionality in 1989
  • Patricia Hill Collins: American sociologist; developed the idea of a matrix of domination in Black Feminist Thought (1990)
  • Aph Ko and Syl Ko: American writers; in Aphro-ism (2017), they discuss racism and speciesism from Black thought
  • A. Breeze Harper: American researcher; edited Sistah Vegan (2010), with writings by Black vegan women
  • Carol J. Adams: American writer; in The Sexual Politics of Meat (1990), she links the oppression of women to that of animals
  • María Lugones: Argentine philosopher (1944–2020); in "Toward a Decolonial Feminism" (2010), she identifies the dichotomy between human and non-human as central to colonial modernity
  • Margaret Robinson: Mi'kmaq researcher from Canada; defends an Indigenous veganism in "Veganism and Mi'kmaq Legends" (2013)
  • Lori Gruen: American philosopher; in Entangled Empathy (2015), she defends an empathy that includes animals in struggles for justice
