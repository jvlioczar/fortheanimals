"Human pleasures are worth more: better to be a human dissatisfied than a pig satisfied."

Utilitarianism: the principle

Maximize well-being and minimize suffering for everyone affected, counting each one's interest equally.

Reply

Bentham, one of the founders of utilitarianism, already said that the question is not whether animals can reason, but whether they can suffer. Mill's line compares kinds of pleasure, but it does not erase anyone's pain: even if intellectual pleasures were worth more, an animal's suffering would still count. And scale changes the sum: tens of billions of land animals and trillions of fish a year.

Ask a question back

If a higher pleasure is worth more, does the pleasure of a taste make up for a whole life of suffering?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Bentham, one of the founders of utilitarianism, already said the question is not whether animals can reason, but whether they can suffer. Excluding them contradicts the theory itself.
  • Mill's line compares kinds of pleasure, but it does not zero out animal suffering. Even if intellectual pleasures were worth more, an animal's pain would still count (B2, A6).
  • Scale changes the sum: tens of billions of land animals and trillions of fish every year.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version grants that animals count, but says that farming with a good life and a painless death could be justified in the calculation, because it brings happy animals into existence (B7).

The answer

That is a real debate within utilitarianism, but it does not describe actual animal farming, in which most animals live in intensive systems. And the idea that one animal can be replaced by another faces serious objections (B7, B2).

Why utilitarianism includes animals so easily

Cochrane sums up the reason in two words: utilitarianism is welfarist and egalitarian. Welfarist because the only good that counts is well-being, whether understood as pleasure, as in Bentham, or as preference satisfaction, as in Singer, and animals have both. Egalitarian because like cases must be treated alike: if the goal is to promote well-being, leaving out those who have well-being because they belong to another species is as arbitrary as leaving out someone of another race or class.

The idea is old. Before Bentham, the Scottish philosopher Francis Hutcheson already grounded animal rights in the capacity for pleasure and pain, and the English clergyman Humphrey Primatt defended, in 1776, a Christian ethic against cruelty to animals based on their suffering. Much earlier, in the third century, the philosopher Porphyry had said that sensation is what makes it possible to be harmed.

The objections of those who think utilitarianism goes too far

The philosophers H. J. McCloskey and R. G. Frey denied that animals have interests. A plant benefits from being watered and a machine from being oiled, yet they do not have interests; having an interest would require desiring the good in question. The reply is that sentient animals show every sign of desiring food, shelter, company and play, which plants and machines do not. We have no direct access to a dog's mind, but we have none to another human's either, and in both cases we infer experience from behavior and anatomy (A1).

The psychologist Lewis Petrinovich argued that preferring one's own species is a natural fact, because every social animal favors its own. Cochrane replies that the description is false, since animals associate with other species and kill members of their own; that something being natural does not make it right; and that if belonging to a group were enough, it would also hold for race and sex (A12, C1).

Which utilitarianism? Classical or preference, total or prior existence

Classical utilitarianism, that of Bentham, Mill and Henry Sidgwick, judges acts by the pleasure and pain they produce. Preference utilitarianism, which Peter Singer adopts in Practical Ethics (2nd ed., 1993) following R. M. Hare, judges them by how far they satisfy or frustrate the preferences of everyone affected. The difference shows up in the case of death. For the classical view, painlessly killing someone who does not know they will die is wrong only because of the future happiness lost and the effects on others, such as the fear of those who know they could be next. For the preference view, killing someone who prefers to go on living already frustrates that preference, even if the victim is not around to regret it. That is why only the second gives direct weight to self-awareness: those with plans for the future have more preferences to lose (B2, A20).

Singer also explains why he starts from utilitarianism. An ethical judgment requires a universal point of view, in which my interests do not count for more just because they are mine. Whoever universalizes the decision they would make out of self-interest comes to weigh the interests of everyone affected, and arrives, as a first position, at a form of utilitarianism. He presents this as a provisional argument, not a proof: the burden falls on those who want to go further, for example with rights or the sanctity of life, and he grants that those other theories are also universal in this sense. For animals, the central point does not depend on accepting utilitarianism: if the point of view is universal, species does not change the weight of an interest (E7).

The other choice concerns population. On the total view, increasing the number of happy lives is a good, just as much as improving those that already exist. On the prior-existence view, only beings who already exist, or who will exist anyway, count. The first opens the way to the replaceability argument: killing a happy animal and raising another in its place would leave the sum unchanged (B7). The second has to explain an asymmetry: why is it wrong knowingly to bring into the world a being who will have a miserable life, if no one has a duty to bring into the world a being who will have a happy one? Singer admits that neither view fully satisfies him. In the 1st edition (1979) he combined the two; in the 2nd he replaced the combination with preference utilitarianism, without treating the question as settled.

The debate between the two versions is still alive among utilitarians themselves. The researcher Brian Tomasik, in "Hedonistic vs. Preference Utilitarianism", gathers intuitions on both sides, proposes giving more weight to the brain systems tied to pleasure and pain, and notes that very strong preferences against extreme suffering may lead to a negative-leaning view that prioritizes reducing suffering. For animals, both versions reach the same starting point: animals have good and bad experiences and they have preferences, and in both cases species doesn't change the weight of an interest.

The line on this page is Mill's, and Singer himself criticizes it. Mill said that those who know both kinds of pleasure prefer the higher ones, and that it is therefore better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. Singer asks whether Socrates would really know what it is like to live the other's life. And he notes that Mill's argument also compares humans with each other: the reasoning that puts human life above a pig's would put one person's life above that of another with lesser cognitive capacities, a conclusion almost everyone now rejects. In any case, the line is about which life one would prefer to live, and does not license making those who live the other one suffer (A5, A6).

The weak points that defenders of animals themselves point out

Singer distinguishes self-conscious beings, who see themselves as existing over time and have plans for the future, from merely conscious beings. Killing the former frustrates their desires; killing the latter, if they had a good life, died painlessly and were replaced by others who would not otherwise exist, may not be wrong in itself. In Practical Ethics (2nd ed., 1993), Singer is categorical only about the great apes: chimpanzees, gorillas and orangutans should at once get the same protection against being killed that we give humans. For whales, dolphins, monkeys, dogs, cats, pigs, cattle, sheep and goats, he speaks of varying degrees of confidence, perhaps reaching all mammals, depending on how far we are willing to extend the benefit of the doubt. Fish are, for him, the clearest case of an animal that is conscious but not a person; the assumption that hens are not self-conscious he himself calls questionable, and the one about ducks, dubious. So the theory would in principle allow breeding and killing some animals under those conditions, but never in factory farming, where their lives are not good. And Singer accepts this reasoning only at the critical level, that of theoretical reflection; at the intuitive level, that of everyday rules, in the distinction drawn by R. M. Hare, he recommends not killing animals for food unless needed to survive. His reason is that killing them for food leads us to treat them as objects, and it is hard to cultivate equal consideration for those we keep eating for pleasure. Cochrane reads this rule as a slippery-slope argument, the worry that society would slide back into intensive farming, and finds it weak; the criticism hits that reading, not the reason Singer himself gives. Either way, the answer to replaceability need not depend on the practical rule, but on the harm death causes and on the real conditions of farming (B2, B7). Tom Regan objects that, for utilitarianism, animals matter as receptacles of well-being, not as individuals.

Another criticism is that the calculation never forbids anything absolutely: a painful experiment on a few animals would be acceptable if it benefited many. The main utilitarian answer is Hare's two levels. In everyday life we calculate in a hurry, with incomplete information and with our own interests at stake, so the consequences are better when we follow firm rules, such as not harming and not killing, than when we redo the sum case by case. Singer compares these rules to a good tennis coach's instructions: now and then an unorthodox shot pays off, but most of the time those who follow the instructions play better. Calculation is kept for the critical level, that of reflecting on which rules to adopt and on truly exceptional cases. Critics such as Bernard Williams object that, if the rules hold only because it is useful to follow them without calculating, the theory must be partly hidden from those who follow them, which he called "Government House" utilitarianism. Similar reasoning applies to institutions: many utilitarians defend an absolute legal ban on torturing humans, because in the long run allowing it would have disastrous effects on trust in institutions. The same long-term reasoning can lead a utilitarian to support legal rights for animals. And for Singer, those who accept painful experiments on animals but refuse the same experiments on humans with similar mental capacities discriminate by species (I1).

The consequences are debated too. The researcher Steven Davis argued that farming with pasture for large herbivores would kill fewer animals than crops alone, because of the animals killed in cultivation. Gaverick Matheny replied that, since crops need far less land to produce the same protein, a plant-based diet still kills fewer (H2). The models defended vary, but all these authors agree that intensive farming should end (E6). The philosopher Gary Varner (1957–2023), in Personhood, Ethics, and Animal Cognition (2012), applied Hare's two levels to animals and reached an intermediate position: factory farming is probably unacceptable, but, since he regards the animals usually kept on farms as replaceable, some forms of farming would be permissible, and he argues for eating less meat and choosing where it comes from. It is a critique of factory farming, not a case for ending the use of animals, and the objections to replaceability apply to it too (B7, B2).

Wild animals in the utilitarian calculation

If what matters is suffering, and not who caused it, utilitarianism cannot ignore the suffering of wild animals, which comes above all from hunger, disease, parasitism and death soon after birth. The equivalence thesis, defended by James Rachels and, with qualifications, by Singer, says that there is no intrinsic difference between causing an avoidable harm and letting it happen, all else being equal (N6). Singer grants differences that usually come with the distinction, such as motivation and the certainty of the outcome, and so does not condemn the omission as if it were the act itself; but he holds that these differences are not enough to make it acceptable. And diminishing marginal utility means that the same resource does more good when it reaches those who are worse off, which strengthens the reason to help animals in extreme situations.

Singer himself was skeptical: in Animal Liberation, he wrote that attempts to change ecosystems on a large scale tend to do more harm than good. According to Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (2022), he changed his position in a 2016 lecture, coming to treat the subject as an important ethical question and to support research. People also ask whether the theory would lead to drastic measures, such as killing animals to stop them from reproducing. Cunha replies that, even for those who look only at consequences, there are good reasons for a general rule against killing: killing reinforces the idea that animals are disposable and confuses help with environmental management, with worse effects in the long run (N17, N11).

There is an internal disagreement. Classical utilitarians add up well-being and suffering; suffering-focused authors, such as Brian Tomasik and Magnus Vinding, give priority to reducing it. The difference matters here, because the balance of welfare in nature is uncertain (Groff and Ng, 2019) and may even be positive (Browning and Veit, 2023). Cunha argues that, even with a positive balance, there would still be an enormous number of lives going very badly, and that the reason to help those lives does not depend on the balance (N1).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Jeremy Bentham: English philosopher (1748–1832), founder of utilitarianism; he wrote that the question is not whether animals can reason or talk, but whether they can suffer
  • John Stuart Mill: defended Bentham on this point: if a practice causes more pain to animals than it gives pleasure to humans, it is immoral
  • Peter Singer: Australian philosopher; in Animal Liberation (1975), he applied the principle of equal consideration of interests to animals
  • Yew-Kwang Ng: economist; in 1995 he proposed welfare biology, the study of the well-being of animals in the wild; in 2019, with Zach Groff, he corrected an error in his original model and concluded that the balance between suffering and well-being in nature is uncertain
  • David Pearce: British philosopher; in The Hedonistic Imperative (1995), he proposes using biotechnology to abolish the suffering of all sentient beings
  • Magnus Vinding: Danish philosopher; in Suffering-Focused Ethics (2020), he argues for prioritizing the reduction of suffering
  • Tobias Baumann: researcher and author of the s-risks.org blog and of the Center for Reducing Suffering; he studies large-scale suffering risks, including those of animals
  • Brian Tomasik: American essayist; in Essays on Reducing Suffering, he argues for reducing the suffering of all sentient beings, including wild animals and invertebrates
  • James Rachels: American philosopher (1941–2003); in Created from Animals (1990), he uses Darwinism to criticize the idea that humans have a special moral status and defends what he calls moral individualism
  • Francis Hutcheson: Scottish philosopher (1694–1746), a forerunner of utilitarianism; in A System of Moral Philosophy, published in 1755 after his death, he attributed rights to animals because they can feel pleasure and pain
  • Gaverick Matheny: American researcher; in "Least Harm" (2003), he replies that a crop-only diet kills fewer animals than one with pasture-raised meat, and in "Utilitarianism and Animals" (2005) he defends a plant-based diet from utilitarianism
  • Steve F. Sapontzis: American philosopher; in "Predation" (1984) and in Morals, Reason, and Animals (1987), he defends animals from the goals of everyday morality, among them reducing suffering, and concludes that, when it can be done without causing more harm, we should protect prey from predation. He does not present himself as a strict utilitarian, but his argument about predation is consequentialist
  • Tyler Cowen: American economist; in "Policing Nature" (2003), he argues that utilitarian, rights-based and holistic standards all point toward modest measures to limit predation, starting with not subsidizing predators. Some of the measures he accepts, such as hunting predators, clash with other defenses of animals
