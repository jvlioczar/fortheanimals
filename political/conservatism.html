"Eating meat is tradition, and conservatives preserve what has worked; animal rights are a modern invention."

Conservatism: the principle

Preserve the institutions, customs and bonds that have proved valuable over time, change with prudence and cultivate duties toward those who depend on us.

Short answer

Conservatism preserves what is good and corrects, with prudence, what has proved wrong; being old was never enough. And industrial animal farming is not even tradition: sheds, confinement and slaughter at scale are only a few decades old. Roger Scruton himself, who denied animals rights, defended duties toward them and condemned factory farming.

Ask a question back

If conservatives preserve what is good, what is there worth preserving in a shed with thousands of confined animals?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Conservatism never defended keeping everything that is old: it preserves what is good and corrects, with prudence, what has proved wrong. Being a tradition is not enough to make something good (D1).
  • What exists today is not the traditional farming the word evokes. Industrial animal farming, with sheds, confinement and slaughter at scale, is only a few decades old and is itself a break with the rural way of life conservatives tend to value.
  • The English conservative philosopher Roger Scruton, who denied that animals have rights, argued in Animal Rights and Wrongs (1996) that we have duties toward them and condemned factory farming. And Matthew Scully, a speechwriter for President George W. Bush, wrote Dominion (2002), one of the harshest critiques of factory farming, from conservative and Christian values.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version, Scruton's, says that rights belong to those who can have duties, and that animals, who cannot, receive piety and care from us, but not rights.

The answer

Even within that vocabulary, Scruton concluded that factory farming violates the duties we have toward animals. And the idea that only those with duties have rights would leave out babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities (A22). For the animals, what matters is whether the harm is avoided, not what the duty is called.

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Roger Scruton: English philosopher (1944–2020); in Animal Rights and Wrongs (1996) he denied that animals have rights but defended duties toward them and condemned factory farming
  • Matthew Scully: American writer and speechwriter for President George W. Bush; author of Dominion (2002), a conservative and Christian critique of factory farming, hunting and whaling
