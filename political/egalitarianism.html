Home / Political and ethical objections / Egalitarianism and prioritarianism

"Equality is among human citizens; animals belong to another category."

Egalitarianism and prioritarianism: the principle

Reduce unjust inequalities and give priority to those who are worst off.

Reply

If priority goes to those who are worse off, animals in intensive farming and many wild animals are among the worst-off beings there are. Egalitarianism fights precisely the disadvantages nobody chose, and nobody chooses the species they are born into. Excluding animals is deciding in advance who can be worse off, which contradicts the principle itself.

Ask a question back

If priority goes to the worst off, why can't the worst off be an animal?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • If priority goes to the worst off, animals in intensive farming and many wild animals are among the worst-off beings there are.
  • That inequality between species is natural does not make it just: egalitarianism fights precisely the disadvantages no one chose.
  • Excluding animals is deciding in advance who can be worst off, which contradicts the principle.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says the relevant equality is equality of political status, which only makes sense among members of a society.

The answer

Even if political status is discussed separately, priority to the worst off refers to well-being, and an animal's well-being can go badly. Among theories that disagree on almost everything, this is a point of convergence.

Equality is a moral principle, not a statement of fact

Peter Singer, in Practical Ethics (2nd ed., 1993), points out that people differ in strength, intelligence and sensitivity. If equality depended on all of us being equal in these capacities, a society that handed out rights by intelligence tests would be just, because it would rest on real differences. Equality is a moral principle: similar interests should get similar weight, whoever has them. Real differences change the content of what is owed to someone, such as the kind of education or care, but not the weight of their interests. It is the same step that takes the principle beyond species (A8, A13).

Singer calls equal consideration of interests a minimal principle of equality, because it does not require equal treatment. By diminishing marginal utility, the same amount of a resource is worth more to those who have little: a handful of rice changes the day of someone who is starving and does almost nothing for someone who eats well. That is why the principle usually favors the worse off. But not always. In his example, with medicine for only one earthquake victim, saving the leg of the less injured person can be better than saving a toe of the more injured one, even though this widens the gap between them.

This is the point where egalitarians and prioritarians part ways with Singer. For the egalitarian, inequality is bad in itself; for the prioritarian, such as Derek Parfit in "Equality or Priority?" (1991), a benefit is worth more the worse off its recipient is, beyond what the sum would indicate. On all three readings, animals in very bad conditions, such as those in intensive farming, weigh heavily, and none of the three allows discounting an interest because of the species of whoever has it.

Wild animals: who is worse off

Wild animals are a central case for egalitarianism and prioritarianism. Most animals are born into species that have hundreds, thousands or millions of offspring, and almost all of them die shortly after birth, from hunger, cold, disease or predation. If the reason to help grows stronger the worse off someone is, these lives are among those that most call for help (N1).

The idea that disadvantages no one chose are unjust, discussed by authors such as Nils Holtug and Kasper Lippert-Rasmussen, also answers the image of a "fair nature" in which every animal has weapons to escape. Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (2022), notes that having similar capacities does not guarantee the same luck, and that predators pick precisely the weakest (N4).

He also replies to those who say that welfare in nature may have a positive balance, as Groff and Ng (2019) and Browning and Veit (2023) argue. For egalitarian, prioritarian and sufficientarian theories, a positive aggregate balance does not cancel the reasons to help those who are very badly off, just as the wealth of some does not lessen the weight of others' hunger.

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Peter Singer: Australian philosopher; in Animal Liberation (1975) and Practical Ethics (1979; 2nd ed., 1993), he argues that equality is a moral principle, not a statement of fact, and that the principle of equal consideration of interests applies to all sentient beings
  • Peter Vallentyne: philosopher; in the article "Of Mice and Men: Equality and Animals" (2005), he discusses what egalitarianism implies once animals are counted
  • Nils Holtug: Danish philosopher; author of "Equality for Animals" (2007)
  • Oscar Horta: Spanish philosopher; in "Egalitarianism and Animals" (2016), he argues that egalitarianism gives priority to animals, because they are worse off
  • Catia Faria: Portuguese philosopher; she argues that equality and priority to the worst off also apply to wild animals
  • Luciano Carlos Cunha: Brazilian philosopher, with a PhD in ethics and political philosophy and coordinator of Animal Ethics in Brazil; he defends equal consideration of the interests of all sentient beings and has devoted works to the suffering of wild animals, who are often the worst off
