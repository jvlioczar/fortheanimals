"Only rational beings are ends in themselves; toward animals we have, at most, indirect duties."

Deontology and Kantianism: the principle

Treat beings who have value of their own as ends, and never merely as means.

Reply

If only reason gave value, torturing an animal in secret, with no effect on anyone, would not be wrong, a conclusion almost nobody accepts. Many humans whom the theory protects as ends also lack moral autonomy. And interpreters of the Kantian tradition itself, such as Christine Korsgaard, argue that sentient animals are ends in themselves, because they have a good of their own.

Ask a question back

Would torturing a dog in secret, with nobody knowing, be wrong?

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Kant said cruelty to animals is wrong because it hardens human character. It would follow that torturing an animal in secret, with no effect on anyone, would not be wrong, a conclusion almost no one accepts (A14).
  • Present-day interpreters of the Kantian tradition itself, such as Christine Korsgaard, argue that sentient animals are ends in themselves, because they have a good of their own.
  • Tom Regan, within a rights theory, showed that being the "subject of a life" is enough to have inherent value.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says dignity comes from moral autonomy, which animals lack.

The answer

Moral autonomy is also missing in many humans the theory protects as ends. If protection does not depend on it in those cases, it cannot depend on it in the case of animals (A18, A19).

Kantian autonomy and preference autonomy

Kant gave human dignity a secular basis: humans would have dignity not because they have a soul, but because they are autonomous, able to give themselves the moral law. This idea lies behind much of today's language of human rights, and it is also why the argument that animals lack dignity, because they are not autonomous, is still so common (A14, A20).

Tom Regan grants that few animals are autonomous in Kant's sense, but proposes another sense: those who have preferences and can act to satisfy them are autonomous. This preference autonomy is one of the conditions for being a subject of a life, and every subject of a life has inherent value and a right to be treated with respect, never merely as a means. The legal scholar Steven M. Wise and the philosopher Evelyn Pluhar also defend rights for animals based on their capacity to act.

The most serious objection comes from a defender of animals. For Cochrane, acting to satisfy preferences is not the autonomy that grounds the idea of a person: if an animal does not choose and revise its own ends, it is unclear why using it without making it suffer would be wrong in itself. Regan and Gary Francione reply that use as a resource already disrespects the value of a subject of a life, and the debate goes on (B18, A24).

Regan's criterion also has a cost. He says we can be confident that mammals over one year old are subjects of a life, and although he does not exclude the others, the logic of the criterion leaves out some sentient beings, animal and human. One way out, proposed by the philosopher Joel Feinberg as early as 1974, is to ground rights in interests: whoever has interests can have rights.

Nathan Nobis, in Animals & Ethics 101 (2016), sums up the structure of this way out. Regan's rights view starts from the second formulation of the categorical imperative, to treat others always as ends and never merely as means, and changes who counts as an end in itself. An inclusive Kantian version asks us to treat all conscious beings as ends, not only rational ones. The principle stays the same; what changes is the circle of those it protects. So the Kantian case for animals does not need to abandon Kant, but to show that rationality is not what actually explains why babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities also count as ends (A18, A19).

Do rights require duties?

The philosophers Michael Fox and Carl Cohen argued that rights are reciprocal: only those who can fulfil duties have rights, so animals would have none. Babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities cannot fulfil duties either, and Cohen replies that they belong to a kind, the human species, whose members normally have that capacity (A18, A21).

The philosopher Nathan Nobis showed the problem with this reply. Every individual belongs to many groups, and nothing explains why species should be the decisive one. Humans also belong to the group of living organisms, which in general reciprocate nothing; if rights depended on what is typical of the group, that group would deny rights to humans themselves. It makes more sense to assign rights by the characteristics of each individual.

Some people also think that knocking down the word "rights" is enough to knock down the cause. Nobis replies that the central question of animal ethics is a different one: whether each use of animals is permissible, obligatory or wrong, and why. A use can be wrong for other reasons even if animals have no rights, and Peter Singer defends animals with hardly any talk of rights. Concluding that a use is permissible because animals supposedly have no rights is a leap (A21, E7).

Nobis's thesis is contested within animal advocacy itself. Regan, Francione and Cochrane, each in their own way, argue that the language of rights is indispensable, not just a way of speaking: only it would protect each individual against being sacrificed in a calculation of benefits to others. Both sides agree that the absence of the word is not enough to permit a use; they disagree about how much the word is needed to protect animals.

Rights and wild animals

Tom Regan argued that, toward wild animals, the duty is to "let them be": rights forbid harming them, but do not require protecting them from predators and disease. Gary Francione goes in the same direction by admitting only negative rights. Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (2022), challenges both. The charge of paternalism does not apply, because nothing suggests that animals prefer not to be helped: fleeing from someone who approaches shows fear of a threat, not refusal of help. And Regan's argument that wild animals have the capacity to escape dangers slides from the species, which does survive, to individuals, most of whom are young and do not survive (N8, N4).

Other authors within the rights tradition defend positive duties. Henry Shue, in Basic Rights (1980), holds that the basic rights to security and subsistence also require protection and aid from others. Kyle Johannsen and Jeff McMahan, who accept deontological constraints, argue for helping wild animals. And Cunha notes that deontology limits the means of helping, such as the ban on killing, but does not support an absolute ban on intervening: the maxim "never intervene in nature" could not hold as a universal law, because no agent survives without intervening (N6, N13).

There is a further tension. Those who reject helping out of respect for rights sometimes appeal to consequences, saying that preventing predation would cause an explosion of prey and more deaths from starvation. But a theory that denies that consequences decide what is right cannot turn to them only in this case. The disagreement also runs the other way: Cunha finds it absurd to see exploitation as wrong in itself, regardless of how it affects animals, and abolitionists and followers of Regan disagree with him on this point (N17).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Tom Regan: American philosopher (1938–2017); in The Case for Animal Rights (1983), he argues that every subject of a life has inherent value and rights
  • Christine Korsgaard: American Kantian philosopher; in Fellow Creatures (2018), she argues that we have direct duties to animals
  • Gary Francione: American legal scholar; he defends the right of animals not to be property and the abolition of all use
  • Leonard Nelson: German neo-Kantian philosopher (1882–1927); he defended rights for animals in the early twentieth century
  • Kyle Johannsen: Canadian philosopher; in Wild Animal Ethics (2020), he argues that wild animal suffering is a moral and political problem
  • Steven M. Wise: American legal scholar (1950–2024), founder of the Nonhuman Rights Project; in Unlocking the Cage (2002), he argues for the legal personhood of animals based on their practical autonomy
  • Paola Cavalieri: Italian philosopher; in The Animal Question (Italian ed. 1999; English ed. 2001), she argues for extending to animals the basic protection of human rights, setting aside the dispute between utilitarianism and Kantianism; with Peter Singer, she edited The Great Ape Project (1993), which asks for the great apes the right to life, the protection of individual liberty and the prohibition of torture
  • Evelyn Pluhar: American philosopher; in Beyond Prejudice (1995), she defends moral rights for animals based on their capacity to act in pursuit of goals
  • Nathan Nobis: American philosopher; in "Carl Cohen's 'Kind' Arguments For Animal Rights and Against Human Rights" (2004), he shows that the "kind" argument would deny rights to humans too
  • Jeff McMahan: American philosopher; he is not a utilitarian and accepts deontological constraints on how persons may be treated; he condemned raising animals for food in "Eating Animals the Nice Way" (2008) and defended helping wild animals in "The Meat Eaters" (2010) and "The Moral Problem of Predation" (2015). With caveats: in the 2010 piece he suggested that it would be good, if it were safe, to let carnivorous species gradually go extinct, a much-contested idea, and his account on which death harms less those who are less psychologically connected to their own future is criticized by other animal advocates, such as Luciano Carlos Cunha
  • Charles K. Fink: American philosopher; in "The Predation Argument" (2005), he examines the argument that, if predators may kill their prey, humans may also kill animals for food, rejects it, and argues that it is not absurd for there to be a duty to protect animals from predators, even if its practical application is limited
