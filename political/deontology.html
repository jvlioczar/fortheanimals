"Only rational beings are ends in themselves; toward animals we have, at most, indirect duties."

Deontology and Kantianism: the principle

Treat beings who have value of their own as ends, and never merely as means.

Why excluding animals contradicts the principle

  • Kant said cruelty to animals is wrong because it hardens human character. It would follow that torturing an animal in secret, with no effect on anyone, would not be wrong, a conclusion almost no one accepts (A20).
  • Present-day interpreters of the Kantian tradition itself, such as Christine Korsgaard, argue that sentient animals are ends in themselves, because they have a good of their own.
  • Tom Regan, within a rights theory, showed that being the "subject of a life" is enough to have inherent value.

The strongest counter-reply

The most serious version says dignity comes from moral autonomy, which animals lack.

The answer

Moral autonomy is also missing in many humans the theory protects as ends. If protection does not depend on it in those cases, it cannot depend on it in the case of animals (A11, A12).

Who defends animals from within this theory

Authors, activists and groups who start from this theory and show why it has to include animals and other sentient beings.

  • Tom Regan: American philosopher (1938–2017); in The Case for Animal Rights (1983), he argues that every subject of a life has inherent value and rights
  • Christine Korsgaard: American Kantian philosopher; in Fellow Creatures (2018), she argues that we have direct duties to animals
  • Gary Francione: American legal scholar; he defends the right of animals not to be property and the abolition of all use
  • Leonard Nelson: German neo-Kantian philosopher (1882–1927); he defended rights for animals in the early twentieth century
  • Kyle Johannsen: Canadian philosopher; in Wild Animal Ethics (2020), he argues that wild animal suffering is a moral and political problem
