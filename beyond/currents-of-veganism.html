The currents of the vegan movement

All vegans want the end of animal exploitation. The currents disagree about why, with whom and how to get there. A map of the ends, the means and the main debates.

This is a map, not an official classification. Names vary from country to country, the currents overlap and the same person can belong to several of them.

The end and the means

What defines veganism is the end: the abolition of animal exploitation. Leslie Cross, one of the early officers of The Vegan Society, wrote in 1951: "One may become a vegetarian for a variety of reasons - humanitarian, health, or mere preference for such a diet; the principle is a matter of personal feeling, and varies accordingly. Veganism, however, is a principle - that man has no right to exploit the creatures for his own ends - and no variation occurs."

A simple way to place each position comes from the philosopher Nathan Nobis, who starts from a case Tom Regan presents in Empty Cages (2004) and proposes a scale of four answers to the question of what we may do to animals. (A) Almost any use is permissible, because we have no duties to them. (B) Seriously harming them, by causing pain, suffering or death, is permissible if they are kept in comfortable cages. (C) It is permissible if, in addition, they are treated gently and killed painlessly. (D) Seriously harming them is generally wrong, even with comfortable cages, gentle treatment and a painless death. Nobis calls those whose final goal is C welfarists, and those whose goal is D animal rights advocates. Veganism is at D, and the currents below disagree about how to get there. The scale also helps to place those who defend "humane slaughter" (B1, B2 and B18).

The currents disagree about the means: which strategies work best, which other struggles the animal cause is tied to and which reasons it rests on. This is common in social movements. Feminism has liberal, radical, Marxist, Black and intersectional currents. The anti-racist movement has been divided between integration and autonomy, between reforming laws and changing structures. In none of these cases did disagreement about means put anyone outside the movement. That is why this guide criticizes lines such as V64, "Anyone who supports step-by-step change isn't really vegan" and V65, "Anyone who won't accept gradual change doesn't understand how the world works".

Each current answers a different question

The labels below are not alternatives on the same list. They answer four different questions, which is why one person can hold several at once.

The main currents

CurrentWhat it holdsNames and notes
Abolitionist veganismVeganism is the moral baseline of the movement. The task is to educate people toward veganism and the end of the property status of animals. It rejects campaigns for welfare reforms, on the view that they make exploitation more acceptable.Gary Francione and the "abolitionist approach". It is a position about strategy: every vegan is an abolitionist about the end.
Pragmatic veganismIt accepts the same end and argues for reaching it in stages: legal reforms, corporate campaigns, reducing consumption and changes that make it easier for more people to join.Tobias Leenaert, Henry Spira and organizations such as The Humane League and Mercy For Animals. Also called liberal-pragmatic or regulationist. Critics call it "welfarist" or "new welfarist", pejoratively. Not to be confused with welfarism proper, which accepts using animals as long as there is less suffering, and is not veganism.
Intersectional veganismThe oppression of animals is tied to other oppressions, such as sexism, racism, ableism and class exploitation, and should be fought together with them.It brings together feminist veganism (Carol J. Adams), Black veganism (Aph Ko, Syl Ko, A. Breeze Harper), anti-ableist veganism (Sunaura Taylor) and, in Brazil, "popular veganism", tied to the left and to the idea of making veganism accessible to working-class people.
Veganarchism and total liberationVeganism and anarchism complete each other: animal exploitation is one of the forms of domination a society without hierarchies has to abolish.Brian A. Dominick, who coined the term in 1995, and Steven Best. See the page on anarchism.
Liberal veganismThe end of animal exploitation is compatible with liberal democracy and a market economy, and can advance through rights, laws, consumer choices and innovation.Alasdair Cochrane, Martha Nussbaum, Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka. See the page on liberalism.
Antispeciesist-sentientist veganismThe foundation is sentience, and veganism is one of the consequences of antispeciesism. So the concern extends to all sentient beings: wild animals, invertebrates and possible future minds.Oscar Horta, Luciano Carlos Cunha, the organization Animal Ethics and the Sentientism movement. See antispeciesism and sentientism.
Technological veganismTechnology is the fastest route to ending large-scale exploitation: plant-based meat, cultivated meat and precision fermentation give people the same comfort without the animals and open the way to then abolishing exploitation.This is not an established label: we use the name for a position that exists. Jacy Reese Anthis, in The End of Animal Farming, and the Good Food Institute are examples. See the debate on the definition of veganism.
Rationalist or evidence-based veganismFoundations and strategies should be chosen by reason and by the best available evidence on what most reduces suffering and exploitation, with a willingness to revise positions.It is the approach of this guide and of the book Veganismo Baseado em Evidências (Evidence-Based Veganism, in Portuguese). It is close to the work of organizations such as Faunalytics, Sentience Institute, Animal Charity Evaluators and Rethink Priorities, and is adopted by many sentientists.

There are also currents defined by their foundation: rights-based veganism, from Tom Regan; veganism tied to the ethics of care and ecofeminism; and religiously inspired veganism, such as the kind that starts from ahimsa, the nonviolence of Jainism, Hinduism and Buddhism.

Currents and meta-currents

Some of these positions really do compete for the same space. Abolitionists and pragmatists give opposite answers to the same question: do reform campaigns help or hinder? Others do not compete with each other. A person can be sentientist in foundation, intersectional in political analysis and pragmatic in strategy.

Evidence-based veganism works as a meta-current, or a meta-strategy: it does not pick a side in advance, but a way of deciding. Much of the dispute between abolitionists and pragmatists, for example, depends on facts: what happens to consumption, to laws and to public opinion after each kind of campaign. Those are questions research can help answer. Technological veganism is similar: it is a bet about what will work, and it can be combined with different foundations and political analyses.

Communicating well: evidence and stories

Whatever the current, the defense of animals depends on how it reaches people. A sound philosophical argument or an accurate scientific finding loses force when it is presented in a biased, exaggerated or confusing way, and a mistake that gets exposed can cost the trust of those who were listening. That is why communication deserves as much care as content.

An article published in Nature in 2020 by researchers at the Winton Centre for Risk and Evidence Communication, at the University of Cambridge, proposes five rules for communicating evidence: inform rather than persuade; offer balance, not false balance; disclose uncertainties; state the quality of the evidence; and inoculate against misinformation. The authors cite the philosopher Onora O'Neill: information should be accessible, comprehensible, usable and assessable. In practice, they suggest:

  • addressing the audience's real questions and concerns, not only those that interest the speaker;
  • anticipating misunderstandings and explaining them before they arise;
  • not cherry-picking findings;
  • presenting benefits and possible harms in the same way, so they can be compared fairly;
  • avoiding the biases of the format itself, for example by showing the same figure from both the positive and the negative side;
  • using numbers, alone or together with words, rather than vague words only;
  • owning uncertainty without apologizing, by showing the range of possible outcomes;
  • saying when you don't know, and how and when you plan to find out;
  • highlighting the quality and relevance of the evidence, for example by describing where the data come from;
  • laying out the content clearly and giving the sources.

A second article, also in Nature, in 2021, points out that stories are a powerful tool: people remember concrete cases, characters and consequences far better than tables. But a story has to stay faithful to the evidence and respect the people in it, and it should not replace the data.

How this applies to defending animals

  • Inform, don't win. The aim is for the person to understand what happens to animals and why it matters, and to decide from there. People who feel manipulated tend to reject the whole message (see When logic isn't enough).
  • Keep the ethical argument apart from the facts. The central argument, that there is no good reason to cause avoidable suffering and death to those who feel, does not depend on any health or climate data. Data serve to correct facts, and should be presented with the strength they really have (see Restraint with indirect arguments).
  • Don't exaggerate. Inflated numbers and cherry-picked studies are easy to take apart and harm the whole movement. The guide collects mistakes of this kind in Stop saying this.
  • Admit uncertainty. On insect sentience, the balance of welfare in nature or the impact of each strategy, a great deal is unknown. Saying so clearly builds trust rather than eroding it.
  • Anticipate objections. The most common objections are predictable, and answering them before they come up keeps them from becoming an excuse to end the conversation. That is what this guide's catalog of arguments is for.
  • Know when to stop, and remember who is reading. When someone shows no openness to dialogue, it is often better to end the conversation and put the energy into those who are open. In public spaces, such as social media, it can still be worth leaving a calm, well-reasoned reply for the other people who read the thread without taking part: point out the reasoning error, without attacks, and cite the source. There is evidence for this. The researchers Leticia Bode and Emily Vraga, who study what they call observed correction, found in experiments that seeing someone corrected on social media reduces false beliefs among those who only watch, especially when the correction cites a credible source; without a source, a correction by an ordinary user had little effect. The studies deal mostly with health and science misinformation, so extending them to ethical debates is a reasonable inference, not a measured result. That is where this guide is most useful: the catalog of arguments and the fallacies page help name the error and find the source.
  • Tell true stories. The story of a rescued animal, of a person who changed their habits or of a documented investigation brings the subject closer to the listener. It should be real, verifiable and told with respect, without sensationalism or images used out of context.
  • Show the sources. Saying where each figure comes from lets people check for themselves, and teaches them to do the same with any information.

Be careful with the technological promise. In a Sentience Institute experiment (Harris, 2020), information about cultured meat and high-tech substitutes reduced opposition to animal farming, while information about simple plant-based foods had no such effect. The study measured opinions about animal farming, not changes in habits: substitutes that resemble meat can make switching easier in daily life, through taste, comfort and convenience. What it suggests is a different caution: not presenting the technology as a solution that makes changing now unnecessary or makes criticism of animal farming pointless (K11).

How to disagree

Disagreeing about means is healthy, as long as the end stays at the center: the animals. When fighting between currents takes the place of outward work, they are the ones who lose (correcting without losing focus). It is worth treating other currents the way this guide treats opposing arguments: present the strongest version, acknowledge what it gets right and decide by the evidence.
