The currents of the vegan movement

All vegans want the end of animal exploitation. The currents disagree about why, with whom and how to get there. A map of the ends, the means and the main debates.

This is a map, not an official classification. Names vary from country to country, the currents overlap and the same person can belong to several of them.

The end and the means

What defines veganism is the end: the abolition of animal exploitation. Leslie Cross, one of the early officers of The Vegan Society, wrote in 1951: "One may become a vegetarian for a variety of reasons - humanitarian, health, or mere preference for such a diet; the principle is a matter of personal feeling, and varies accordingly. Veganism, however, is a principle - that man has no right to exploit the creatures for his own ends - and no variation occurs."

The currents disagree about the means: which strategies work best, which other struggles the animal cause is tied to and which reasons it rests on. This is common in social movements. Feminism has liberal, radical, Marxist, Black and intersectional currents. The anti-racist movement has been divided between integration and autonomy, between reforming laws and changing structures. In none of these cases did disagreement about means put anyone outside the movement. That is why this guide criticizes lines such as V54, "Anyone who supports step-by-step change isn't really vegan" and V55, "Anyone who won't accept gradual change doesn't understand how the world works".

Each current answers a different question

The labels below are not alternatives on the same list. They answer four different questions, which is why one person can hold several at once.

QuestionWhat is in disputeCurrents
FoundationWhy do animals matter?Antispeciesist-sentientist, animal rights, ethics of care, religious traditions
Political analysisWhich other oppressions and systems is animal exploitation tied to?Intersectional, veganarchism, popular veganism, liberal veganism
StrategyWhich actions end exploitation fastest?Abolitionist, pragmatic, technological

The main currents

CurrentWhat it holdsNames and notes
Abolitionist veganismVeganism is the moral baseline of the movement. The task is to educate people toward veganism and the end of the property status of animals. It rejects campaigns for welfare reforms, on the view that they make exploitation more acceptable.Gary Francione and the "abolitionist approach". It is a position about strategy: every vegan is an abolitionist about the end.
Pragmatic veganismIt accepts the same end and argues for reaching it in stages: legal reforms, corporate campaigns, reducing consumption and changes that make it easier for more people to join.Tobias Leenaert, Henry Spira and organizations such as The Humane League and Mercy For Animals. Also called liberal-pragmatic or regulationist. Critics call it "welfarist" or "new welfarist", pejoratively. Not to be confused with welfarism proper, which accepts using animals as long as there is less suffering, and is not veganism.
Intersectional veganismThe oppression of animals is tied to other oppressions, such as sexism, racism, ableism and class exploitation, and should be fought together with them.It brings together feminist veganism (Carol J. Adams), Black veganism (Aph Ko, Syl Ko, A. Breeze Harper), anti-ableist veganism (Sunaura Taylor) and, in Brazil, "popular veganism", tied to the left and to the idea of making veganism accessible to working-class people.
Veganarchism and total liberationVeganism and anarchism complete each other: animal exploitation is one of the forms of domination a society without hierarchies has to abolish.Brian A. Dominick, who coined the term in 1995, and Steven Best. See the page on anarchism.
Liberal veganismThe end of animal exploitation is compatible with liberal democracy and a market economy, and can advance through rights, laws, consumer choices and innovation.Alasdair Cochrane, Martha Nussbaum, Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka. See the page on liberalism.
Antispeciesist-sentientist veganismThe foundation is sentience, and veganism is one of the consequences of antispeciesism. So the concern extends to all sentient beings: wild animals, invertebrates and possible future minds.Oscar Horta, Luciano Carlos Cunha, the organization Animal Ethics and the Sentientism movement. See antispeciesism and sentientism.
Technological veganismTechnology is the fastest route to ending large-scale exploitation: plant-based meat, cultivated meat and precision fermentation give people the same comfort without the animals and open the way to then abolishing exploitation.This is not an established label: we use the name for a position that exists. Jacy Reese Anthis, in The End of Animal Farming, and the Good Food Institute are examples. See the debate on the definition of veganism.
Rationalist or evidence-based veganismFoundations and strategies should be chosen by reason and by the best available evidence on what most reduces suffering and exploitation, with a willingness to revise positions.It is the approach of this guide and of the book Veganismo Baseado em Evidências (Evidence-Based Veganism, in Portuguese). It is close to the work of organizations such as Faunalytics, Sentience Institute, Animal Charity Evaluators and Rethink Priorities, and is adopted by many sentientists.

There are also currents defined by their foundation: rights-based veganism, from Tom Regan; veganism tied to the ethics of care and ecofeminism; and religiously inspired veganism, such as the kind that starts from ahimsa, the nonviolence of Jainism, Hinduism and Buddhism.

Currents and meta-currents

Some of these positions really do compete for the same space. Abolitionists and pragmatists give opposite answers to the same question: do reform campaigns help or hinder? Others do not compete with each other. A person can be sentientist in foundation, intersectional in political analysis and pragmatic in strategy.

Evidence-based veganism works as a meta-current, or a meta-strategy: it does not pick a side in advance, but a way of deciding. Much of the dispute between abolitionists and pragmatists, for example, depends on facts: what happens to consumption, to laws and to public opinion after each kind of campaign. Those are questions research can help answer. Technological veganism is similar: it is a bet about what will work, and it can be combined with different foundations and political analyses.

How to disagree

Disagreeing about means is healthy, as long as the end stays at the center: the animals. When fighting between currents takes the place of outward work, they are the ones who lose (correcting without losing focus). It is worth treating other currents the way this guide treats opposing arguments: present the strongest version, acknowledge what it gets right and decide by the evidence.
