The common minimum

Finding the point of convergence: someone who rejects the whole conclusion can almost always accept part of it. Showing that part is not giving up what you argue for, it is not demanding that people change their worldview before taking the first step.

Every position has a point of convergence: the part of it someone can accept without having to agree with the rest. Finding that point is part of the work of arguing, not a concession.

There are two ways to defend a position. One is to present it whole, with everything you believe to be right. The other is to show how much of it someone can accept without having to swap ethical theory, religion or political outlook. Both are needed, and confusing them is costly: the first, used alone, drives away anyone who disagrees on a single point; the second, used alone, never reaches what matters.

The compatibility range

Call the compatibility range the number of additional ideas someone has to accept in order to accept the main idea. A narrow range demands many: to agree, a person would have to adopt a specific ethical theory, a political outlook, a whole set of conclusions. A wide range demands few, and so the idea can be accepted by people who disagree about almost everything else.

The commonest strategic mistake is presenting as a package what is not one. If thesis A doesn't depend on thesis B, defending B in order to convince someone of A only gives them one more reason to refuse A. And if they already rejected B, the result is predictable: the conversation is lost over something that wasn't even needed. Correctly identifying how wide the range is, then, is a strategic decision, not a concession.

There is a side effect that matters just as much: making clear that one can accept the goal while disagreeing on other questions reduces polarisation. People don't have to choose between switching sides and rejecting everything.

Goals that don't require changing theory

Some goals can be accepted without adopting any particular ethical or political theory:

  • consideration for all sentient beings;
  • rejecting speciesism;
  • ending animal exploitation;
  • reducing wild animal suffering;
  • preventing risks of large-scale suffering;
  • taking the long term seriously.

This guide's political objections are that idea applied: they show, theory by theory, that excluding animals contradicts each one's own principle. Nobody is asked to change theory; they are asked to apply their own to the end.

The total approach and the sufficient approach

The distinction is Luciano Carlos Cunha's. The total approach defends in full what you believe to be the best decision. The sufficient approach shows that someone who disagrees can still accept part of the position, or some of its implications, and clarifies that certain arguments used to reject it don't in fact lead to rejecting all of it.

Both, not one

They are not mutually exclusive. You can argue for the ideal and, at the same time, make clear that partial acceptance is worth something too. Each used alone has its own cost:

  • Only the total one. You lose the people who would have agreed with much of it, and every single disagreement becomes a reason to discard the whole.
  • Only the sufficient one. The controversial subjects are never discussed, and the public never grows used to them. Starting that conversation early is what, in the long run, makes the ideas less strange.

The balance depends on two empirical questions, not on taste: how many people would accept the whole goal compared with how many would accept part of it, and how much each full adherence contributes compared with a partial one. A world with many antispeciesist people is very different from a world where animals are merely considered a little: the total approach remains necessary.

Where the limit is

Converging on the common minimum doesn't mean accepting any route to it. An argument can raise immediate agreement while teaching the wrong principle: that is the case with health and environmental arguments, which can lead someone to swap beef for chicken and multiply the number of victims (Restraint with indirect arguments). The difference is this: the sufficient approach asks less of the person about the same reason; the indirect argument swaps the reason.

How this shows up in the guide

  • Every chapter ends with a section on what the argument gets right. It isn't courtesy: it is recognising the part that doesn't need to be disputed.
  • The political objections apply each theory's own principle to it, instead of asking for it to be abandoned.
  • The Stop saying this page corrects bad arguments on our own side, which narrow the range for no reason.
  • The questions back in each chapter ask for a step, not a conversion.
