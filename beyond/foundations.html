What supports animal ethics, and what weakens it

Critical reason, relevance, impartiality and sentience support veganism and animal ethics. Partiality, relativism, tribalism, ableism, making abstractions sacred and weak will weaken them.

Veganism and animal ethics don't depend on liking animals or on any particular ethical theory. They depend on a few principles almost everyone already accepts in other contexts. When those principles are taken seriously, the conclusion comes with them. When they're abandoned, it disappears, and other just causes tend to go with it.

Why this matters

The principles in the first table are the same ones that support human rights, the fight against racism, sexism and ableism, and care for future generations. The attitudes in the second table are the same ones that, throughout history, have served to exclude humans from the moral circle. That's why defending animals doesn't compete with other just causes: it rests on the same foundations and faces the same adversaries.

Knowing isn't enough, but it helps

Some people know animal ethics well and still eat animals. That doesn't show moral beliefs motivate no one. It shows they don't decide behavior on their own: a person may think the wrong isn't serious, may have stronger competing motivations, such as habit, pleasure and social pressure, or may simply choose not to act. As Luciano Carlos Cunha argues, emotional persuasion faces the same limits, and rational persuasion has the advantage of explaining why a practice should be rejected. That's why, besides arguments, it helps to create conditions that make action easier: environments, habits and groups that make it easier to do what one already knows is right.

It's also why choosing arguments well is worthwhile. An argument that persuades someone through selfishness, tribalism or anthropocentrism can win a conversation and, at the same time, strengthen exactly what weakens the cause. That's the subject of the page Restraint with indirect arguments.
