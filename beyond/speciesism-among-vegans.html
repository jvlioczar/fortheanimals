Speciesism among vegans

Giving up eating animals does not eliminate speciesism. It survives in priorities, in arguments and in ways of speaking, including among vegans and the organizations that represent them.

Rejecting exploitation for food does not guarantee that the sentience criterion is being applied in full. Speciesism, the unjustified disadvantageous consideration or treatment of those who are not classified as belonging to a certain species, also appears among vegans, in choices of focus, in arguments and in communication habits. The examples below are common. Pointing them out is not accusing anyone of bad faith: it is applying to the movement itself the test it applies to others. The list of hierarchies that tend to survive is in Antispeciesism and sentientism.

The most numerous animals are left off the agenda

Fish, crustaceans such as shrimp and lobsters, insects and other sentient invertebrates are, by number, the largest victims of exploitation. According to the estimates gathered by Oscar Horta and Mat Rozas (2024), more than 80 billion land vertebrates, up to about 2 trillion aquatic vertebrates and up to tens of trillions of sentient invertebrates are killed every year. Even so, many campaigns and images from vegan organizations deal almost only with mammals and birds. If the criterion is sentience and the number of victims, the priority should run the other way. The evidence of pain in fish is solid, and the evidence of sentience in decapods led the United Kingdom to recognize them by law (A2); for insects it is weaker, but precaution applies (A3, N20). There are organizations devoted to these animals, but they are still the exception.

Cute, large or pedigree animals

Feeling more affection for some animals, such as dogs, cats or young animals, is human, and a personal preference is not the problem. The problem is when it becomes a moral decision: giving more weight to an animal's suffering because it is cute, large, charismatic or pedigree, and less to another that feels just the same. This shows up in campaigns that rally for pandas, dolphins and dogs and stay silent about fish, chickens and insects, and in valuing pedigree animals above mixed-breed ones. Appearance, size and breed don't change the capacity to feel; using them to decide who deserves care repeats the logic of speciesism (If you love some animals, why do you eat others?). See also the list of hierarchies in Antispeciesism and sentientism.

Biodiversity without individuals

Large vegan organizations sometimes share conservation content, such as "saving species", reintroducing predators or rewilding areas, without the perspective of welfare biology, the field that studies the welfare of wild animals as individuals. Conservation counts species and ecosystems, not those who live in them. More biodiversity can mean more animals with lives that go badly, and one more species is not, in itself, good for anyone (A10, N3, N15). Sharing that content uncritically teaches the very criterion antispeciesism rejects.

Indirect arguments that forget the animals

Defending veganism only for health, climate or world hunger, without mentioning the animals, rests the cause on a borrowed reason: when the reason falls, the motivation falls with it, and the animal disappears from the conversation. Talking about health and the environment is not wrong; the problem is when the animals stop appearing. And this is common: in many debates between vegans and non-vegans, the suffering and well-being of animals are hardly discussed, and the conversation stays almost entirely on the environment, health and other reasons. See Direct arguments and the "Borrowed reason" group in Stop saying this.

Demanding of vegans what is demanded of no one else

Some people demand that vegans be anticapitalist, hold a position on every human cause or debate every topic before talking about animals, and make no such demand of non-vegans, even when the requirement has nothing to do with veganism. It is a double standard: it treats the defense of animals as something that needs a special license. Vegans, like everyone, have a duty not to discriminate against people, and so they need to be antiracist and fight other forms of discrimination, so as not to contradict themselves (Veganism is only about animals, nothing else matters). What is questioned is the demand made only of them, as in Being vegan requires being anti-capitalist, Veganism and environmentalism without class struggle is just gardening and Veganism that doesn't discuss racism is just rich kids' cuisine, and in the arguments L13 and L11. See also Intersectionality.

"If only subsistence fishing and hunting existed, there would be no need for a vegan movement"

The claim is partly true by the criterion of necessity: someone who hunts or fishes so as not to go hungry has no alternative, and veganism speaks of avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable" (G1, D7). But the movement would still exist, with a much smaller reach, as a philosophical question about the value of animal life: the animal killed still loses their life, and there would still be reason to seek alternatives that make hunting unnecessary. The claim can also suggest that the problem is only the industrial scale, not the harm to each animal, and it forgets that wild animal suffering would exist all the same. Moving beyond subsistence that depends on killing animals would, then, be a goal, since there would still be victims. If humans were the victims of a subsistence way of life, no one would accept keeping it, and that difference in reaction shows speciesism.

"I would accept a xenotransplant"

Some vegans say they would use an organ from a pig bred to be killed. Faced with the risk of dying, the reaction is understandable, and veganism's "possible and practicable" may cover a real emergency with no alternative. But treating the technique as a neutral option helps normalize a new form of exploitation at scale, and the test is the usual one: no one would accept breeding humans to take their organs. The consistent position is to defend the alternatives, such as increasing human organ donation and grown organs, and not to endorse breeding animals for this (I6).

Refusing the debate about wild animals

Refusing to discuss and take seriously the suffering of animals in nature, the largest source of suffering there is, keeps a hierarchy based on the origin of the harm: what matters becomes who caused it, not who suffers it. The reason not to exploit animals is that they feel, and an animal with hunger, disease or parasites feels just the same (Helping wild animals isn't veganism; it's a different cause, N14). See Wild animal suffering.

How to point it out without accusing

Prioritizing is unavoidable, and choosing a focus for strategic reasons is legitimate. What separates a priority from speciesism is the reason given: if the choice rests on the number of victims, tractability or evidence, it can be discussed; if it rests on species or emotional closeness, it repeats the mistake veganism criticizes. When pointing out these cases, it is worth talking about ideas and practices, not people, and acknowledging what each organization already does.
