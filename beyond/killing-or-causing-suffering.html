Killing vs causing suffering: the debate on the value of life

Does a painless death harm the one who dies? Philosophy answers in several ways, and the answer decides whether it is enough to end animals' suffering or whether we must stop killing them.

This is an open debate. This page presents the main positions on the harm of death and the value of life, who defends them and who contests them. At the end, it states this guide's position and why.

Why the question matters

Almost everyone accepts that making an animal suffer without need is wrong. The disagreement starts with death. If suffering were the only harm, painless farming followed by instant slaughter would stop being a problem, and the defense of animals would shrink to improving farming conditions (B18). If death also harms the one who dies, killing remains a harm even when no one suffers (B2). This answer is what separates welfarism from the positions that call for an end to use (see the currents of the vegan movement).

What makes death bad for the one who dies

ViewWhat it saysWhat it implies for animals
Experiential (Epicurus)Only what is felt can be bad, and death, when it comes, is no longer feltKilling without pain would harm no animal, but it would harm no human either, which is why almost no one accepts it in their own case
Deprivation (Thomas Nagel, 1970)Death is bad because of what it takes away: the good experiences life would still have broughtIt applies to any being with a future of good experiences, human or not
Preferences (Michael Tooley, Peter Singer)Killing is wrong above all because it frustrates desires, starting with the desire to go on livingIt weighs more for those with desires about the future, which opens room for hierarchies between species and between individuals
Time-relative interests (Jeff McMahan, 2002)The harm is the lost future, discounted by how strongly the being is psychologically connected to that futureAn animal's death would be a real harm, but smaller than that of an adult human
Inherent value (Tom Regan, 1983)Every subject-of-a-life has a value of its own, equal for all, that is neither added up nor tradedKilling a subject-of-a-life violates its right, whatever the balance of pleasure

The first two rows are the core of B2: anyone who thinks killing a sleeping person harms them has already accepted the deprivation view, and there is no reason to swap it for the experiential view when the victim is an animal. The other three try to answer a harder question: if death is a harm, is it the same size for everyone?

Singer: self-consciousness and replaceability

In Practical Ethics (2nd ed., 1993), Peter Singer distinguishes self-conscious beings, who see themselves as someone existing over time and have desires about their own future, from merely conscious beings, who feel pleasure and pain without that perspective. He calls the former persons, in John Locke's sense, and for them death frustrates plans and desires and is therefore, in general, more serious. He draws partly on Michael Tooley, who argued in 1972 that only a being capable of desiring to go on existing as a subject has a right to life.

For merely conscious beings, Singer accepts a conclusion he himself recognizes as the most criticized point of the book: the loss caused by death could be made up for by the birth of another being with an equally good life. This is the replaceability argument (B7). The conclusion depends on how welfare is counted:

  • Total view: counts the pleasure of everyone, including beings who may come to exist. Creating a happy being adds something to the world, so the death of one can be made up for by the birth of another.
  • Prior existence view: counts only beings who already exist or will exist anyway. There is no compensation: killing an animal that would still have months or years of good life is a wrong.

Singer sees costs in both and calls the models with which he tries to explain why persons are not replaceable, such as life seen as a journey already under way, in which plans, hope and effort have been invested, metaphors rather than a solution.

The limits Singer himself sets

  • The argument does not apply to factory farming, where the animals' lives are not good, nor to hunting, where the animal killed is usually not replaced by another.
  • Since it is hard to know which animals are self-conscious, he argues for giving them the benefit of the doubt, like the hunter who does not shoot when unsure whether what is moving in the bushes is game or another hunter. By this standard, he says, much of the killing of animals must be condemned.
  • In practice, he recommends not killing animals for food, also for indirect reasons and because a simple rule protects better than case-by-case calculations.

Where this guide does not follow

Singer applies the same logic to human newborns, including disabled ones, and goes as far as accepting infanticide in some cases. This guide rejects that conclusion. Disabled scholars and activists rightly criticize the way philosophy has used disabled people as examples: Eva Feder Kittay, mother of a daughter with severe cognitive disability, disputes that cognitive capacities decide who has a full right to life; Sunaura Taylor, in Beasts of Burden (2017), shows how ableism and speciesism use the same yardstick; and the lawyer Harriet McBryde Johnson recounted in 2003 her public debate with Singer. The consistent antispeciesist answer is not to lower the protection of humans to the level at which we now treat animals, but to raise that of animals (A26, A20).

The criticisms of replaceability and of a hierarchy of lives

  • Tom Regan, in The Case for Animal Rights (1983), rejects the idea that an individual is a receptacle of pleasure, interchangeable with another holding the same amount. Every subject-of-a-life, with beliefs, desires, memory, a sense of the future, an emotional life and a welfare that matters to it, has equal inherent value and cannot be treated merely as a means. Regan does not deny that death can be a greater harm for some than for others: in his hypothetical lifeboat case, he accepts sacrificing a dog rather than a human. But that applies to extreme emergencies, and does not license routine breeding and slaughter.
  • Gary Francione holds that every sentient being has an interest in going on living, because sentience is precisely the means that allows a being to pursue its own survival. Self-consciousness would not be needed to be harmed by death, and treating animals as replaceable resources is an effect of their status as property.
  • Elizabeth Harman (2011) points to an inconsistency: whoever thinks an animal's pain matters because of its interests cannot explain why death, which frustrates all those interests at once, would matter little.
  • Luciano Carlos Cunha, in volume III of A Journey Through Animal Ethics, answers point by point. Replaceability would allow parents to replace their children, and it confuses being like someone with being the same individual: not creating a new being harms no one, while killing someone who already exists does. Against the time-relative interest account, he notes that a simpler mind changes less over time and may be, proportionally, more connected to its own future, and that someone who has lived little and would have a good future loses a great deal, whatever that connection. And he argues that a two-tier ethics, with special protection for persons and a mere sum of interests for everyone else, fails the test of impartiality: the strength of the reasons against killing should depend on the size of the harm.

The best version of the other side deserves to be stated clearly. For McMahan, psychological connection to the future makes a real difference: an adult human loses more by dying than an animal with shorter memory and plans. But even this version concedes the essential point. The animal's death is still a harm, and a harm has to be weighed against what is gained by causing it.

This guide's position

Killing is a harm to any being that has a future of positive experiences ahead of it. That is the core of the deprivation view, and it does not depend on the being knowing it will die (B2), making plans or being a person in Locke's sense. Degrees of connection to the future may change the size of the loss, and the guide does not hide that debate, but a smaller harm is still a harm.

The view also has limits, and they matter. It does not condemn euthanasia carried out in the interest of the one who dies, when what lies ahead is mostly pain without relief. And it measures what each one loses, not how much each one is worth: it cannot be used to grade the right to life of humans or animals by cognitive capacity.

What changes in practice, on the plate

  • "Humane" slaughter does not solve it. Even without any pain, it takes from animals almost all the life they would have had: broiler chickens are slaughtered at about six weeks and pigs at about six months, out of possible lives of several years.
  • Replaceability does not rescue "happy meat". On the deprivation view and on the prior existence view, the birth of another animal does not undo the loss of the one who died (B7). And not even for Singer does the argument justify the farming that actually exists, where lives are not good.
  • What sits on the other side of the scale is small. In most cases, the gain is taste and habit (J6), and there are accessible alternatives.
  • When in doubt, do not kill. The hunter's rule, which Singer himself uses, also applies to doubt about who feels and who has an interest in going on living.
