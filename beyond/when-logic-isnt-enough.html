When logic isn't enough

Facts and arguments show what is right, but they rarely suffice to make someone change. Apathy, habit and indifference call for other reasons: someone to care about, people to change with and an easy path to follow.

Many animal advocates act as if presenting the right facts and the right reasoning were enough: show the suffering, prove the contradiction, and the person changes. Sometimes they do. Most of the time, they don't. Not because the argument is bad, but because knowing what is right and doing what is right are different things, and what drives the second is rarely the first alone.

The idea that logic isn't enough is true, but only half true. Research shows that information on its own changes behaviour little. It also shows that it isn't useless: it is the foundation without which no change holds, and it works far better alongside other things.

What the research shows

The belief that informing is enough has a name, and it has been abandoned. In science communication, the idea that people disagree because they lack information is called the "deficit model". Decades of research showed it doesn't describe how people form opinions, and a 2016 study set out precisely to explain why it persists anyway: it is intuitive to those already convinced, and simpler than dealing with everything that weighs on someone's decision.

There is a wide gap between intention and act. Psychology calls it the intention–behaviour gap: many people who intend to change don't, or change only briefly, because of habit, forgetting, lack of opportunity or lack of support. It is what philosophy calls weakness of will, and it appears in argument E15, "I know it's wrong, but I won't change".

Moral judgements tend to come before the reasons. Jonathan Haidt proposed that, most of the time, intuition decides and reasoning comes afterwards, to justify. The proposal is influential and also contested: Joshua May, among others, argues that reasoning plays a larger role than Haidt allows. But even the critics agree that intuitions weigh heavily, and that an argument running against someone's intuition meets resistance that has nothing to do with its quality.

With meat, the resistance is active. People who eat meat and care about animals live with a conflict, the meat paradox, and reduce the discomfort in several ways: attributing less mind to the animals they eat, falling back on the justifications that eating meat is natural, normal, necessary and nice, or simply avoiding knowing. Researchers call the last one strategic ignorance: many people who seem indifferent are in fact deliberately looking away.

Large numbers don't move us. Paul Slovic showed that compassion shrinks as the number of victims grows, which he called psychic numbing. Saying that billions of animals die every year can produce less reaction than the story of a single one.

But facts aren't useless

It would be a mistake to conclude that arguing is pointless. A 2021 meta-analysis of around a hundred studies found that interventions showing or describing what happens to farmed animals reduce meat consumption, purchasing or intention to eat meat, with meaningful effects at least in the short term. The authors note the limitations: studies that are generally short, outcomes almost always self-reported, and methodological quality from moderate to low. Still, the signal is consistent: showing what happens to animals works better than not showing it.

And the argument does a job nothing else does. Emotion gives the push, but doesn't say where to go; without reasons, the same emotion that leads someone to veganism could lead them anywhere. Logic is what lets someone know the change is right, defend it to others and keep it once the impulse fades. It isn't enough, but it is the part that can't be missing. Slovic himself reaches this conclusion: since compassion fails in the face of large numbers, the response can't depend on it alone, and has to come from moral argument and institutions too.

Apathy isn't malice

Indifference is the normal state of most people towards most of the world's problems, not a character flaw of meat eaters. People who care about animals are indifferent to many other causes too. Treating apathy as cruelty tends to produce the opposite effect: the person defends themselves, and the defence becomes identity. Understanding why someone doesn't change is the first step towards helping them change.

What this changes in practice

  • Keep arguing, and argue well. The direct arguments, about sentience and the harm to animals, remain the foundation. That logic isn't enough is no reason to swap it for indirect arguments, which move people for other reasons and fall away when those reasons change.
  • Join reason to the one affected. An animal with a name and a story, alongside the argument, moves people more than the argument alone, and more than the statistic.
  • Show the path, not just the destination. Recipes, places, products and people who have already made the change make the decision possible.
  • Ask for a step, not a conversion. Those who take the first step tend to take the second; those who feel they must change everything at once often don't start.
  • Be patient with indifference, and firm about what is true. Respecting someone's pace doesn't require agreeing that exploitation is acceptable.

Where the idea goes wrong, if taken too far

"Logic isn't enough" can become an excuse to use anything that works: exaggerating, frightening, manipulating. That has a cost. False claims, once found out, take with them the credibility of whoever used them and of the whole cause (see Stop saying this). And moving someone without giving reasons treats them as someone to be steered, not convinced. Emotion and social norms are allies of reason, not substitutes for it.

The underlying philosophical debate is old. David Hume wrote that reason is the slave of the passions, meaning that it alone moves no one; other philosophers hold that recognising a reason can itself motivate. Recent research hasn't settled the question, but it points in a practical direction: people change when reason, emotion, surroundings and other people all point the same way.
