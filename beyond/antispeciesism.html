Antispeciesism and sentientism

Veganism is one consequence of a broader criterion: what matters morally is the capacity to be harmed or benefited.

Two names, one idea

Sentientism holds that what matters morally is the capacity to be harmed or benefited, present in any sentient being, human or not. Antispeciesism is the consequence: giving similar weight to similar harms, whatever the species.

That is why the discussion does not end at the plate. If we accept the criterion, it also applies to what we do by omission, to animals no one exploits and to beings that may come to exist.

Sentientism: what it is and what it is not

Sentientism (also called sentiocentrism) is the position that what matters morally is the capacity to have positive and negative experiences. A being that can be harmed or benefited from the point of view of the one living the situation has interests of its own, and those interests count in themselves, not because of what others do with the being. Jeremy Bentham's question, "can they suffer?", is usually cited as the origin of the idea (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). The term was already in use in 1977, in a text by John Rodman (Wikipedia).

Sentience is not only pain. It covers anything that can be lived as good or bad: pain, fear, frustration, boredom, comfort, pleasure, play, attachment. So the criterion includes positive well-being, not only the absence of suffering. The scientific declarations cited below speak of consciousness, and here are read as evidence about sentience.

Criterion, consequence and practice

  • Sentientism is the criterion: who is inside the moral circle.
  • Antispeciesism is a consequence: if what counts is sentience, species cannot be the factor that decides whether an interest weighs or not.
  • Veganism is a practice that follows for the use of animals. Sentientism goes further: it also applies to animals no one exploits, to invertebrates and, if it comes to that, to digital minds.

The criterion is defended, with important differences among them, by Peter Singer (for whom the capacity to suffer is a prerequisite for having interests), Oscar Horta and Luciano Carlos Cunha (who take it to wild animal suffering, and who work in Brazil through the organization Animal Ethics), and the Sentientism movement, developed by Jamie Woodhouse, which describes it as a worldview built on evidence, reason and compassion for all sentient beings (sentientism.info). Jeff Sebo argues for widening the moral circle to beings with a non-negligible chance of well-being, including invertebrates and AI, but he combines sentience with other possible bases, such as agency. Jonathan Birch works on the practical problem of acting without knowing who feels (see below). Not every defender accepts every conclusion of the others.

The variants of sentientism

Accepting that sentience matters leaves two questions open. The criteria table on the site Ética Para Iniciantes (Ethics for Beginners, in Portuguese) calls the answers partial versions of sentientism, as opposed to the full version.

Gradualist or non-gradualist

The question: does an experience of the same intensity count equally in any being?

  • The non-gradualist says yes: being sentient is enough, and an equal pain weighs the same, whether in a human or a pig.
  • The gradualist says the weight varies with capacities such as intelligence and self-awareness: more complex minds would have interests of greater weight. There is also a reading in which the weight varies with the degree or richness of sentience itself.

One distinction keeps the positions from being confused. What is at stake changes with capacities: a being with plans and an anticipated future loses more by dying than one without, and almost no one disputes that. The weight of an equal harm is the gradualism question proper.

Who holds what. Singer defends the principle of equal consideration of interests, which is not equal treatment nor equal weight for everything (see N14). Shelly Kagan, in How to Count Animals, More or Less (2019), defends a hierarchy: persons count more than animals and some animals count more than others. David DeGrazia criticized Kagan's claim that humans lacking the capacities of persons get extra status.

Main objections. (1) The argument from marginal cases: if intelligence sets the weight, infants and people with severe cognitive disability would weigh less, and many adult animals would weigh more than they do. Gradualist replies appeal to potential, to relationships or to Kagan's "modal personhood", and each is contested. (2) The intensity of pain may not track intelligence: a simple mind, with no consolation or distraction, may suffer a great deal.

Temporalist or non-temporalist

The question: does suffering or well-being count for less because it happens later?

  • The temporalist gives less weight to the future, because of uncertainty, distance, or the view that future beings are less real, applying something like a discount rate.
  • The non-temporalist (atemporalist) says the date does not change the value: like distance in space, it changes what we know, not what it is worth.

Derek Parfit distinguished discounting for uncertainty, which is legitimate (we weigh by probability), from pure discounting, merely for being later, which he rejected; Henry Sidgwick had already said that time does not change value. Longtermism (Toby Ord, William MacAskill) takes the distant future seriously, with criticisms about fanaticism. Objection: today's decisions change who will exist (Parfit's non-identity problem), which complicates talk of "harming" future beings.

Possibilities and limits

Who is sentient?

We do not observe another being's experience, only signs in behavior, brain and evolution. Two declarations by researchers help, without closing the question. The Cambridge Declaration (2012) states that humans are not unique in having the neurological substrates that generate consciousness, and mentions mammals, birds and octopuses. The New York Declaration (2024) says there is strong scientific support for mammals and birds and at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates and many invertebrates (cephalopods, decapod crustaceans and insects), and that ignoring that possibility is irresponsible. Both are statements by experts, not proof.

Under uncertainty, Birch proposes treating as "sentience candidates" the systems with a credible, non-negligible possibility of feeling, and acting proportionately to the evidence, without demanding certainty (The Edge of Sentience, 2024). This is the benefit-of-the-doubt argument, valid in any variant.

Moral weight

Giving consideration to insects is not giving an ant the same weight as a person. Provisional estimates place insects well below mammals, but the number of individuals is huge. Chapter N14 covers this.

Wild animals

If sentience is the criterion, suffering that no one caused also counts. Horta and Kyle Johannsen reach this by different routes, and the central point is to research before intervening. See wild animal suffering.

Digital minds

The criterion does not depend on the material, so an artificial system that felt would count. Today none is recognized as sentient, and there is no consensus on how we would know. See sentience in AI and robots.

What is not speciesism

Recognizing real differences is not discrimination. Saying an animal does not need to vote is an observation. Saying it can suffer without that mattering, because it is not very intelligent, is speciesism. The line between recognizing a difference and using it to discard interests is where the debate should happen.

Hierarchies that survive among vegans

People who have already rejected exploitation for food can keep speciesism in other forms:

  • Humans above animals in any conflict of interests, accepting experimentation or ritual sacrifice.
  • Pets above farmed animals.
  • Mammals and birds above fish, who are numerically the largest victims of exploitation.
  • Vertebrates above invertebrates, dismissing insects and decapods without examination.
  • Exploited animals above wild ones, which reveals an omission bias: who caused the harm matters more than who suffers it.
  • Abstract concepts above individuals, such as biodiversity or ecosystem balance.
  • Those who exist now above those yet to come, a time discount that disregards future suffering.

The test is the veil of ignorance: would you accept the hierarchy without knowing in which position you would be born? There are billions of humans and quintillions of animals, counting invertebrates. No one who took that ratio seriously would bet on a hierarchy with humans systematically on top.

Humanism taken all the way

This is not anti-humanism. Humanism brought universality and impartiality, which helped overcome hierarchies between human groups, but those principles stopped at the species line without justification. Posthumanism keeps the commitment to evidence, equality and individual freedom, and corrects that failure.
