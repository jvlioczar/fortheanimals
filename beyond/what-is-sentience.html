What is sentience?

Sentience is the capacity to have good or bad experiences. It is not the same as nociception, because reacting to harm is not feeling pain, and it requires a minimal consciousness, someone who feels, but not self-awareness. Understanding the difference avoids two opposite mistakes.

Sentience is the capacity to have good or bad experiences. It is what makes a being someone rather than something: a fish or a bee, not a stone or a plant. And that is why it matters for ethics: only those who feel can be harmed or benefited. But in the debate about animals the word is often confused with two others, nociception and consciousness. The confusion runs both ways. Those who want to deny a fish's pain say it "only has nociception". Those who want to defend it sometimes treat any reaction to a stimulus as proof of suffering. Both shortcuts are wrong, and the distinction between the terms shows why.

Three words

  • Nociception is the detection of stimuli that damage or threaten to damage the body, such as heat, pressure or corrosive substances, by dedicated receptors and nerve pathways, the nociceptors. It can trigger reflexes without any experience at all.
  • Sentience is the capacity to have experiences that are good or bad for the one living them, such as pain, pleasure, fear, comfort or frustration. The philosophers Heather Browning and Jonathan Birch define it as the capacity to have valenced experiences, that is, experiences felt as good or bad (Animal sentience, 2022).
  • Consciousness, in the sense that matters here, phenomenal consciousness, is there being something it is like to be that being, from its own point of view, in the philosopher Thomas Nagel's phrase (1974). It includes experiences without valence, such as seeing a color or hearing a sound. Other senses of the word, such as being awake, having self-awareness or being able to report one's thoughts, are different things, and none of them is required to feel pain.

Nociception is not sentience

Nociception can happen without anyone feeling anything. When you touch a hot pan, you pull your hand away before you feel the pain, because the reflex runs through the spinal cord. Patients under general anesthesia have nociceptive activity during surgery and feel no pain. That is why the International Association for the Study of Pain, in its definition revised in 2020, states that pain and nociception are different phenomena, and that pain cannot be inferred solely from activity in sensory neurons (Raja et al., 2020).

As far as we know, nociception also does not need a centralized nervous system. The withdrawal reflex runs through the spinal cord, without the brain; the hydra, a relative of jellyfish, has a nerve net with no center and contracts in response to a harmful stimulus; and the worm C. elegans, with 302 neurons, moves away from heat through short circuits. What matters for the other question, whether the stimulus is felt, is integration: the information has to be brought together and weighed with other information, and that is what the sentience criteria look for. That is why centralization usually counts as evidence of sentience, not as a condition of nociception. For the organization Animal Ethics, a centralizing organ, such as a brain or ganglia, is the main physiological criterion: animals without one, such as jellyfish and sea cucumbers, would lack the structures needed for consciousness, and there is still no consensus on the minimum degree of centralization (Quais seres são sencientes?, in Portuguese). The octopus is the borderline case for this criterion, not for sentience: it has a central brain, but about two thirds of its neurons are in its arms, which act with considerable autonomy. Even so, it shows the signs of integration that led the United Kingdom to recognize it as sentient.

But this cuts both ways. If nociception is not enough to prove pain, it is not enough to deny it either: saying an animal "only has nociception" assumes exactly what is in question. The scientific question is whether, beyond the nociceptors, there are signs that the stimulus is felt. Researchers look for behaviors a reflex does not explain: the animal trades a reward to escape harm, tends the injured area, learns to avoid the place where it was hurt, seeks out a painkiller when injured and changes its behavior when it gets one. These criteria are in the report that led the United Kingdom to recognize octopuses, crabs and lobsters as sentient (Birch et al., 2021), and many fish meet them (see A2).

Sentience requires minimal consciousness, not self-awareness

Sentience presupposes consciousness. To feel, there has to be someone who feels: a subjectivity, a point of view for which the experience is good or bad. This is minimal phenomenal consciousness, there being something it is like to be that being, in Nagel’s formula.

The converse does not necessarily hold. Every experience felt as good or bad is conscious, but there can be consciousness without valence: seeing the blue of the sky is an experience, and it need not hurt or please. The philosopher David Chalmers imagines "Vulcans", beings that would have experiences without anything being good or bad for them. That is why the word "sentience" is used in more than one sense, as Chalmers shows (Sentience and Moral Status). Sometimes it means only the part of consciousness that has valence, as at the Sentience Institute. Sometimes it means consciousness in general: Animal Ethics uses the two words as synonyms, because for it having experiences already means being able to be affected positively or negatively, and the psychologist Nicholas Humphrey, in Sentience (2022), ties sentience to the phenomenal consciousness of sensations, which in his view only mammals and birds have, a minority position. And sometimes it means only the capacity to perceive the environment through the senses, as in the book Sentient (2021), by Jackie Higgins, about the senses of animals. Chalmers proposes speaking of "consciousness sentience" and "affective sentience" to avoid the confusion. In this guide, sentience is the capacity to have good or bad experiences, close to the definition by the researcher Gilson Volpato quoted by the blog consCIÊNCIA animal: subjectively experiencing pain, cold, comfort or discomfort and telling these states apart as good or bad (O que é senciência?, in Portuguese). In most animals the two go together, but the distinction matters in borderline cases, such as possible artificial minds (see Sentience in AI and robots and N21).

And sentience does not require the other senses of "consciousness", which are often confused with it: self-awareness, reflection on one’s own states, verbal report, abstract reasoning or a sense of the future. The sentientist movement, for example, chooses to speak of sentience precisely because "consciousness" sometimes includes intelligence, planning or sapience (Sentientism). None of these abilities is necessary to suffer. A baby does not recognize itself in the mirror and does not speak, and it feels pain. Demanding these abilities to recognize sentience is switching criteria halfway (see A15 and A16).

Sentience and cognitive abilities

Some definitions include cognitive abilities. The animal welfare scientist Donald Broom, of the University of Cambridge, starts from the idea that sentience is the capacity to have feelings, but spells out what this would involve: a sentient being would have some ability to evaluate the actions of others in relation to itself and third parties, to remember some of its own actions and their consequences, to assess risks and benefits, to have some feelings and to have some degree of awareness (Considering animals’ feelings, 2016). The list is useful as a research guide, because it gathers signs that can be observed. But as a definition it mixes what we want to know, whether there are feelings, with abilities that usually come along and serve as evidence. Taken literally, it would leave out a newborn, who feels pain before being able to evaluate the actions of third parties. That is why most authors, and Broom himself in the summary of his book, keep to the core: sentience is the capacity to have feelings.

Something similar happens with intelligence, which has two senses. In the broad sense, it is the ability to solve problems and achieve goals in varied environments, to do something well; the researchers Shane Legg and Marcus Hutter gathered dozens of definitions and arrived at a synthesis of this kind (A Collection of Definitions of Intelligence, 2007). In this sense, an octopus that opens a jar, a crow that uses a tool and a program that plays chess are intelligent. In the strict sense, the word usually refers to general intelligence, tied to abstraction: reasoning, planning, thinking abstractly and understanding complex ideas, as in the definition signed by 52 researchers in the field in 1994 (Mainstream science on intelligence). Neither sense is a condition for feeling. A system can be very intelligent without feeling anything, like today’s artificial intelligences, as far as we know, and an animal can feel pain without solving any problem. Intelligence can help show that someone is there and can change what a being is able to suffer, such as the anguish of foreseeing the future, but it is not what makes it capable of being harmed. Giving more weight to those who are more intelligent is what some authors call intelligentism (see A15).

What is under debate

  • What gives moral status. For the most common sentientism, what matters is valence: a being counts if it can have good or bad experiences. The philosopher David Chalmers disagrees: he imagines "Vulcans", conscious beings who feel neither pleasure nor pain, and argues that killing them would be wrong, so that consciousness would be enough for moral status (Sentience and Moral Status). Defenders of valence reply that such beings may not even be possible, or that, with nothing good or bad for them, they would have nothing to lose (Sentience, Vulcans, and zombies). For the defense of animals, the dispute changes little: the animals we exploit have valenced experiences.
  • Which brain structures are needed. A minority of scientists, such as the neuroscientist Brian Key, argue that without a cortex like that of mammals there is no conscious pain, and that fish therefore would not feel pain (Why fish do not feel pain, 2016). The article drew dozens of critical responses from other researchers, who point out that similar functions can be performed by different structures, like wings in birds and insects. The Cambridge (2012) and New York (2024) declarations went in the opposite direction to Key (see A1). Behind this dispute lies an epistemic anthropocentrism that is hard to avoid: the criteria for sentience start from the human case, and so they may miss ways of feeling very different from ours (see A7).
  • How to decide under uncertainty. In many cases, such as insects and some invertebrates, the evidence does not allow certainty. Jonathan Birch, in The Edge of Sentience (2024), proposes treating as "sentience candidates" the beings with enough evidence for the possibility to be realistic, and taking proportionate precautions, instead of demanding definitive proof before protecting them.

Why the distinction matters

It avoids both mistakes. The first is denying suffering by calling it a reflex, without looking at the evidence that goes beyond nociception. The second is attributing suffering to anything that reacts, such as plants, thermostats or computer programs, which empties the criterion (see A4). Between the two, the right question is always the same: is there someone there for whom things can go well or badly? The terms are in the glossary, and the page Antispeciesism and sentientism explains why sentience is the criterion.
