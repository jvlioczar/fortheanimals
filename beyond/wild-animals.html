Wild animal suffering

Hunger, disease and predation were not caused by us. But if sentience is what matters, this suffering cannot be dismissed just because it is natural.

The argument

If our omissions are also moral choices, neglecting suffering we did not cause, but could relieve to some extent, is also morally relevant. Authors such as Oscar Horta and Kyle Johannsen reach this conclusion by different routes, which suggests it does not depend on one specific ethical theory. In Brazil, Luciano Carlos Cunha devoted a book to the subject, Razões para ajudar (Reasons to Help, 2022), which brings together the arguments for helping and answers the most common objections.

The scale

Most wild animals are invertebrates. Brian Tomasik's estimates, which he himself considers very uncertain, put the number alive at any one time at somewhere between 1017 and 1019 terrestrial arthropods, between 1013 and 1015 or more fish, and between 1011 and 1012 wild mammals. For comparison, there are about 8 billion humans.

The number of births weighs even more. Most species reproduce by having many offspring and taking almost no care of them; few have few offspring and care for them for a long time. An ocean sunfish can lay hundreds of millions of eggs in one spawning. In a population that stays stable, on average only one offspring of each parent goes on to reproduce, and the others die, almost always soon after birth. Oscar Horta makes an illustrative calculation with a single population of cod: even assuming that nine in ten eggs die before there is any sentience, premature deaths would reach hundreds of billions in a single breeding season.

The distinction between species that bet on many offspring (r-strategists) and on few (K-strategists) comes from an ecological theory now considered outdated in several respects, but the basic difference between the two patterns of reproduction is still accepted. And one point remains open: it is not known from what stage the young of many of these species feel anything (see the contested points below).

What happens to them

Cunha describes, drawing on material gathered by the organization Animal Ethics, the harms animals suffer without any human involvement:

  • Weather: cold, heat and sudden changes in temperature, which can kill whole populations.
  • Disasters: fires, floods, droughts, storms and eruptions.
  • Disease and parasites: viral, bacterial and fungal infections, and parasites that live inside their host or consume it bit by bit (N18).
  • Hunger and thirst: above all among the young, in winter and in the dry season.
  • Stress and fear: the constant threat of predators.
  • Conflicts: with other species, such as predation, and with their own, in disputes over territory, food and mates.
  • Accidents: falls, fractures and injuries that, even when they do not kill, reduce the ability to find food and to flee. In arthropods, shedding the exoskeleton, molting, is a common cause of injury and death.

The best-known academic version of these objections is the rule of noninterference of the philosopher Paul W. Taylor (1986), which asks us to let wild animals live in freedom, without aiding them except to return them to their environment. It assumes that freedom is a synonym for well-being, which the lives of most wild animals disprove; see the entry noninterference in the glossary and point 14 of N8.

The myth of idyllic nature

The idea of a nature that is intrinsically good for those who live in it is, at best, an unverified assumption. At worst, it is a convenient way of justifying inaction. Copying nature does not work as a criterion either: as John Stuart Mill pointed out, nature kills, tortures and destroys all the time.

In nature, is violence the rule or the exception?

In ethics among humans, it is often said that nonviolence is the rule and violence the exception, to be justified by whoever commits it (see A22). In nature, the situation seems reversed: in most species, almost all the young die early, of hunger, cold, disease or predation, and violence is part of the normal workings of ecosystems. This shows a limit of negative rights, which only bind those who act: a predator is not a moral agent and violates no right. If the suffering of wild animals matters, the answer cannot come only from the ban on causing harm. It depends on positive duties, such as helping when this is possible without making things worse, and that is why this debate divides those who defend only negative rights for animals from those who also defend duties of assistance (see A21).

The most common objections

The objections to helping wild animals are answered, one by one, in family N of this guide:

  • On life in nature: N1, "Wild animals live free and happy"; N2, "Suffering and death are part of nature"; N3, "A balanced nature is good for animals"; N4, "Nature is fair, every animal has weapons to escape".
  • On the duty to help: N6, "We didn't cause it, so we have no duty to help"; N7, "Domestic animals are our responsibility, wild ones are not"; N13, "We'd have to drop everything to help wild animals".
  • On intervening: N8, "Who are we to interfere with nature?"; N9, "Meddling with nature is wrong; we could make everything worse"; N12, "It's impossible to solve suffering in nature; it's too big and complex"; N10, "Using medicine and genetics to end suffering is playing God"; N11, "Saving a wild animal only prolongs its agony"; N5, "Helping the weak ruins natural selection".
  • On what sentientists supposedly defend: N15, "Sentientists are against preserving species and biodiversity"; N16, "Sentientists want to get rid of forests"; N17, "Sentientists want to get rid of predators"; N18, "Sentientists defend parasites"; N19, "Sentientists support population control and antinatalism for wild animals"; N20, "Sentientists give the same weight to insects, invertebrates and mammals".
  • On strategy: N14, "Helping wild animals isn't veganism; it's a different cause".

What is already being done

The question is old. Lewis Gompertz was already discussing, in Moral Inquiries on the Situation of Man and of Brutes (1824), the suffering animals cause one another, and the economist Yew-Kwang Ng showed in 1995 the scale of suffering in nature, in a paper that only became influential in the 2000s, according to Daniel Dorado's survey (2015). Among activists, the topic began to circulate in that same decade. Brian Tomasik recounts that, on reading Peter Singer in 2005, he realized animals mattered, but that Singer himself, in 1973, had defended not interfering with predation on practical grounds; in the following years, the debate spread in forums and groups until dedicated organizations, such as Animal Ethics, appeared.

Helping wild animals is not just an idea. Many interventions already exist. They are almost always motivated by human health or species conservation, but they benefit the animals themselves and provide data for future help:

  • Vaccine baits. Oral rabies vaccination campaigns for foxes, begun in Switzerland in 1978, eliminated fox-transmitted rabies from much of Western and Central Europe. In the United States, baits with a vaccine against sylvatic plague are distributed to prairie dogs, and in Montana they have also been tested with drones.
  • Treating disease. In a field trial published in 2019, bats treated with a probiotic bacterium against white-nose syndrome had an apparent winter survival of 46%, against about 8% without treatment, in a small group of animals. In Australia, wombats with mange are treated with moxidectin delivered through flaps installed at the entrance of their burrows, without capture, with results that vary between populations.
  • Disasters. In Kaziranga National Park, India, raised platforms serve as shelter during the yearly floods. Officials credit them with part of the drop in animal deaths, from about 400 in 2017 to around 200 in 2019, although experts point out that the solution is partial. Animals are also rescued from fires, floods, broken ice and mud.
  • Orphans and shelters. Orphanages such as the Sheldrick Wildlife Trust, in Kenya, raise young who have lost their mothers, and artificial nests and shelters are used by birds, bats and other animals.
  • Food and reproduction. Feeding starving animals can, on its own, increase reproduction and hunger in the next generation; combined with reproductive control, it avoids that effect. The sterile insect technique, developed in the mid-twentieth century, reduces populations of disease-carrying insects by releasing sterile males, without killing anyone.

Research before action

Ecosystems are complex, and a well-intentioned intervention, such as vaccinating a population, can have effects that make the outcome worse. That does not mean never intervening. It calls for serious research and small, reversible interventions before anything bigger. That is the proposal of what is called welfare biology: studying how animals living outside human control are affected, for better and for worse.

It is not the same as neighboring fields. Conservation biology studies animals as members of species and parts of ecosystems. Compassionate conservation, associated with Marc Bekoff, looks for conservation methods that do not harm animals. Conservation welfare, associated with Ngaio Beausoleil, studies the welfare of animals when this serves conservation goals. Welfare biology looks at the good of the animals themselves, whether or not it serves conservation.

The right question is not "should we intervene in nature?". It is what the evidence suggests about this specific intervention, and how much it needs to improve before justifying a bigger action.

Precautionary rules, and what falls to each of us

Cunha proposes some rules so that helping does not make things worse:

  • No legitimate intervention kills animals. The rule also keeps the proposal from serving as a front for hunting.
  • Outside emergencies, those who intervene are professionals, such as biologists, veterinarians and welfare scientists.
  • Nothing large-scale before there are studies; meanwhile, keep up the small interventions that have already worked.
  • Decide by expected impact, weighing the probability of each scenario, and not only by the worst imaginable scenario.

For most people, the duty is simpler: support research, through donations and taxes, and bring the subject into public debate, showing that the idyllic view of nature is false and that helping animals is not the same as meeting environmental goals. No one has to drop everything or go into the forest (N13).

Contested points

Part of the debate remains open, including among those who defend animals:

  • The balance of welfare. The economist Yew-Kwang Ng proposed, in 1995, that suffering outweighs well-being in nature. In 2019, with Zach Groff, he corrected an error in that model and concluded that the balance is uncertain; Heather Browning and Walter Veit (2023) argue that it may be positive. Cunha replies that, even with a positive balance, there would still be an immense number of lives going very badly, and that this alone is enough for there to be a reason to help (N1).
  • When the young can feel. It is not known from what stage the larvae and young of many fish, amphibians and invertebrates are sentient. Cunha cites studies in which zebrafish larvae respond to noxious stimuli in a way similar to adults, but this remains the most uncertain empirical point in the debate (N20).
  • Not expanding nature. Cunha, Horta and Tomasik argue against expanding or recreating natural areas, because of the births this would multiply. Conservationists and climate policies argue the opposite, and the argument depends on the balance of welfare, which is uncertain (N16).
  • Climate and the number of animals. Oscar Horta and Mat Rozas (2024) argue that global warming is likely to make cold regions habitable, among the largest land areas on the planet, and increase the number of land animals, especially those who have many offspring, even while reducing biodiversity. It is a forecast, which depends on how ecosystems respond, but it shows that loss of species and number of individuals are different things. Brian Tomasik, who has analyzed the topic in detail, considers the net effect very uncertain, with similar odds either way, and still argues for reducing emissions (N23).
  • Reprogramming the biosphere. The philosopher David Pearce proposes using gene editing, gene drives and fertility control to reduce suffering in nature over decades, in what he calls compassionate biology. The proposal is contested because of ecological risks and uncertainty, and most of those who defend helping wild animals now prioritize research and small interventions (N10, N17).
  • Reproductive control. Cunha accepts, under certain conditions, sterilizing wild animals without consent, and compares the case to human situations. The comparison can be read as an echo of eugenic arguments, and perspectives such as reproductive justice tend to reject the principle (N19).
  • Intervening or letting be. There is disagreement within animal advocacy. Tom Regan, Gary Francione, Sônia T. Felipe, Clare Palmer, and Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka are against intervening in nature to help wild animals, or accept it only in limited cases. Oscar Horta, Catia Faria, Luciano Carlos Cunha, Jeff McMahan, Steve Sapontzis, Tyler Cowen, Charles Fink, David Pearce and Mikel Torres Aldave argue that there are reasons to help. Torres Aldave, in "The Case for Intervention in Nature on Behalf of Animals" (2015), reviews the main objections to intervention and concludes that none holds up. The pages on deontology, environmentalism and liberalism show how each theory enters this debate.

What this does not say

  • It does not license exploitation. See C5, "Lions eat other animals": lions are not moral agents and have no alternative.
  • It does not require locking up predators. F4 is often used to ridicule the question, which is serious and does not favor eating animals.
  • It is not against environmental care. The environment is treated as a means to the lives of real sentient beings, not as an end.
