Sentience in extraterrestrials, plants and other forms of life

There is no evidence of life beyond Earth, let alone sentient life, and no robust evidence that plants or fungi feel. Both questions test whether our criterion is really a criterion, and what we would do if the evidence changed.

This is a possibility, not a fact. To date there is no evidence of extraterrestrial life. This page is an exercise in consistency, not a claim about what exists.

The criterion ignores the planet

If we ever discover sentient beings elsewhere in the universe, the same criterion would require their interests to be taken into account. The species line was never the right criterion, and the planetary one would not be either.

The symmetry test

By the principle of A10, "They're just animals", sentient extraterrestrials could be bred for food because they are not of our species. By the same rule, they could breed us. Almost no one accepts the second half, which shows the principle is not taken seriously.

The same goes for A2 and A11: if superiority or "what is normal for the species" decided, a more capable species would have a status above ours. Whoever rejects the conclusion is rejecting the premise.

How would we know

Probably with more difficulty than with animals, with whom we share an evolutionary kinship that helps interpret signs of pain. A very different mind may have no structure or behavior we recognize. Lack of resemblance to us would not be proof of an absence of experience.

How to decide under uncertainty. Instead of asking only "is this being sentient, yes or no?", ask how likely it is and weigh that by the size of the harm or benefit at stake. A moderate probability multiplied by an immense scale can outweigh a high certainty over small numbers. Uncertainty about a borderline case does not contaminate the certainty about the criterion: sentience is still what matters.

What about plants, fungi and other forms of life?

Some people, including within the debate on animal ethics, raise the possibility that plants, fungi or other forms of life without a nervous system also feel. The question is legitimate, and the honest answer has three parts.

What the evidence says today. There is no robust evidence of consciousness in beings without a brain, ganglia or some other structure that centralizes information. Plants and fungi react to their environment, exchange chemical and electrical signals and adjust their growth. But reacting is not feeling: a thermostat also reacts to temperature. These behaviors have a physico-chemical explanation that does not require subjective experience, which is not the case for the behavior of insects, crustaceans and other animals with a centralized nervous system (A14).

What remains open. Science is not a closed field. There are hypotheses of sentience in other forms of life, and there are philosophical views such as panpsychism, on which consciousness would be a basic property of nature, present in different degrees. These ideas have serious defenders and also strong criticisms: the physicist Sabine Hossenfelder, for example, argues that attributing mental states to particles such as electrons contradicts what particle physics observes.

What would change. If the evidence comes to point more and more in that direction, ethics will have to be adapted to include those beings too, as is already being discussed for digital minds. The criterion would stay the same: what matters is who can feel. And even in that scenario, eating plants directly would cause less harm than eating animals, who consume far more plants over their lives (F11).

What this changes here and now

Almost nothing in practice, and that is exactly why the exercise is useful: with no personal interest at stake, it is easier to see that what would make us respect an unknown being is the capacity to feel, not appearance, intelligence or origin. That capacity already exists, here, in those we call animals.
