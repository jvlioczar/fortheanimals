K4

"There is no ethical consumption under capitalism"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about effectiveness and individual responsibility, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended only by each person's impact, anyone who proved their purchase changes nothing would be let off. But no one accepts this for other harms: taking part in an injustice is still wrong even when each person's share is small.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

there's a partial truth here: almost every product involves some exploitation or harm in its supply chain. But using that truth to cancel out every difference is a mistake. Between buying beans and buying meat from animals raised and killed, the difference in harm is enormous. That there's no perfect consumption doesn't mean all consumption is the same.

Short answer

It's true that almost every product involves some harm in its supply chain. But that doesn't make all choices the same. Between buying beans and buying meat, the difference in harm is enormous: one involves raising and killing animals, the other doesn't. That there's no perfect consumption doesn't mean there's no better consumption. And criticizing the system doesn't stop anyone from changing their own plate.

For children

Almost everything we buy might have some problem in how it was made. But some things cause much more harm than others. Buying beans is very different from buying meat, which requires an animal's death. Even if nothing is perfect, we can choose what hurts less.

Ask a question back

"If nothing is perfect, isn't buying something that causes less harm still better?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Everything is exploitation. Your sneakers were made with slave labor."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Under capitalism, supply chains involve exploitation of workers, environmental harm and animal suffering.
  2. P2No consumer can avoid these harms completely.
  3. P3If no consumption is ethical, individual consumption choices have no moral significance.
  4. CSo veganism makes no one more ethical, and change must be systemic.

P1 and P2 are largely true. The problem lies in P3: the impossibility of purity doesn't make all choices equivalent.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument turns "no choice is perfect" into "all choices are equal":

    ClaimStatus
    Almost all consumption involves some harmTrue
    No consumption is perfectly ethicalTrue
    So all choices are equivalentFalse: the differences in harm are enormous
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's the nirvana fallacy.

    The impossibility of perfection doesn't make striving for better irrelevant.

    2. The differences in harm are enormous.

    Meat requires raising and killing animals, as well as more land, water and emissions (H6, H7). Beans and rice have some impact too, like all agriculture (H2), but far less, and they don't require raising and killing animals.

    3. Criticizing the system and changing consumption complement each other.

    No one has to choose one or the other (K9).

    4. The argument works against any ethical choice.

    If no consumption is ethical, there'd be no point avoiding products of slave labor either, which those who use the argument usually don't accept.

    5. Critics of capitalism themselves make choices.

    Many boycott companies or products for ethical reasons.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Our World in Data, how many animals are factory-farmedAbout 74% of farmed land animals live in factory farmsShows the scale of the harm linked to meat
    Our World in Data, land use and dietsA global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%Shows the difference in impact between diets
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: focusing on consumption depoliticizes

    The most serious version says that focusing on individual choices distracts from structural change, and that veganism as a lifestyle can end up serving the market itself.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The two can go together.

    Individual choices and political action reinforce each other (K9).

    Not buying meat isn't a lifestyle; it's no longer paying for deaths.

    Structural change also depends on people.

    Movements start with individuals acting consistently.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    That no consumption is perfect doesn't answer the question about the animals. Among imperfect choices, one involves paying for the death of sentient beings, and the other doesn't. The question remains: why choose the one that kills?

What the argument gets right

What is true

The critique of capitalist supply chains points to real problems: exploitation of workers, environmental harm and suffering behind many products. It's also true that structural change is needed, and that individual consumption has limits.

But

But recognizing those limits doesn't make all choices equivalent. Among the possible harms, no longer paying for animals to be killed is a simple and significant choice.
