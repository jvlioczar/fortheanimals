F6

"You also kill mosquitoes and rats at home"

The answer in one sentence

self-defense and real conflict are different from raising animals to eat. A mosquito that transmits dengue or malaria, a tick attached to a dog or rats contaminating food pose a concrete threat; a pig in a shed threatens no one. But not even risk authorizes killing at will: humans are among the animals that kill the most humans, and we don't claim self-defense to kill people as a precaution. The right order is to prevent first, then use the least harmful means, and kill only as a last resort, in an emergency. And prevention often protects more: in several cities, releasing mosquitoes carrying the Wolbachia bacterium, which doesn't kill mosquitoes, has sharply reduced dengue. If killing mosquitoes were inconsistent, that would be a problem for whoever kills mosquitoes, not a justification for raising and slaughtering billions of animals.

Reply

When you protect yourself from a mosquito that carries dengue, remove ticks from your dog or keep rats from contaminating food, you're defending yourself against a real threat. Raising an animal to eat it isn't that: it threatens no one. But risk, on its own, doesn't authorize killing. Mosquitoes are the animals that kill the most humans, and humans themselves come right after; we don't claim self-defense to kill people as a precaution because of that. The right order is: prevent first (screens, sealing, removing breeding sites, treating parasitized animals), then use the least harmful means, and only as a last resort kill, in an emergency. Prevention often protects more: releasing mosquitoes carrying the Wolbachia bacterium, which doesn't kill mosquitoes, cut dengue by 77% in a trial in Indonesia. In the end, none of this makes it acceptable to raise and slaughter billions of animals.

For children

If a mosquito can give you a disease, keeping it away is protecting yourself. If a tick is hurting your dog, taking it off is caring for your dog. That's different from raising a little pig in a cramped place just to eat it later. And the best protection is often to stop the bugs from coming: getting rid of standing water, using window screens, cleaning the yard. That way we stay protected and don't need to kill the butterfly, the ladybug and so many other creatures that harm no one. Killing is only for when there's no other way.

Ask a question back

"Was the pig that became bacon threatening anyone?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"You use insect repellent. If there's a cockroach, you kill it. You put flea treatment on your dog. Mosquitoes carry disease and a scorpion can kill a child. Killing these creatures is about protection. So where do you draw the line?"

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Some animals are real threats: mosquitoes transmit dengue, Zika, chikungunya and malaria, certain scorpions can cause serious stings, rats contaminate food, and lice, fleas and ticks harm those they parasitize.
  2. P2Killing these animals protects us, which is why almost everyone, including many vegans, kills them.
  3. P3If it's acceptable to kill animals for one human interest, it's also acceptable to kill them for another, such as eating meat.
  4. CSo killing insects and other small animals is justified in general, and vegans can't condemn meat eaters without being inconsistent.

P1 is true. P2 is only partly true: in several cases, killing isn't the most effective form of protection, and not all vegans kill these animals. The bigger problem lies in P3: the interest in not catching a disease, in protecting a parasitized animal or in not having one's home contaminated is very different from the interest in a flavor. And the conclusion goes beyond the premises, moving from "some species, in some cases" to "insects" in general.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, which equates interests of very different weights, at P2, which assumes killing is the best protection, and in the generalized conclusion.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Self-defense is a recognized exception.

    Every ethics accepts defense against real threats, including among humans. That doesn't make it acceptable to harm those who threaten no one.

    2. Risk, on its own, doesn't authorize killing.

    According to Our World in Data, mosquitoes are by far the animals that kill the most humans: about 760,000 deaths a year, mostly from malaria. In second place come humans themselves, with about 600,000 deaths a year from direct violence among ourselves. We don't claim self-defense to kill people as a precaution just because humans can kill us. With humans, we first prevent (locks, lighting, policing, policies against violence); then we use the means that cause the least harm; and lethal force is accepted only as a last resort, in the face of a concrete attack. The same reasoning applies to other animals.

    3. The right order: prevent, reduce harm, and only then kill.

    This yields a simple principle for conflicts with animals:

    1. Prevent first. Stop the conflict from happening: remove breeding sites, use screens and repellents, seal cracks, store food, treat and protect companion animals.
    2. Then, the least harmful means. If the animal is already there, shoo it away, catch and release it, or use methods that don't kill.
    3. Last, kill, in an emergency. Only in the face of a concrete and immediate threat, when the other options have failed or aren't within reach, and with the method that causes the least suffering.

    Killing isn't forbidden in every case, but it's the last option, not the first.

    4. The interests have different weights.

    Avoiding dengue, mange or contamination is a serious interest; the taste of meat is a lesser interest that can be met in other ways (J6).

    5. The threat comes from few species.

    Among the millions of insect species, only a small fraction transmit diseases to humans or cause serious harm. The phrase "killing insects protects us" uses the danger of a few to justify killing all.

    6. Against scorpions, poison can make things worse.

    An example comes from Brazil, where the yellow scorpion causes many stings: the Ministry of Health doesn't recommend chemical control of scorpions in urban areas, because the products have no proven effectiveness and can make the animals leave their hiding places, which increases the risk of stings. The guidance is to manage the environment: clean yards, no rubble, sealed drains and cracks. Scorpions, by the way, aren't insects but arachnids.

    7. Against dengue, prevention works better.

    Fogging only kills the adult mosquitoes it reaches and is reserved for outbreaks. The main measure is removing breeding sites. The Wolbachia method releases mosquitoes carrying a bacterium that hinders virus transmission, without killing the population: in a randomized trial in Yogyakarta, Indonesia, it cut dengue by 77%, and in Niterói, Brazil, it was associated with 69% fewer cases.

    8. The method matters.

    Even when killing seems necessary, some methods cause more suffering than others. Glue traps, for example, hold the animal until they die of exhaustion or hunger. And broad-spectrum insecticides also kill the animals that eat mosquitoes, such as spiders, geckos and toads.

    9. Not all vegans do this.

    Many shoo, catch and release, or prevent animals from getting in. The argument generalizes to all what some do, and even those who kill don't show that animal farming is acceptable.

    10. Insects count too.

    There's a realistic possibility that insects are sentient (A3). That doesn't make the case equal to raising pigs, but it asks us to take their lives seriously as well: preferring prevention to killing and, when killing is unavoidable, choosing methods that don't prolong suffering.

    11. Parasitism is a special case.

    Lice, fleas and ticks live at the expense of another sentient being, who suffers itching, wounds, anemia and disease. Treating parasitism protects the host. Many parasites are probably not sentient, and death isn't a harm for those who don't feel. And preventing new infestations kills no one: it only keeps new parasites from being born and new hosts from suffering. Luciano Carlos Cunha discusses this point in detail in his book Razões para ajudar (Reasons to Help, in Portuguese), devoted to wild animal suffering.

    12. The best scenario has to be calculated.

    Sometimes controlling a population, including of a species, may be what causes the least suffering overall: removing mosquito breeding sites, for example, means fewer mosquitoes are born to die and fewer people and animals fall ill. But that calculation has to consider how many beings are affected, how much they suffer and which methods do the least harm. Population control is a decision justified by its outcome, not a license to kill at will.

    13. Inconsistency doesn't change what's right.

    If someone kills mosquitoes needlessly, that's their problem, not permission for everyone to cause more harm. It's the tu quoque fallacy.

    14. The logic is different.

    Farming brings billions of animals into existence every year precisely so they can die. Controlling pests and parasites responds to a concrete threat that already exists, and even then should follow the order: prevention, less harmful means and, only last, killing.

    15. Judging whoever acts isn't the same as judging what happens.

    Someone who kills a mosquito by reflex may be excused, and that concerns their character. But the question of what should be done, and of the harm the animal suffers, is a different one: it depends on what is at stake for each party, not on whether the person acting is vegan (Cunha, 2022, pp. 158–159, 191, 223–224).

    16. Less harm is possible even in these cases.

    Brian Tomasik avoids killing healthy insects and, when he finds an injured or dying insect, tries to spare it agony with the quickest method he knows. There are practical guides to avoid hurting insects at home, such as sealing entry points, removing exposed food and releasing animals outside. The point isn't perfection, but that the difficulty of avoiding all harm doesn't authorize causing avoidable harm.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: in a moment of danger, there's no time to prevent

    The most serious version says that when there's a scorpion in a child's bedroom or a dengue epidemic in the neighborhood, there's no time for prevention, and killing is the immediate response. And it adds that vegans often kill cockroaches or mosquitoes not because of a real threat but out of annoyance, an interest as trivial as taste.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Immediate danger is the exception.

    That's exactly the third step of the order: killing as a last resort, in the face of a concrete threat. It doesn't justify killing all insects, or spraying the house routinely.

    Even in an emergency there are options.

    The scorpion can be covered with a jar and taken to the local animal or pest control service, as health authorities in many places advise.

    The emergency shows why prevention matters.

    The more prevention, the fewer dangerous situations and the fewer deaths, of humans and of other animals.

    If it's out of annoyance, it's wrong too.

    The criticism hits the behavior, not the principle. And it doesn't justify meat: that someone kills a cockroach out of annoyance doesn't make raising billions of animals acceptable.

    Consistency points toward more care.

    The right conclusion is to treat all animals better, including small ones, not worse.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The argument points to a real issue: mosquitoes transmit serious diseases, some scorpions can kill children, and conflicts with animals exist. Protecting yourself from real threats is legitimate. And killing pests and parasites also involves beings that may feel; animal advocates shouldn't pretend this is irrelevant.

But

But the threat comes from few species, and the best protection, according to health authorities themselves, is often prevention. The order is to prevent, then reduce harm, and only then kill, in an emergency. And defense against a real threat is different from raising animals to eat: the first is a hard conflict; the second is a choice that can be avoided.
