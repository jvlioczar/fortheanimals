F5

"You also kill mosquitoes and rats at home"

The answer in one sentence

self-defense and real conflict are different from raising animals to eat. A mosquito that transmits dengue or malaria, a tick attached to your dog or rats contaminating food pose a concrete threat; a pig in a shed threatens no one. Not all vegans kill these animals, and insects count too: that's why it's best to prevent before killing. Sometimes controlling populations may be what causes the least suffering overall, but that has to be calculated, not used as an excuse. And if killing mosquitoes were inconsistent, that would be a problem for whoever kills mosquitoes, not a justification for raising and slaughtering billions of animals.

Short answer

Protecting yourself from a mosquito that carries dengue, removing ticks from your dog or keeping rats from contaminating food is defense against a real threat. Raising an animal to eat it isn't: it threatens no one. Not every vegan kills these animals, and the ideal is prevention: screens, sealing, removing breeding sites, treating parasitized animals. Insects count too, and that's exactly why it's worth thinking about what causes the least suffering. None of this makes it acceptable to raise and slaughter billions of animals.

For children

If a mosquito can give you a disease, keeping it away is protecting yourself. If a tick is hurting your dog, taking it off is caring for your dog. That's different from raising a little pig in a cramped place just to eat it later. And when we can, the best thing is to stop the bugs from showing up, with screens and cleaning, so no one has to be killed.

Ask a question back

"Was the pig that became bacon threatening anyone?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"You use insect repellent. If there's a cockroach, you kill it. You put flea treatment on your dog. So where do you draw the line?"

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Vegans also kill animals, such as mosquitoes, cockroaches, lice, fleas, ticks and rats, when they cause problems.
  2. P2This shows they accept killing animals for human interests.
  3. P3Eating meat also serves a human interest.
  4. CSo vegans can't condemn meat eaters without being inconsistent.

P1 is true in many cases, though not all. The problem lies in P2 and P3: the interest in not catching a disease, in protecting a parasitized animal or in not having one's home contaminated is very different from the interest in a flavor.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, which equates interests of very different weights, and at P1, which generalizes to all vegans what only some do.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument equates very different situations:

    SituationIs there a threat?AlternativesType of harm
    Disease-carrying mosquitoYes, to healthScreens, repellents, removing breeding sitesDefense
    Lice, fleas and ticksYes, to a sentient host, human or notPrevention, treating the hostDefense of the parasitized
    Rats at homeYes, to health and foodSealing, traps that don't killDefense
    Pig raised for meatNoMany: other foodsExploitation
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Self-defense is a recognized exception.

    Every ethics accepts defense against real threats, including among humans. That doesn't make it acceptable to harm those who threaten no one.

    2. The interests have different weights.

    Avoiding dengue, mange or contamination is a serious interest; the taste of meat is a lesser interest that can be met in other ways (J5).

    3. Not all vegans do this.

    Many shoo, catch and release, or prevent animals from getting in. The argument generalizes to all what some do, and even those who kill don't show that animal farming is acceptable.

    4. Insects count too.

    There's a realistic possibility that insects are sentient (A3). That doesn't make the case equal to raising pigs, but it asks us to take their lives seriously as well: preferring prevention to killing and, when killing is unavoidable, choosing methods that don't prolong suffering.

    5. Parasitism is a special case.

    Lice, fleas and ticks live at the expense of another sentient being, who suffers itching, wounds, anemia and disease. Treating parasitism protects the host. Many parasites are probably not sentient, and death isn't a harm for those who don't feel. And preventing new infestations kills no one: it only keeps new parasites from being born and new hosts from suffering. Luciano Carlos Cunha discusses this point in detail in his book Razões para ajudar (Reasons to Help, in Portuguese), devoted to wild animal suffering.

    6. The best scenario has to be calculated.

    Sometimes controlling a population, including of a species, may be what causes the least suffering overall: removing mosquito breeding sites, for example, means fewer mosquitoes are born to die and fewer people and animals fall ill. But that calculation has to consider how many beings are affected, how much they suffer and which methods do the least harm. Population control is a decision justified by its outcome, not a license to kill at will.

    7. Inconsistency doesn't change what's right.

    If someone kills mosquitoes needlessly, that's their problem, not permission for everyone to cause more harm. It's the tu quoque fallacy.

    8. The logic is different.

    Farming brings billions of animals into existence every year precisely so they can die. Controlling pests and parasites responds to a concrete threat that already exists, and even then should prefer prevention and less harmful methods.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: comfort is also an interest

    The most serious version says vegans often kill cockroaches or mosquitoes not because of a real threat but out of annoyance, and that this would be an interest as trivial as taste.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    If it's out of annoyance, it's wrong too.

    The criticism hits the behavior, not the principle.

    It doesn't justify meat.

    That someone kills a cockroach out of annoyance doesn't make raising billions of animals acceptable.

    Consistency points toward more care.

    The right conclusion is to treat all animals better, including small ones, not worse.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The argument points to a real issue: conflicts with animals exist, and killing pests and parasites also involves beings that may feel. Animal advocates shouldn't pretend this is irrelevant. They should prefer prevention and non-lethal methods whenever possible, and think carefully about what causes the least suffering, including when population control is necessary.

But

But defense against a real threat is different from raising animals to eat. The first is a hard conflict; the second is a choice that can be avoided.
