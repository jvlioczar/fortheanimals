N14

"Sentientists give the same weight to insects, invertebrates and mammals"

The answer in one sentence

equal consideration does not mean equal weight. The principle says that similar interests should weigh the same, whoever has them. But how much a being can suffer or flourish may vary from species to species, and each individual's weight depends on that. Insects and other invertebrates are counted because there is growing evidence that many of them feel, and the number of victims is enormous. How much each one weighs is an open, debated question, and sentientists give several answers.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

Equal consideration doesn't say that all beings weigh the same, but that similar interests weigh the same, whoever has them. How much an insect suffers is debated, and estimates point to a weight well below that of a mammal. But the number of insects exploited and killed is in the trillions a year, and there is already enough evidence to give them the benefit of the doubt. Even with a low weight per individual, the total can be large. The subject is controversial, and it is worth discussing what each position implies.

For children

Giving equal attention isn't treating everyone the same way. If a baby and an adult are both hungry, both matter, but what each one needs is different. With animals it's similar: an insect may feel less than a dog, or feel in a different way, and nobody knows for sure yet. But if it does feel, what happens to it matters too, and we should be careful.

Ask a question back

"If each insect counted a thousand times less than a person, what should we do about trillions of them being bred and killed?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"So for you an ant is worth the same as a cow, or a person? That's absurd."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Sentientists hold that every sentient being deserves equal consideration.
  2. P2Insects and other invertebrates are in that group, or may be.
  3. P3Giving the same weight to an ant and to a mammal is absurd.
  4. CSo equal consideration is absurd, or sentientism has to drop the insects.

P1 and P2 describe real positions. The problem lies in P3 and in the bridge between P1 and P3: equal consideration of interests does not require the same weight for every individual, but the same weight for similar interests. The argument is invalid as a criticism of the principle. There is, however, a legitimate underlying question: how much does each being weigh, and with what certainty?

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument mixes up two things:

    ThingWhat it says
    Equal considerationSimilar interests should be taken into account the same way, whatever the species
    Equal weightEach individual counts exactly the same in every situation

    The first is the principle defended by Peter Singer. The second is a far stronger position that almost no sentientist holds.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Equal consideration is not equal treatment.

    Singer states that the principle of equal consideration of interests does not require equal treatment: different interests call for different treatment. Giving the same consideration to a baby and to an adult does not mean giving both the same food, the same school or the same medicine. It means taking into account what each can lose and gain.

    2. Weight depends on how much a being can feel, and that is uncertain.

    The Moral Weight Project, from the research center Rethink Priorities, estimated the "welfare range" of several species relative to humans, that is, how well or badly their lives can go. According to the Faunalytics summary, the estimate was about 50% for pigs, 30 to 35% for chickens, 20 to 25% for octopuses and less than 10% for bees, silkworms and black soldier flies. The estimates are provisional: data on insects are scarce, traits with no evidence were marked "unknown", and the results depend on modeling choices. A welfare range is also not a moral weight by itself, which depends on further ethical choices.

    3. There is evidence that many invertebrates feel.

    The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024) states that there is at least a realistic possibility of conscious experience in all vertebrates and in many invertebrates, including cephalopods, decapod crustaceans and insects. For insects, the chapter in the Questão de Ética collection gathers evidence of flexible behavior, distinct brains and integration of sensory information, and concludes that there is reason to give the benefit of the doubt to all insects.

    4. Lower weight is not zero weight, and numbers count.

    The same chapter proposes an exercise: suppose each human counts a thousand times more than each insect. To invest the same in insects and in people in equally bad situations, there would have to be a thousand times more insects. It is estimated that the cochineal (dye), edible-insect and silkworm industries alone kill, together, several trillion a year. Even with a low weight per individual, the total can be large.

    5. Uncertainty calls for care, not indifference.

    Under uncertainty, the question isn't "does it feel or not", but "what is the probability, and how much is at stake". A moderate probability multiplied by an immense scale can outweigh a high certainty in small numbers. That is the logic of precaution, which applies to any being.

    6. Some do defend total equality, but it is a minority position.

    The author Joan Dunayer holds, in Speciesism (2004), that a firefly has as much claim to moral consideration as a rabbit. The philosopher Steve Best criticized this position as leading to incoherence, since we all choose between life forms all the time. Someone who rejects total equality can also reject excluding insects: they are two different positions.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: if there is uncertainty, it is better not to count insects

    The most serious version says: if we don't know whether insects feel and, when they do, they feel little, the prudent course is to treat the question as speculation and concentrate efforts on vertebrates, where the evidence is stronger.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Uncertainty is not zero weight.

    The risk of ignoring beings who feel, on an enormous scale, is large. Caution cuts both ways.

    Prioritizing is not excluding.

    It is reasonable to give priority to those with stronger evidence and, at the same time, avoid exploiting insects at industrial scale when there is an alternative, such as plant proteins.

    The human test.

    If there were doubt about whether a group of people feels pain, no one would accept treating them as objects until the doubt was settled.

The same confusion the argument makes

Contradictions

Accepts different weights between humans and other animals, but not between animals.

The same reasoning that lowers the weight of insects lowers that of birds, fish and others.

Demands certainty only from non-humans.

For people, doubt benefits those who may feel.

Finds it absurd to count insects, but isn't bothered by the scale of trillions.

The absurdity lies in the number, which is a fact, not in the principle.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It is right that the question of weight is hard and worth discussing frankly. There is no consensus on how much insects feel, on how to compare experiences between species, or on how to turn those estimates into priorities. Ethics here is controversial, and sentientists disagree among themselves. It is also right that taking an idea all the way, even when it sounds strange, is a good test.

But

But this shows that we need to discuss how to weigh, not that insects should be ignored. The weight may be lower and, even so, the number of victims and the growing evidence that they feel are reasons to take the exploitation of insects seriously.
