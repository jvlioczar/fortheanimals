J8

"I don't want to feel left out; eating together is how you belong"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the need to belong is real, and eating together is one of the rituals that bind people most. But what binds is the table, the conversation and the time spent together, not identical contents on every plate. You can be present with different food. The discomfort of being "the odd one out" exists, is greatest at the start and fades with time and support. And it doesn't compare with what the meal costs the animal, who loses their life.

Short answer

Wanting to belong is human, and I want it too. But what brings us together is the table, not matching plates. I still go to the barbecue, the birthday and the Sunday lunch, with my own food. It's uncomfortable at first, then it passes, and people get used to it. And there's someone in this sum who isn't heard: for the animal, the cost of the meal is their whole life.

For children

At snack time, everyone opens their own lunchbox. One brought a sandwich, another brought fruit, another brought cake. No one stops being friends because of that. What makes a group a group is sitting together, talking and laughing. You can be part of the group while eating something different.

Ask a question back

"If a friend of yours stopped drinking, would you drop them from the group or just order them a juice?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I don't want to be the odd one out at the table. Eating is what we do together. If I don't eat the same thing, I'm on the outside."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Belonging to a group is a basic human need, not a whim.
  2. P2Eating together is one of the main rituals through which groups form and hold together.
  3. P3Anyone who refuses the group's food risks being seen as an outsider, and research shows that vegetarians and vegans are evaluated negatively.
  4. CSo, to protect my belonging, I'm right to keep eating what the group eats.

P1, P2 and P3 are true and supported by research. The problem lies in the step to the conclusion: belonging doesn't require eating the same dish, and the social cost, however real, is weighed alone, without what is on the other side.

The argument is invalid: what follows from the premises is that the social cost must be taken seriously, not that it settles the question.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument bundles three things that can be separated:

    What the group offersDoes it depend on eating the same dish?
    Being together, talking, laughing, sharing memoriesNo
    Taking part in the ritual (Sunday lunch, the barbecue, the party)No: you can take part with different food
    Not standing out, not being the subject of remarksPartly, especially at the start

    Only the third item is tied to the plate, and it is the one that has to be weighed against what happens to the animal.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The need to belong is real.

    In 1995 the psychologists Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary gathered evidence that the need for bonds is a strong and general human motivation. And the British anthropologist Robin Dunbar found, in a national survey in the United Kingdom, that people who eat with others more often say they are happier and more connected to their community. There is nothing trivial about this concern.

    2. The stigma is real too.

    In 2017 the psychologists MacInnis and Hodson showed that omnivores evaluated vegetarians and vegans as negatively as, or more negatively than, several groups that are common targets of prejudice. In 2019 the sociologists Kelly Markowski and Susan Roxburgh ran focus groups with university students in the United States: the non-vegans expected to be judged and excluded if they changed, and the vegans confirmed that this had happened to them. It's a small, qualitative study, but it shows clearly the fear this argument speaks of.

    3. Belonging doesn't require the same dish.

    What studies of eating together measure is company. People with diabetes, allergies, celiac disease or religious restrictions sit at the same table every day (J4 covers the practical side: restaurants, travel and parties).

    4. The discomfort fades, and support makes a difference.

    In the Faunalytics survey of former vegetarians and vegans in the United States, 63% disliked that their diet made them stick out, and 84% took part in no vegetarian or vegan group or community. A later study by the same organization, which followed people at the start of the change, found that social strategies (such as asking friends and family for support or joining a community) were the most useful against the widest range of barriers. Having a group helps, and that group can be a new one (M3 covers the case of family).

    5. The costs aren't the same size.

    On one side there are remarks, jokes and some awkwardness, which tend to fade. On the other there is the life of a being who feels. Comparing the two and choosing the first only seems natural because the second isn't at the table to object.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: for some people, the group is all they have

    The most serious version says: not everyone can choose another group. In small towns, in closed families, in certain workplaces or in cultures where refusing food is a serious offense, standing out can cost a great deal, and loneliness does real harm.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    That calls for care in the how, not for giving up.

    Veganism speaks of doing what is possible and practicable. Someone in a hard situation can go slowly, pick their battles and look for support elsewhere.

    The hard case isn't the common case.

    Most people who use the argument have friends who would accept the change after a few jokes.

    Groups change from within.

    The person who stays at the table, without fighting and without giving in, is often the one who most changes what the others eat. Leaving the group or eating the same aren't the only options.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument is about what a person feels at the table. It's a legitimate feeling, but the conversation about animals isn't about it. On the other side of the meal there is someone who also wanted to stay with their own, and who was killed to be on the plate. The question is whether the fear of being seen as different is enough to justify that. When the same fear shows up elsewhere (laughing at a cruel joke so as not to stand out, for example), we usually say that it explains the behavior but doesn't justify it.

Contradictions

Doesn't exclude friends who eat differently.

The colleague with an allergy, the friend who doesn't drink and the cousin who fasts are still in the group.

Has already stood apart from the group over smaller things.

Football team, religion, vote.

Says friendship is what matters.

But assumes it wouldn't survive a different plate.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that belonging is a need, not fussiness, and that the table is one of the places where it is met. It's right that stigma against vegans exists and has been measured. And it's right to point out that changing costs more for those with a thinner support network: anyone who answers "just don't care" hasn't understood the problem.

But

But the limit lies in what is asked in exchange. The social cost falls on the person who changes, tends to fade and can be shared with others. The cost to the animal is total and can't be undone. Being part of the group is possible without someone dying for it.
