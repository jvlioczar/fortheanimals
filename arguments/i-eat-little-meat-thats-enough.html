J12

"I eat little meat; that's enough"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

eating less meat is better than eating more, and it deserves recognition. But cutting down doesn't justify what remains: if causing an animal's death without need is wrong, doing it less often is less wrong, not right. Cutting down can be an excellent path, as long as it's a path and not a destination.

Short answer

Eating less meat is good, and it deserves recognition: fewer animals die. But if killing an animal without need is wrong, doing it less often is less wrong, not right. Cutting down works very well as a path. The problem is when it becomes the destination and a justification for what remains. For every meal that still has meat, the question is the same.

For children

Hurting someone less often is better than hurting them a lot. But the best thing is not to hurt them. Eating less meat already helps animals, and it's a good start. The next step can be gradually swapping the meals that still have meat.

Ask a question back

"If cutting down was good for the animals, why stop at cutting down?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I only eat meat on weekends. I do Meatless Monday. I'm flexitarian."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Reducing meat consumption reduces the number of animals killed and the environmental harm.
  2. P2A large reduction by many people can have more impact than total abstention by a few.
  3. P3Demanding total abstention puts people off.
  4. CSo eating little meat is enough.

P1 is true. So is P2, in terms of aggregate impact. P3 has some strategic support. The problem lies in the conclusion: that reducing is good doesn't make the rest justified.

The argument is valid as a strategic argument. It fails as a moral justification of what remains.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument mixes two kinds of claim:

    ClaimTypeStatus
    Cutting down is better than not cutting downComparativeTrue
    Cutting down is enoughNormativeDoesn't follow from the first
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Better isn't enough.

    That an action reduces a harm doesn't mean the remaining harm is acceptable.

    2. The question applies to every meal.

    Each animal killed for a remaining meal has the same interest in living as the animals spared.

    3. It's moral licensing.

    Having done something good can make one feel entitled not to do more (E6).

    4. Strategy doesn't change ethics.

    It may be good to encourage reduction as a communication strategy, but that doesn't make what remains morally neutral.

    5. Cutting down already shows it's possible.

    Whoever manages days without meat has already shown that meat isn't necessary.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Our World in Data, less meat or sustainable meatEating less meat almost always cuts emissions more than switching to "sustainable" meatShows the real value of cutting down
    Kagan, *Do I Make a Difference?* (2011)Each meat purchase has a real expected effect on productionShows that remaining consumption counts too
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the perfect is the enemy of the good

    The most serious version says that insisting on total abstention can discourage people who are cutting down, and that it's better to celebrate reduction than to criticize it.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Celebrating isn't closing the matter.

    It's possible to recognize progress and invite the next step.

    The criticism is of the argument, not the person.

    "That's enough" is a conclusion, and it can be discussed respectfully.

    Cutting down is the most common path to veganism.

    Many vegans started by cutting down.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Cutting down implicitly recognizes that eating animals has a moral cost. If it does, the question doesn't disappear with the reduction: it still applies to every animal still on the plate.

Contradictions

Recognizes that cutting down is good.

And therefore that eating meat has a cost.

Shows they can go without meat.

For most meals.

Wouldn't accept "a little" for other harms.

Such as mistreating animals "only now and then".

What the argument gets right

What is true

Cutting down on meat is a real change, which spares animals and reduces environmental harm. Those who have cut down have done something valuable, and it's fair to recognize it. Strategically, encouraging reduction can reach many people.

But

But cutting down is a path, not a place to stop. The same reason that led to the reduction applies to what's still on the plate.
