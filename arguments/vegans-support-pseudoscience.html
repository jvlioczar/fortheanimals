L25

"Vegans support pseudoscience"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

some vegans do promote pseudoscience, as in any large group of people: diets that "cure" diseases, the idea that humans are herbivores, alkaline eating, distrust of vaccines. This guide has a whole list of claims like these that it asks people not to use. But the case for animals depends on none of them. It rests on sentience, which is what science describes, and the adequacy of a well-planned vegan diet is the position of the major nutrition bodies. Judging an idea by its worst advocates is a mistake that, applied consistently, would bring down any cause — including that of meat eaters, who have pseudosciences of their own.

Note: this chapter is about credibility. The idea that veganism is a fad is L24, and that the documentaries are manipulated is L26

Short answer

Some vegans spread pseudoscience, and that's bad: this guide has a whole list of those claims, asking people not to use them. But the case for animals doesn't depend on them. It rests on sentience, which science describes, and a well-planned vegan diet is considered adequate by the major nutrition bodies. Judging an idea by its worst advocates would bring down any cause, including meat eating, which has its own pseudosciences, such as the idea that an all-meat diet is the healthiest.

Question back: "Which claim, exactly, do you think is pseudoscience? If it's false, I'll gladly stop using it. But that animals feel pain isn't one of them."

For children

In every group of people there are some who believe things that aren't true, and some vegans do too. But the main reason not to hurt animals doesn't depend on any of those ideas: it depends on something science shows very well, that animals feel pain and fear. One person's wrong idea doesn't make a right idea wrong.

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Vegans believe anything: alkaline diets, meat rotting in your gut, milk causing cancer. It's all pseudoscience."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Many vegans spread claims about health and nutrition with no scientific basis.
  2. P2A movement that tolerates or promotes pseudoscience isn't trustworthy.
  3. CSo veganism's claims don't deserve credit.

P1 is true of some vegans. P2 is reasonable as a warning, but doesn't license C: the distrust should fall on the false claims, not on true claims made by the same movement.

The argument is invalid: the falsity of some claims doesn't make the others false. Each has to be examined on what it says.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument mixes three levels that need separating.

    LevelExampleWhere it stands
    False claims by some vegans"Humans are biologically herbivores", "a vegan diet cures cancer"False, and should be dropped
    Well-founded nutritional claimsA planned vegan diet, with B12, is adequate at all stages of lifePosition of the major nutrition bodies
    The moral foundationAnimals feel, and that countsSupported by the science of animal consciousness

    The argument uses the first row to discard the other two.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The case for animals doesn't depend on the false claims.

    The foundation of veganism is that animals are sentient and that exploiting them causes suffering and death without necessity. That is what science supports: the 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness states there is strong scientific support for attributing conscious experience to mammals and birds. Removing every pseudoscience from the debate leaves that foundation intact.

    2. The core nutritional claim is consensus, not pseudoscience.

    The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics considers well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets appropriate for all stages of life. Some bodies are more cautious about specific groups, such as Germany's DGE, but adequacy for healthy adults with B12 supplementation is widely accepted (G1).

    3. Judging by the worst part is a reasoning error.

    It is guilt by association: if the existence of advocates with false ideas invalidated a cause, none would survive. Medicine, environmentalism and meat eating itself all have advocates who talk nonsense.

    4. Meat eating has its own pseudosciences.

    The idea that an all-meat diet is the healthiest (G16), that vegans' B12 shows the diet isn't natural (G4), or that milk is indispensable for bones (G6) are claims the evidence doesn't support. Nobody concludes from this that eating meat is a superstition.

    5. The movement itself corrects these claims.

    Organisations and animal advocates do this work, and this guide keeps a list of claims to avoid, including several about nutrition and biology. A movement that criticises its own failings isn't the same as one that relies on them.

    6. Criticism, when specific, is useful.

    Pointing to a concrete claim as false helps everyone. The argument only fails when it generalises: "you support pseudoscience, so nothing you say counts".

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024)There is strong scientific support for attributing conscious experience to mammals and birds, and at least a realistic possibility of it in all vertebrates and many invertebratesSigned by hundreds of researchers

  4. d

    The best reply: the movement tolerates error when it suits it

    The serious version doesn't say every vegan believes pseudoscience. It says the movement as a whole is lenient with exaggerated claims when they help the cause, and that this leniency undermines trust in the rest.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It asks for rigour, and rigour is welcome.

    It is exactly what this guide tries to do: drop what doesn't hold up.

    It doesn't reach the foundation.

    Even a lenient movement can be right about the main point, and the main point here is well supported.

    It applies to any movement.

    Every cause has advocates who exaggerate, and nobody concludes from this that all causes are wrong.

Contradictions

It doesn't dismiss medicine because of quacks.

Nor environmentalism because of exaggerations.

It accepts pseudosciences on its own side.

Such as the all-meat diet as the healthiest.

It demands rigour from some and not others.

The burden of proof falls only on those who defend animals.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets something right, and it must be acknowledged: some vegans spread pseudoscience, and that harms the cause, the health of those who believe it and public trust. Some of these claims appear in popular documentaries and on social media, and they're worth correcting every time. That's why this guide keeps a list of claims not to use (see Stop saying this).

But

But the case for animals doesn't depend on those claims. It rests on what science describes well, that animals feel, and the adequacy of a planned vegan diet is the position of the major nutrition bodies. Judging an idea by its worst advocates is a standard no cause would survive, including that of meat eaters.
