J5

"Meat tastes good"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the pleasure of taste is real, but it's a small interest set against the greatest interest of another being: to stay alive and not suffer. In no other context do we accept that someone's taste justifies killing. And the pleasure of eating doesn't depend on the animal: taste is largely learned, and much of what makes meat tasty comes from preparation, salt, fat and seasoning.

Root chapter: one of the three roots where conversations end, along with A8 and E15

Note: although it sits in Part IND, this is one of the frankest arguments in the catalog. It claims neither necessity nor impossibility: it admits the reason is pleasure.

Short answer

No one doubts that meat tastes good to people who like it. The question is whether that pleasure justifies someone's death. On one side is the taste of a meal; on the other, an animal's whole life. In no other context do we accept that someone's taste justifies killing: no one would defend eating dogs because their meat is tasty. And the pleasure doesn't depend on the animal: taste adapts, and seasoning, salt and fat are in plant food too.

For children

Imagine you love a toy, but to play with it someone has to get hurt. Would it be right to play anyway, just because it's fun? Most people would say no. Liking something is good, but it doesn't let us hurt others. And we can discover tasty new foods that don't hurt anyone.

Ask a question back

"If dog meat were the tastiest in the world, would that make eating it acceptable?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I know, but meat is just too good. I can't live without a barbecue."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Pleasure is a real good, and a good life includes pleasures, among them those of food.
  2. P2Eating meat gives a pleasure that, for many people, no substitute offers in the same way.
  3. P3Giving up an important pleasure is a legitimate cost, which should go on the moral scales.
  4. CSo the pleasure of eating meat is a valid reason to keep eating it.

P1 and P3 are true: no one needs to give up pleasures for no reason, and the cost of changing exists. The problem lies in what is put on the other side of the scales.

The argument is valid only in the form "pleasure is a reason". It fails when the conclusion is "pleasure is a sufficient reason", because the scales have two sides and the other side is someone's whole life.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument is honest, and that's a virtue: it doesn't pretend there's a necessity. But it lays out clearly what's on the scales.

    Side of the scalesWhat weighs
    Whoever eatsThe difference in pleasure between a meal with meat and a meal without
    The animalTheir whole life, freedom from suffering and all the experiences they'd still have

    The principle of equal consideration of interests, formulated by Peter Singer, says that similar interests should carry similar weight, and that a lesser interest shouldn't prevail over a greater one just because it belongs to someone of our species. Here, a trivial interest on one side is set against a vital interest on the other.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Pleasure doesn't justify harm in any other context.

    No one accepts "I did it because I enjoy it" as a justification for hurting a dog, for cockfighting or for any harm to humans. The criterion is only accepted when the victim is a farmed animal.

    2. The disproportion is enormous.

    The difference in pleasure between one meal and another is small and fleeting. On the other side are an animal's whole life and, in practice, the suffering of an animal raised on an industrial scale. Even those who assign animals less value would struggle to make that calculation add up.

    3. Taste is learned.

    Food preferences form through experience and repeated exposure, especially in childhood, and they change over time. Many people who stop eating meat report that the craving fades. The pleasure that seems irreplaceable is largely a habit.

    4. The pleasure doesn't come only from the animal.

    Much of the flavor associated with meat comes from salt, fat, smoking, caramelization and seasoning, all available in plant dishes. There are also alternatives that imitate texture and flavor, and the plant cooking of many cultures is old and rich (J6).

    5. The argument concedes the moral point.

    People who say "it tastes good" rarely say "there's nothing wrong with it". By appealing to pleasure, they admit there's no other reason. That's why J5 is one of the roots of the connections map: it's where the conversation arrives when the other justifications fall.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: small pleasures add up to a good life

    The most serious version doesn't say the pleasure of a meal is worth more than a life. It says the pleasures of food are an important part of a good life, tied to affection, culture and company, and that asking someone to give them up is asking a lot. Besides, one person's purchase doesn't directly kill a specific animal (K1, K2), so the scales wouldn't be a life against a meal.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Company doesn't depend on the animal.

    Affection, culture and gatherings carry on with a different menu (D3). What's lost is an ingredient, not social life.

    Added up, the animals add up too.

    If meals accumulate over a life, so do the animals eaten: one person eats hundreds or thousands of animals over a lifetime.

    The causal objection has an answer.

    Even without directly killing a specific animal, each purchase has an expected effect on how many animals are raised and killed, as chapter K1 shows.

    "Asking a lot" speaks to difficulty, not justice.

    Difficulty may explain why someone hasn't changed yet. It doesn't make what's done to the animal fair.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    This is one of the three points where conversations usually end, because it admits the essential: what's left is pleasure. The moral question becomes sharp: does the pleasure of a flavor, which can largely be recreated with plants, outweigh the whole life of an animal who feels?

Contradictions

Doesn't accept pleasure as a justification for cruelty.

The same person would condemn someone who hurts an animal "because they enjoy it".

Wouldn't eat dog even if it were tasty.

Flavor stops mattering when the species changes.

Has changed tastes before.

Almost everyone has come to like foods they disliked as children, and stopped liking others.

Gives up pleasures for lesser reasons.

Diets and restrictions for health or appearance show pleasure isn't untouchable.

Admits the problem by appealing to pleasure.

If nothing were wrong, there'd be no need to say "but it tastes good".

What the argument gets right

What is true

The argument has an honesty many others lack: it doesn't invent a necessity. And there's truth in it. The pleasure of food matters, is part of a good life and is tied to memories, culture and affection. Changing one's diet has real costs, and no one should pretend it's easy for everyone.

But

But precisely because it's frank, the argument shows where the question lies. If the only reason is pleasure, the question is whether the pleasure of a meal is worth someone's life. Almost everyone says no in every other case, and pleasure, unlike life, can be found on another plate.
