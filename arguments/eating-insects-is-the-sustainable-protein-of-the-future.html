H17

"Eating insects is the sustainable protein of the future"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the environment and agriculture, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on environmental grounds, low-impact exploitation would be acceptable, even if it killed more sentient beings. And it is already being promoted: insect farming, "sustainable" hunting, "sustainable" leather. It is possible to reduce environmental impact and increase exploitation at the same time.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

farming insects may emit less and use less land than livestock farming, but it trades an environmental problem for an unprecedented number of victims. Because they're small, trillions of insects are needed to produce what a few large animals provide, and there is growing evidence that many insects are sentient. There are plant proteins just as efficient, with no one to suffer.

Short answer

Sustainable for whom? For the insects, it means trillions of deaths a year, counting those farmed for food and feed. And there's more and more evidence that many of them feel. If we want protein with little land and few emissions, beans, soy, lentils and chickpeas already do that with no one to suffer. Reducing environmental impact doesn't justify increasing the number of victims.

For children

A cricket is really small. To make a cricket burger, you need lots and lots of crickets. Scientists are finding out that insects learn, remember things and might feel pain. So if we swap cows for crickets, we may not be helping animals: we might be making many more animals suffer. And beans, lentils and chickpeas have lots of protein too.

Ask a question back

"If there's a protein as sustainable as cricket protein with no victims at all, why choose the cricket?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Cricket flour is the future. Lots of protein, little land, almost no emissions. And insects don't feel anything."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Livestock farming is one of the main causes of emissions, deforestation and water use.
  2. P2Insects convert feed into protein much more efficiently than cattle or pigs, with fewer emissions and less land.
  3. P3Insects probably don't feel pain, or feel very little.
  4. CSo replacing meat with insects is a sustainable and ethically acceptable solution.

P1 is true, and P2 has some support: in 2013 the FAO highlighted the feed efficiency of edible insects.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, which runs against growing evidence, and the conclusion ignores plant alternatives that meet P2 without victims.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    PremiseWhat it claimsStatus
    P1Livestock farming degrades the environmentTrue
    P2Insects are more efficient than cattlePartly: depends on the feed and the species
    P3Insects don't feelContrary to growing evidence
    Alternative ignoredPlant proteinsAs efficient or more, without victims
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. There is evidence of sentience in insects.

    Insects show flexible behavior, learning and integration of information. They have centralized brains with specialized structures, the mushroom bodies, with hundreds of thousands of neurons. That's enough, at the very least, to give them the benefit of the doubt, as we already do with octopuses and crustaceans (A2).

    2. The number of victims explodes.

    According to estimates cited in A Journey Through Animal Ethics, between 2 and 3.2 trillion insects farmed for food and feed die each year, plus trillions of cochineal insects for dye and hundreds of billions of silkworms. Since each insect is tiny, substitution multiplies the victims.

    3. The killing isn't gentle.

    The usual methods include freezing, immersion in water above 80 °C, microwaves and shredding, after 12 to 24 hours without food.

    4. A smaller brain doesn't guarantee less suffering.

    In animals with fewer cognitive resources, pain may be the main driver of behavior, and would therefore need to be intense (A6).

    5. The plant alternative already exists.

    Legumes meet the environmental goal without breeding anyone to die.

    6. Most insect farming is for feed, and the scale is enormous.

    According to Rethink Priorities, between 200 and 300 billion black soldier flies are already farmed each year, mainly as feed for farmed animals and pets, and projections for 2030 reach trillions of larvae. So insect farming often doesn't replace meat: it feeds the farming of other animals, and adds victims.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: even if they feel, they count far less

    The most serious version grants that insects may feel, but says each one counts far less than a cow or a pig, because its mental life is much simpler. So swapping one cow for thousands of crickets would still be a moral gain.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Counting less doesn't license exploitation.

    Even with a lower weight, another argument would be needed to justify breeding and killing.

    Scale cancels the discount.

    If a human counted a thousand times more than an insect, a thousand insects would even it out, and the real numbers are in the trillions.

    The right comparison isn't with the cow.

    It's with beans. Against an alternative without victims, any number of victims loses.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    This chapter shows the risk of defending veganism on environmental grounds: insect farming can be great for the climate and terrible for animals. The question that matters isn't how much land a protein uses, but whether it's fair to breed sentient beings to die when we don't need to.

Contradictions

Says they want to reduce harm.

And multiplies the number of animals killed.

Accepts that octopuses and crabs feel.

On the basis of evidence similar to what exists for insects.

Champions efficiency.

And ignores legumes, which are more efficient than any farming of animals.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Livestock farming causes enormous environmental harm, and looking for alternatives is necessary. It's also true that there is real uncertainty about the sentience of many insect species.

But

But uncertainty calls for caution, not license. And the alternative without victims is already on many people's plates.
