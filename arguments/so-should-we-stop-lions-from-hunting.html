F3

"So should we stop lions from hunting?"

The answer in one sentence

the question is asked as a provocation, but it touches a serious issue: the suffering of wild animals. Taking that issue seriously doesn't lead to "locking up predators", but to studying carefully how to reduce suffering without causing greater harm. And whatever the answer, it doesn't favor eating meat: that there is suffering in nature doesn't authorize causing more.

Short answer

The suffering of wild animals is a serious issue, and researchers are studying ways to reduce it, such as vaccination and rescue during disasters. No one needs to "lock up predators": poorly thought-out interventions can cause more harm. But whatever the answer, it doesn't favor eating meat. That there's suffering in nature is no reason to cause more. If there's a lot of suffering we don't control, that's all the more reason not to add the suffering we do control.

For children

In nature, many animals suffer: from hunger, cold, disease and predators. That's sad, and some people study how to help, for example by vaccinating sick wild animals. But the fact that there's suffering in nature doesn't mean we can cause even more suffering.

Ask a question back

"If suffering in nature is a problem, isn't that one more reason not to cause extra suffering?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Will you lock up every predator? So nature is immoral?"

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1If animal suffering matters, it also matters when caused by predators.
  2. P2It would be absurd to intervene in nature to prevent predation.
  3. P3So whoever takes animal suffering seriously reaches an absurd conclusion.
  4. CSo the premise that animal suffering matters must be rejected or weakened.

P1 is true. The problem lies in P2 and P3: recognizing the problem doesn't commit anyone to absurd solutions, and the conclusion doesn't apply to what we do to animals.

The argument is valid as a reductio ad absurdum. Its soundness fails at P2, which confuses "taking seriously" with "intervening in any way".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument mixes up two questions:

    QuestionAnswer
    Should we stop causing animals suffering?Yes, whenever we can avoid it
    Should we intervene to reduce suffering caused by others, including in nature?Perhaps, carefully, when there are safe and effective ways

    The duty not to cause harm is stronger and simpler than the duty to help. The difficulty of the second question doesn't affect the first.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Not causing harm comes before helping.

    Even those who don't think we should intervene in nature accept that we shouldn't cause unnecessary suffering.

    2. Predators aren't moral agents.

    The lion can't be held responsible (C2). The question isn't punishing predators, but whether we, as agents, can help the victims.

    3. The issue is serious, and already studied.

    The field that studies wild animal suffering analyzes interventions such as vaccination against disease, rescue during disasters and fertility control, always weighing the ecological risks.

    4. Poorly thought-out interventions can make everything worse.

    Removing predators from an ecosystem can cause overpopulation, starvation and more suffering. That's why taking the problem seriously demands caution, not absurd solutions.

    5. The question doesn't favor meat.

    Suffering in nature, whatever the answer, doesn't make suffering on farms acceptable.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: if we don't intervene, animal suffering weighs little

    The most serious version says that if we aren't willing to intervene in nature, it's because deep down we don't give much weight to animal suffering, and then eating meat wouldn't be serious either.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Not intervening doesn't mean giving no weight.

    We often don't intervene because we don't know how to help without causing more harm.

    The asymmetry between causing and allowing also exists among humans.

    Not saving everyone who suffers in the world doesn't make it acceptable to cause someone suffering.

    Many animal advocates do support intervening.

    The issue is far from ignored.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Straw man
Attributes to vegans a proposal to lock up predators
False reductio ad absurdum
Assumes taking the problem seriously leads to absurd solutions

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Confusing causing with allowing
Treats the duty not to cause harm as a duty to prevent all harm

Contradictions

Helps wild animals in other situations.

Many people support disaster rescues and care for injured animals.

Doesn't think human suffering in nature is irrelevant.

Natural disasters move people.

Uses nature as an argument only when convenient.

No one draws rules for human life from it on other subjects.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The provocation gets one thing right: if animal suffering matters, it matters in nature too. That's a conclusion many philosophers take seriously, and it goes beyond veganism. It's also true that intervening in nature is hard and risky.

But

But recognizing this problem doesn't weaken the argument against causing suffering. On the contrary: in a world with so much suffering we don't yet know how to reduce, the least we can do is not add what we can avoid.
