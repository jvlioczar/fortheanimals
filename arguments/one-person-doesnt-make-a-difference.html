K1

"One person doesn't make a difference"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about effectiveness and individual responsibility, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended only by each person's impact, anyone who proved their purchase changes nothing would be let off. But no one accepts this for other harms: taking part in an injustice is still wrong even when each person's share is small.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

one person's purchase rarely decides on its own whether one more animal will be raised, but sometimes it does, and when it does it affects many at once. By expected-value reasoning, every purchase has a real average effect. Economists confirm the direction: less demand leads to less production. And even if the effect were zero, taking part in an unjust practice would still be a moral matter.

Note: this chapter also answers the technical version of the argument, "supply and demand don't work like that with meat", which was merged into it.

Short answer

It's true that the slaughterhouse doesn't change production because of one tray. But it does change because of many, and someone's purchase is always the one that triggers the next order. Since no one knows if it'll be theirs, the average effect of each purchase is real: a small chance of affecting many animals. Economists confirm that when demand falls, production falls, though less than proportionally. And even if it were zero, paying for something you consider unjust would still be taking part in it.

For children

Imagine a line to get into school, and the door only opens when ten children arrive. If you arrive and you're the tenth, the door opens because of you. You don't know if you'll be the tenth, but sometimes you will be. Shopping is similar: the store only orders more meat once it sells a certain amount, and sometimes it's your purchase that triggers the order. Every choice counts.

Ask a question back

"If no one makes a difference alone, how does the demand of billions of people sustain production?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I alone won't change anything. If I stop eating meat, the slaughterhouse will kill the same number anyway."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1An action is only obligatory if it makes a difference to the outcome.
  2. P2Meat production responds to orders in large batches, not to individual purchases.
  3. P3The chance that my purchase changes a batch is negligible.
  4. CSo my purchase makes no difference, and I have no obligation to change.

P2 is true: slaughterhouses and supermarkets don't adjust production for every tray sold. The problem lies in P3 and P1.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, which confuses a small chance with a small effect, and at P1, which reduces morality to individual consequences.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument has a correct starting point and a wrong conclusion. Production works by thresholds: the market only adjusts its orders when sales change by a certain amount.

    SituationWhat happens
    Your purchase doesn't change the batchZero effect, which is the most common case
    Your purchase is the one that changes the batchA large effect: a whole batch of animals
    Average across the casesThe small chance multiplied by the large effect gives, roughly, an effect proportional to the purchase

    This is expected-value reasoning, set out by the philosopher Shelly Kagan in the paper Do I Make a Difference? (2011), which uses precisely the example of buying chicken. If the threshold is a thousand chickens, the chance your purchase is decisive is about one in a thousand, but when it is, it affects about a thousand chickens. The expected effect comes close to one chicken per chicken bought.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. A small chance isn't a small effect.

    P3 looks only at the chance and forgets the size of the effect when it happens. Multiplied together, they give a real average effect, as in the threshold example.

    2. Aggregate demand is made of individuals.

    If no purchase made a difference, the sum of all purchases wouldn't either, and production wouldn't respond to demand. But it does: that's how markets work, and it's why the industry invests in advertising.

    3. Economics confirms the direction.

    The effect of reduced demand on production is less than one-to-one, because the price falls a little and some consumption is picked up by other buyers. But the effect is positive: buying less leads to producing less. Economic estimates cited in the debate, such as those by Norwood and Lusk, fall below one animal fewer per animal not bought, and above zero.

    4. The effect adds up over a lifetime.

    A person eats thousands of meals. Even with an expected effect below one, the total over the years amounts to many animals.

    5. There are effects beyond the purchase.

    Choices are seen by others, influence the supply of plant options and change what seems normal. The impact of a worldview extends beyond the life of whoever adopts it.

    6. Morality isn't reduced to causal effect.

    Even if the purchase changed nothing, paying for a practice you consider unjust is taking part in it. No one would accept buying a product made with slave labor on the grounds that the factory would produce it anyway.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Kagan, *Do I Make a Difference?* (2011)In threshold cases, contributing has positive expected value; the central example is buying chickenPhilosophy & Public Affairs, 39(2); author's page

    Nefsky, Consequentialism and the Problem of Collective Harm (2011)A critique of expected-value reasoning in cases of collective harmShows the philosophical debate exists; used in (d)
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: not every case has a threshold

    The most serious version, defended by philosophers such as Julia Nefsky, accepts expected value where there are clear thresholds, but doubts the meat supply chain works that way. Waste, stocks, exports and long-term contracts can absorb small variations, so many purchases may affect nothing at all. In that case, the probability of making a difference could be even smaller than the threshold reasoning assumes.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It doesn't show the effect is zero.

    It shows it's uncertain. If aggregate demand moves production, and it does, the average contribution of each purchase can't be nil.

    Economists look at the aggregate.

    Estimates of how production responds to demand already include stocks, waste and exports.

    Nefsky herself doesn't conclude one may buy freely.

    Her critique is of how the duty is justified, not of the duty itself: she argues that contributing to a bad outcome can be wrong even without making a difference.

    The moral argument doesn't depend on the causal one.

    Even with zero effect, paying for an unjust practice is still taking part in it, as in the slave labor case.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument doesn't say animals don't matter; it says one person doesn't change the outcome. But each purchase has a real expected effect, and taking part in an unjust practice counts in itself. The moral question remains: if it's wrong to make animals suffer and die without need, why pay for it to happen?

Contradictions

Votes in elections.

The chance of one vote deciding an election is tiny, and the same person considers voting important.

Recycles, saves water or donates.

We do many things whose individual effect is small, because the combined effect matters.

Wouldn't buy a product of slave labor.

Even knowing the factory would produce it anyway.

Believes demand moves the market in everything else.

The same person understands that a product disappears from the shelves when no one buys it.

Says it makes no difference, but resists changing.

If the choice really were irrelevant, changing would make no difference to the chooser either.

What the argument gets right

What is true

There's truth in the argument. The meat supply chain doesn't respond to each purchase in isolation, and one person's effect is small next to an industry of billions of animals. It's also true that system changes, such as laws, technology and public policy, have a reach no individual choice can match, and deserve as much attention as each person's plate (K6).

But

But small isn't zero, and individual isn't irrelevant. The sum of choices is exactly what sustains the industry, and whoever takes part in it has a share, however small, in what it produces. Changing the system and changing one's own consumption don't compete: one usually feeds the other.
