M1

"Wild animals live free and happy"

The answer in one sentence

the wild animals we see are the survivors. In most species, each female produces hundreds, thousands or even millions of offspring over her life, and in a stable population only a few reach adulthood. All the others die young, of hunger, cold, disease, parasites or predation. A nature in which the rule is to be born and die within a few days is not a paradise for those who live in it.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted.

Short answer

The animals we see are the ones that survived. In most species, each mother has a huge number of young, and almost all of them die of hunger, cold, disease or predation soon after birth. If this happened to human children, we'd call it a catastrophe, not freedom. That doesn't make exploitation any less wrong: it only shows that leaving animals alone isn't enough for them to live well.

For children

A sea turtle lays lots of eggs on the beach. Of the babies that hatch, almost all are eaten by birds, fish and crabs before they grow up. Only a few become big turtles, and those are the ones we see in videos. Nature is beautiful, but life for the animals living in it is often very hard.

Ask a question back

"If almost all the babies in a human population died of hunger in their first days, would you say they live free and happy?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"A free animal is a happy animal. In nature they're free; no one is exploiting them."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1What harms animals most is human action: exploitation, hunting and habitat destruction.
  2. P2In nature, away from humans, animals live according to their own way of life.
  3. P3Living according to one's own way of life is, in general, living well.
  4. CSo wild animals live well, and the only moral task is to leave them alone.

P1 is largely true for exploited animals. The problem lies in P3, which describes an idealized nature, and in the conclusion, which looks only at the visible adults.

The argument is valid, but not sound: P3 is refuted by the population dynamics of most species.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument looks at a biased sample: the adults we manage to see.

    Who we seeWho we don't see
    Healthy adults, filmed in documentariesThe young, eggs and larvae that died before growing up
    A small fraction of each generationThe vast majority of each generation
    Large animals, such as mammals and birdsFish, amphibians, insects and other invertebrates, which are the overwhelming majority

    It's a classic case of survivorship bias: drawing conclusions about a group by looking only at those who made it through the filter.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The population arithmetic refutes the idyllic view.

    In a stable population, each animal is, on average, replaced by a single offspring that goes on to reproduce. If a female produces hundreds or thousands of young over her life, almost all of them must die before reproducing. This is arithmetic, not opinion. It's the so-called r-selected reproductive strategy, the most common one in nature.

    2. Those who die young usually die badly.

    Hunger, dehydration, cold, parasites, disease and predation aren't quick and painless deaths. If these animals are sentient, as many of them seem to be, their short lives are mostly deprivation.

    3. The numbers are immense.

    Estimates suggest hundreds of billions of wild birds and mammals, trillions of fish, and somewhere between a hundred quadrillion and ten quintillion terrestrial arthropods. Even with great uncertainty about the sentience of the smallest, the scale is many times greater than that of animal farming.

    4. The human test.

    If a human population lived without medical care, with most children dying of hunger or infection in their first days, no one would call it freedom. We'd call it a humanitarian crisis and send help. The only thing that changes in the case of animals is the victims' species.

    5. Freedom isn't the same as wellbeing.

    Being free from humans doesn't mean being free from hunger, disease or fear. The word "free" hides the question of how life goes for the one living it.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

    Tomasik, "How many wild animals are there?"Mammals: 10¹¹ to 10¹²; birds: about 1 to 4 × 10¹¹; fish: 10¹³ to 10¹⁵ or more; terrestrial arthropods: 10¹⁷ to 10¹⁹Estimates with wide margins

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: we don't know these lives are bad

    The most serious version says: we have no way to measure the wellbeing of a baby fish or an insect. Perhaps their short lives have good moments that make up for the end. Concluding that nature is dominated by suffering would be as speculative as concluding it's a paradise.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Uncertainty doesn't favor the idyllic view.

    If we don't know, we can't claim animals "live free and happy". The counter-reply brings down the original argument, not the concern.

    Some deaths we know are bad.

    Dying of hunger, of cold, or eaten alive by parasites isn't an ambiguous experience for whoever feels it.

    Uncertainty calls for research.

    That's exactly what welfare biology proposes: studying the wellbeing of wild animals before concluding or acting (M5).

Contradictions

Doesn't idealize human life without medicine.

No one thinks past infant mortality was a sign of freedom.

Takes their own animal to the vet.

Recognizes that disease and parasites do harm, even though they're natural.

Is moved by rescues of wild animals.

And that presupposes the animal was in danger, not "free and happy".

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that human exploitation is an enormous and avoidable source of suffering, and that for exploited animals the first step is to stop harming them. It's also right that nature has beauty and that many animals have moments of wellbeing.

But

But the beauty of the landscape isn't the same as the wellbeing of those who live in it. For most animals born in nature, life is short and hard, and that matters if what counts is what they feel.
