N1

"Wild animals live free and happy"

The answer in one sentence

the wild animals we see are the survivors. In most species, each female produces hundreds, thousands or even millions of offspring over her life, and in a stable population only a few reach adulthood. All the others die young, of hunger, cold, disease, parasites or predation. A nature in which the rule is to be born and die within a few days is not a paradise for those who live in it.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Reply

The animals you see are the ones that survived. In most species, each mother has a huge number of young, and almost all of them die of hunger, cold, disease or predation soon after birth. If this happened to human children, would you call it freedom or a catastrophe? That doesn't make exploitation any less wrong: it only shows that leaving animals alone isn't enough for them to live well.

For children

A sea turtle lays lots of eggs on the beach. Of the babies that hatch, almost all are eaten by birds, fish and crabs before they grow up. Only a few become big turtles, and those are the ones we see in videos. Nature is beautiful, but life for the animals living in it is often very hard.

Ask a question back

"If almost all the babies in a human population died of hunger in their first days, would you say they live free and happy?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"A free animal is a happy animal. In nature they're free; no one is exploiting them."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1What harms animals most is human action: exploitation, hunting and habitat destruction.
  2. P2In nature, away from humans, animals live according to their own way of life.
  3. P3Living according to one's own way of life is, in general, living well.
  4. CSo wild animals live well, and the only moral task is to leave them alone.

P1 is largely true for exploited animals. The problem lies in P3, which describes an idealized nature, and in the conclusion, which looks only at the visible adults.

The argument is valid, but not sound: P3 is refuted by the population dynamics of most species.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument looks at a biased sample: the adults we manage to see.

    Who we seeWho we don't see
    Healthy adults, filmed in documentariesThe young, eggs and larvae that died before growing up
    A small fraction of each generationThe vast majority of each generation
    Large animals, such as mammals and birdsFish, amphibians, insects and other invertebrates, which are the overwhelming majority

    It's a classic case of survivorship bias: drawing conclusions about a group by looking only at those who made it through the filter.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The population arithmetic refutes the idyllic view.

    In a stable population, each animal is, on average, replaced by a single offspring that goes on to reproduce. If a female produces hundreds or thousands of young over her life, almost all of them must die before reproducing. This is arithmetic, not opinion. It's the so-called r-selected reproductive strategy, the most common one in nature.

    2. Those who die young usually die badly.

    Hunger, dehydration, cold, parasites, disease and predation aren't quick and painless deaths. If these animals are sentient, as many of them seem to be, their short lives are mostly deprivation.

    3. The numbers are immense.

    Estimates suggest hundreds of billions of wild birds and mammals, trillions of fish, and somewhere between a hundred quadrillion and ten quintillion terrestrial arthropods. Even with great uncertainty about the sentience of the smallest, the scale is many times greater than that of animal farming.

    4. The human test.

    If a human population lived without medical care, with most children dying of hunger or infection in their first days, no one would call it freedom. We'd call it a humanitarian crisis and send help. The only thing that changes in the case of animals is the victims' species.

    5. Freedom isn't the same as wellbeing.

    Being free from humans doesn't mean being free from hunger, disease or fear. The word "free" hides the question of how life goes for the one living it.

    6. The idyllic view is the default view, and it comes from skewed images.

    Most people picture nature with large, charismatic animals, mostly mammals and birds, which are not representative of wild animals. Animal Ethics cites a survey of National Geographic covers over 55 years: mammals were more than 60% of the animals featured, although they are a tiny fraction of the animals that exist, and invertebrates, who are the vast majority, appeared on fewer than 6% of covers. The ones who appear most are the least representative. Documentaries that show the hard side of wild life, with hunger, disease, predation and everyday threats, help correct this picture: see The other face of nature (in Spanish), from Animal Ethics, and the trailer for Nightmares of Nature, from Netflix.

    7. "They can fend for themselves" applies, at most, to adults.

    The idea that wild animals are independent and need no one pictures large adults. But most victims are newborns, who have no independence at all. And even a capable adult human needs help if they get lost in the forest or fall ill. If dependence counts for anything, it gives more reasons to help, not fewer (Cunha, 2022, p. 124).

    8. Freedom is valued for the life it allows.

    Few people would trade a life with shelter, food and health, even with less space, for a "free" life in a desert, hungry and sick. Freedom matters because it usually makes life better, not as a value that beats all others. And many kinds of help take away no freedom at all: water in a drought, food, vaccines in baits spread in the environment, like those used against rabies in foxes in Europe (Cunha, 2022, pp. 144–146).

    9. "Nature gives everyone the weapons to escape" doesn't hold up.

    Even if predator and prey had balanced abilities, equal abilities don't guarantee equal luck: whoever is born in a drought year or near a hungry predator loses, however fast they are. And predators don't choose at random: they prefer the young, the old, the sick and the injured, exactly those least able to escape (Cunha, 2022, pp. 136–137; Faria, 2016).

    10. The numbers of young are hard to imagine.

    A female ocean sunfish can lay up to 300 million eggs at once; in a stable population, on average only about two of them reach adulthood (Cunha, 2022, p. 155, based on FishBase). Horta (2010) and Cunha (2022, pp. 31–32) also use the example of cod in the Gulf of Maine: using stock data from 2007, they estimate about 2 trillion eggs in a single breeding season, with almost all of them dying before adulthood. The stock has fallen sharply since then, and the number only illustrates the scale.

    11. The balance of wellbeing in nature is debated, and the reason to help doesn't depend on it.

    Ng (1995) argued that suffering probably outweighs wellbeing in nature. In 2019, Ng himself, with Zach Groff, corrected an error in the 1995 model and concluded that it leaves open whether suffering or enjoyment predominates. Browning and Veit (2023) go further and argue that many wild animals may have net-positive lives, partly because death by predation can be quick and dulled by shock. Cunha (2022, pp. 131–134) replies that, even if the overall balance were positive, there would still be an astronomical number of short, bad lives, just as hunger in poor countries weighs no less because rich countries exist. Several theories keep the reason to help in that case: those that give priority to the worst off (prioritarianism, egalitarianism, sufficientarianism), those that give more weight to preventing suffering than to promoting happiness, and even classical utilitarianism, because the same resource usually yields more wellbeing when it goes to those who are very badly off.

    12. Do tiny young feel anything? This is the most uncertain point.

    Groff and Ng and Browning and Veit also suggest that the young and larvae of species with huge broods would have less intense experiences, or not be sentient at all. Cunha (2022, pp. 32–34) replies in three steps. Many of these young are precocial, that is, they are born already fending for themselves in the environment, which makes some sentience early on likely. The zebrafish larva, with about a hundred thousand neurons, reacts to noxious stimuli much like the adult, which has about ten million, and the reaction decreases with painkillers (Lopez-Luna et al., 2017). And a simpler mind doesn't necessarily mean weaker pain: without other forms of motivation, pain may be what weighs most. Still, this is the most open empirical question in the debate, and the honest answer is that we don't know how much these young feel.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: we don't know these lives are bad

    The most serious version says: we have no way to measure the wellbeing of a baby fish or an insect. Perhaps their short lives have good moments that make up for the end. Concluding that nature is dominated by suffering would be as speculative as concluding it's a paradise.

    The counter-reply has academic support. Groff and Ng (2019) showed that Ng's (1995) model, widely cited as proof that suffering predominates, doesn't support that conclusion, and Browning and Veit (2023) argue that the balance may be positive (points 11 and 12).

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Uncertainty doesn't favor the idyllic view.

    If we don't know, we can't claim animals "live free and happy". The counter-reply brings down the original argument, not the concern.

    Some deaths we know are bad.

    Dying of hunger, of cold, or eaten alive by parasites isn't an ambiguous experience for whoever feels it.

    Uncertainty calls for research.

    That's exactly what welfare biology proposes: studying the wellbeing of wild animals before concluding or acting (N9).

    A positive balance wouldn't erase the bad lives.

    Even if the sum of everything came out positive, the billions of young who are born and starve would still exist, and those worst off would still have the strongest claim to help (point 11).

Contradictions

Doesn't idealize human life without medicine.

No one thinks past infant mortality was a sign of freedom.

Takes their own animal to the vet.

Recognizes that disease and parasites do harm, even though they're natural.

Is moved by rescues of wild animals.

And that presupposes the animal was in danger, not "free and happy".

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that human exploitation is an enormous and avoidable source of suffering, and that for exploited animals the first step is to stop harming them. It's also right that nature has beauty and that many animals have moments of wellbeing.

But

But the beauty of the landscape isn't the same as the wellbeing of those who live in it. For most animals born in nature, life is short and hard, and that matters if what counts is what they feel.
