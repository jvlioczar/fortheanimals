J19

"What would we do with the animals if everyone went vegan?"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

no one goes vegan all at once across the whole world. Change is gradual, and with less demand, fewer animals are bred. Farmed animals exist in enormous numbers because they're bred for this (C12). Those who already exist could live in sanctuaries or until the natural end of their lives. The problem isn't what to do with them; it's that we keep bringing them into the world to die.

Short answer

No one goes vegan all at once across the whole world: change is gradual. With less demand, farmers breed fewer animals, because breeding is controlled through insemination and hatcheries. The animals that exist can live out their lives, some in sanctuaries. There wouldn't be cows loose in the streets; there'd be fewer cows being born to die.

For children

People won't all stop eating meat on the same day. It happens little by little. When fewer people buy meat, farms breed fewer animals. The animals that already exist can live until they're old, some in sanctuaries, which are like homes for rescued animals.

Ask a question back

"If hatcheries incubate eggs according to demand, what happens when demand falls?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Release the cows into the streets? They'd have to kill them all at once."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1There are billions of farmed animals in the world.
  2. P2Many wouldn't survive if released into the wild.
  3. P3If everyone stopped eating meat, there'd be nothing to do with them.
  4. CSo veganism is impractical or would lead to a catastrophe for the animals themselves.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3: change would be gradual, and the animals' breeding is controlled by the farmers.

The argument is valid for an instantaneous change. Its soundness fails because that change doesn't happen.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument imagines a scenario that doesn't happen:

    How the argument imagines itHow change happens
    Everyone stops eating meat at onceConsumption falls gradually
    Billions of animals are left with nowhere to goFewer animals are bred each year
    The animals would have to be released or killedExisting ones live out their lives; new ones stop being born
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Change is gradual.

    Meat consumption shifts over years and generations.

    2. Breeding is controlled.

    Chickens hatch in hatcheries and cattle and pigs are born through insemination, in numbers set by demand (C12).

    3. The existing animals can live.

    In sanctuaries, on small holdings or to the end of their cycle, while breeding declines.

    4. The question reverses the responsibility.

    The problem isn't what to do with the animals if we stop eating them; it's that we keep breeding billions of them to die.

    5. Selectively bred breeds may decline.

    Many production breeds have health problems caused by breeding (B7). Breeding fewer of them is no loss to the animals.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Bar-On, Phillips and Milo, 2018, *PNAS*Farmed animals make up about 60% of mammal biomassShows that the number of animals results from production; also used in C12
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: some breeds would disappear

    The most serious version points out that, with the end of farming, many domestic breeds could disappear, and that this would be a loss of diversity.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Breeds aren't individuals.

    What matters morally is the animals who feel, not the continuity of a breed (A10).

    Some could continue in sanctuaries.

    Without being bred to die.

    Many breeds suffer because of selection.

    Chickens who can barely walk lose nothing by not being born.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question about the animals' fate is legitimate, but the answer is simple: stop breeding them for slaughter. The moral problem isn't what to do with them afterwards; it's continuing to bring them into the world to die.

Reasoning errors

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Unrealistic scenario
Imagines an instantaneous change

Logical

Is the form valid?

Reversing responsibility
Treats a consequence of farming as an argument for continuing to farm

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Species in place of the individual
Worries about the breed, not about who is born to die

Biases

Motivated reasoning

An imaginary scenario excuses real change

General literature on motivated cognition

Status quo bias

Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988

Contradictions

Knows production follows demand.

For any other product.

Doesn't worry about the animals' fate today.

Which is slaughter.

Doesn't think we must preserve other breeds at any cost.

Many domestic breeds have already disappeared.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The question about the fate of existing animals is legitimate, and a responsible transition needs to answer it, with sanctuaries, public policy and care for the animals already alive.

But

But the scenario of billions of animals set loose doesn't happen, because change is gradual and breeding is controlled. The best way to care for farmed animals is to stop breeding them to die.
