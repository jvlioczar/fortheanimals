I30

"The bird was born in a cage; it wouldn't survive if released"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's true that a bird raised in a cage may not survive if suddenly released, so anyone who already has one should keep caring for it, with as much space and enrichment as possible. But that doesn't justify buying or breeding others: each new bird is born to live in a cage. And the bird and exotic pet market feeds trafficking: a 2001 estimate by Brazil's anti-trafficking network RENCTAS put the number of wild animals taken from nature in Brazil at about 38 million a year, with only 1 in 10 reaching the buyer alive.

Short answer

It's true that a bird raised in a cage may not survive if suddenly released; anyone who already has one should keep caring for it, with as much space as possible. But that doesn't justify buying or breeding others: each new bird is born to live caged. And the bird market feeds trafficking: a 2001 estimate by Brazil's network RENCTAS put the figure at about 38 million wild animals taken each year in Brazil, with only 1 in 10 reaching the buyer alive.

For children

Birds were born to fly far, in flocks, looking for food and choosing where to stay. In a cage, they just hop from one perch to another. If a bird was already born caged, it's best to look after it well, with plenty of space. But it's better not to buy others, so no new bird is born to live caged.

Ask a question back

"If the problem is that it wouldn't survive outside, is the solution to look after it well, or to buy more birds that also wouldn't survive outside?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"My bird was born in a cage, it's legal. If I let it go, it dies. And it sings because it's happy."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Birds raised in captivity don't know how to survive in the wild.
  2. P2Legal birds don't come from trafficking.
  3. P3The bird sings and eats, signs that it's doing well.
  4. CSo keeping birds in cages is acceptable.

P1 is true for the bird that already exists, and it's a reason to keep caring for it. P2 is partly true, but the legal and illegal markets overlap. P3 confuses surviving with living well. The conclusion about breeding new birds doesn't follow.

The argument is valid for the bird that already exists. It fails when used to justify breeding and buying.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    QuestionBird already in captivityNew bird bought or bred
    Is releasing it a good idea?No, without proper rehabilitation—
    What to do?Care for it, with space and enrichmentDon't buy or breed
    What does the market encourage?—More breeding and, indirectly, trafficking
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Surviving isn't living well.

    Birds fly, live in groups and explore large areas. In a cage, even well fed, they are deprived of almost everything they naturally do. Song may be a territorial or mating call, not a sign of contentment.

    2. The argument is about the bird, not the market.

    That a captive-bred bird wouldn't survive outside is a reason to care for it, not to bring others into the same situation.

    3. The legal and illegal markets overlap.

    Brazil's National Network to Fight Wild Animal Trafficking (RENCTAS) estimated in 2001 that about 38 million wild animals were taken from nature each year in Brazil, and that only 1 in 10 reached the final buyer alive. The estimate is old, but it remains the reference cited.

    4. Exotic pets and aquariums have the same problem.

    Reptiles, primates, parrots and aquarium fish have needs that are hard to meet in a home, and demand for them encourages capture and breeding.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Jornal da USPBrazil accounts for the trafficking of about 38 million wild animals a yearCites the 2001 RENCTAS report (in Portuguese)
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: buying from a legal breeder fights trafficking

    The most serious version says a legal breeder offers an alternative to the trafficked animal, and that banning breeding would push buyers to the illegal market.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The legal market keeps demand going.

    Keeping caged birds still seems normal, and some buyers turn to trafficking for price or species.

    A legal bird also lives caged.

    Legal origin doesn't change life in a cage.

    There are ways to love birds without caging them.

    Watching free birds, planting native trees and supporting rehabilitation centers.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question isn't whether the bird that already exists should be suddenly released, but whether it's fair to bring new birds into the world to live in a cage. Caring for those that exist and not buying others solves both.

Likes birds.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that suddenly releasing a captive-bred bird can kill it, that many people love their birds and care for them well, and that legal breeding is better than trafficking.

But

But that calls for caring well for the birds that already exist, not for buying or breeding others for the same life.
