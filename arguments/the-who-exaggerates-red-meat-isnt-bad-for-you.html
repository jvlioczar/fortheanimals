G17

"The WHO exaggerates; red meat isn't bad for you"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about health and nutrition, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on health grounds, anyone who manages to be healthy eating animals, and many people do, would have no reason to change. And if an animal product turned out to be demonstrably healthier, the same argument would start defending exploitation. The eater's health says nothing about whether what is done to the animal is just.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the argument picks the convenient studies and ignores the whole. The WHO cancer agency's classification was based on more than 800 studies: processed meat is carcinogenic to humans, and red meat is probably carcinogenic. The WHO also recommends limiting saturated fat to less than 10% of calories. Nutrition studies have limits, but the answer to those limits is to weigh all the evidence, not to discard what's inconvenient.

Short answer

The WHO's cancer agency reviewed more than 800 studies before classifying processed meat as carcinogenic and red meat as probably carcinogenic. Every 50 grams of processed meat a day was linked to an 18% higher risk of colorectal cancer. The WHO also recommends limiting saturated fat. Nutrition studies have limits, but the answer is to weigh the whole body of evidence, not to pick only the studies that confirm what you want to eat.

For children

Scientists from around the world looked at lots of research on meat and health, more than 800 studies. They saw that eating a lot of processed meat, like sausages and ham, increases the chance of getting a type of cancer. That doesn't mean everyone will get sick, but that the risk goes up.

Ask a question back

"If you distrust observational studies, which studies do you rely on to say meat isn't bad for you?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Nutrition studies are all observational. Saturated fat has been cleared. Meat doesn't cause cancer."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Many nutrition studies are observational and don't prove causation.
  2. P2Some recent reviews have questioned the role of saturated fat and red meat.
  3. P3The increase in individual risk attributed to meat is small.
  4. CSo the recommendations against red meat are exaggerated.

P1 is true. So is P2: there are reviews that dispute it. P3 is true in absolute terms. The problem lies in the conclusion: the existence of uncertainty doesn't cancel the weight of the body of evidence, and small risks per person add up to a lot across a population.

The argument is valid as a criticism of exaggeration. It fails when it concludes that red and processed meat carry no risk.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument selects criticisms and ignores the whole:

    What the argument highlightsWhat it leaves out
    Observational studies don't prove causationSeveral lines of evidence converge: cohorts, mechanisms, trials
    Some reviews dispute itHealth organizations maintain their recommendations
    The individual risk is smallSmall risks add up to millions of cases across a population
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The classification is based on many studies.

    The WHO cancer agency's working group, with 22 experts from 10 countries, reviewed more than 800 studies before reaching its conclusion.

    2. The classification measures the strength of evidence, not the size of the risk.

    Being in the same group as tobacco doesn't mean being as dangerous as tobacco, and the WHO itself clarified this. It means the evidence that it causes cancer is strong.

    3. The saturated fat recommendation stands.

    In 2023, the WHO published a guideline recommending that saturated fat be limited to less than 10% of calories.

    4. Small risks add up.

    An 18% increase in the relative risk of colorectal cancer may be small for one person, but it represents many cases across a whole population.

    5. Picking only the criticisms is cherry picking.

    Every field has dissenting studies. Using them to discard the whole is the same method used to deny consensus in other fields.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    IARC, press release no. 240 (2015)Processed meat is carcinogenic (group 1) and red meat is probably carcinogenic (group 2A); every 50 g of processed meat a day linked to 18% higher risk of colorectal cancerBased on more than 800 studies
    WHO, guideline on saturated and trans fat (2023)Recommends limiting saturated fat to less than 10% of caloriesOfficial guideline
    GBD 2017 Diet Collaborators, *The Lancet* (2019)Dietary risks linked to about 11 million deaths in 2017Most came from too much salt and too little whole grain and fruit; red and processed meat weigh less. It shows that diet matters, not that meat is the main risk
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: some reviews say the evidence is weak

    The most serious version cites reviews that rated the evidence against red meat as low certainty, and that concluded most people can keep eating meat.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Low certainty isn't absence of risk.

    The same reviews found associations in the same direction.

    Those reviews were heavily criticized.

    Health organizations kept their recommendations.

    The moral question doesn't depend on this.

    Even if meat were harmless to whoever eats it, it isn't harmless to the animal.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The debate about the risk meat poses to those who eat it is important, but it's about the eater's health. The animals die either way. Even if meat did no harm at all, the question would remain: is it fair to kill them when we don't need to?

Contradictions

Accepts observational studies when they favor meat.

But rejects them when they don't.

Trusts the WHO on other subjects.

Such as vaccines and tobacco.

Doesn't demand decades-long clinical trials for other risks.

No one expects a randomized trial on smoking.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that nutrition studies have limitations, that headlines exaggerate, and that the individual risk of meat is smaller than that of tobacco. Skepticism toward alarmism is healthy, and there are legitimate debates about the size of the effects.

But

But the body of evidence points to real risks from processed and red meat, and the main health organizations maintain their recommendations. And even if meat did no harm to whoever eats it, the harm to the animal would remain.
