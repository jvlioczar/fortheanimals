I26

"The cart is the carter's livelihood, and the horse is well cared for"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the livelihood of people who work with carts is a real social issue, and any ban needs to come with alternative sources of income. But an urban draft horse usually works on asphalt, in the heat and in traffic, pulling heavy loads, and what happens to it when it gets sick or old is rarely seen. Cities such as Petrópolis, in Brazil, decided to replace horse-drawn carriages with electric vehicles: in a 2018 plebiscite, about 68% of voters rejected animal traction in tourist rides. The way forward that respects both sides is to replace the horse, not the worker.

Short answer

The carter's livelihood is a real social issue, and any change needs to come with alternative income. But an urban draft horse works on asphalt, in the heat and in traffic, pulling heavy loads, and what happens to it when it gets sick or old is rarely seen. Petrópolis, in Brazil, decided to replace tourist carriages with electric vehicles after a 2018 plebiscite. The way forward is to replace the horse, not the worker.

For children

Some people use horses to pull carts around the city, carrying heavy loads on hot asphalt and among cars. These people's work is important, but the same work can be done with motorized or electric carts. That way the person keeps working and the horse gets to rest.

Ask a question back

"If the carter got a motorized vehicle and support to work, would he still need the horse?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"You want to take away a poor carter's livelihood because of a horse? He looks after the animal; it's how he makes a living."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Many poor families depend on carts to work, collecting recyclables or doing hauling.
  2. P2The owner has an interest in looking after the horse, his working tool.
  3. P3Banning carts takes income away from those who need it most.
  4. CSo animal traction should continue.

P1 is true. P2 holds in part, but economic interest doesn't guarantee welfare. P3 is true if the ban comes without an alternative; it doesn't have to.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails because it treats the horse-drawn cart as the only way to keep that income.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    ProblemAn answer that respects both sides
    The carter's incomePrograms replacing carts with motorized or electric vehicles, registration and support
    The horse's welfareRetirement from work, referral to care or adoption
    Tourist carriagesElectric carriages, as proposed in Petrópolis
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Economic interest doesn't guarantee welfare.

    Caring enough for the animal to keep working is different from respecting its needs. Asphalt, heat, loads and traffic cause injuries, exhaustion and accidents.

    2. The choice isn't between the horse and the poor.

    Brazilian cities that restrict animal traction combine the measure with vehicle replacement programs and support for workers. The social problem is the lack of an alternative, not the lack of a horse.

    3. Some places have already decided.

    In Petrópolis, in a 2018 plebiscite, about 68% of voters rejected animal traction in tourist rides, and the city began replacing carriages with electric vehicles.

    4. Caring about the horse doesn't exclude caring about the carter.

    The accusation assumes a competition that the combined solution removes (J16).

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    IstoÉ, cities ban carriage ridesPetrópolis decided to end horse-drawn carriages after a plebiscite, with a proposal for electric vehiclesReport (in Portuguese)
    TRE-RJ, plebiscite resultsAbout 68% of votes against animal tractionOfficial results (in Portuguese)
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: replacement programs don't reach everyone

    The most serious version points out that, in practice, many carters are left without an alternative when a ban arrives, and the measure ends up punishing the poorest.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's a criticism of how the policy is done, not of its goal.

    The right thing is to demand that replacement reach everyone.

    The two problems can be solved together.

    Motorized or electric vehicles keep the income and take the horse out of suffering.

    Keeping the horse doesn't solve poverty.

    Dependence on the cart is a symptom of lack of opportunity, not a solution.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question is whether it's fair for an animal to pay, with its body, for the lack of alternatives of the people who work. A fair answer looks after both.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

False dilemma
Either the horse works, or the carter goes hungry

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Economic interest as a guarantee
Assumes those who depend on the animal look after it well

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

The animal pays for inequality
The lack of human alternatives justifies the animal's suffering

Biases

False dichotomy

Caring for the animal seems opposed to caring for the person

Contradictions

Defends the poor.

And doesn't demand programs that give them an alternative.

Says the horse is well cared for.

And sees exhausted horses in traffic.

Wouldn't accept the same with dogs.

Dogs pulling carts around the city would be seen as cruelty.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that carters' income is a serious social issue, that many are fond of the animal, and that bans without alternatives punish the poorest.

But

But the fair solution isn't to keep the horse on the asphalt; it's to guarantee people alternative work and the animals care.
