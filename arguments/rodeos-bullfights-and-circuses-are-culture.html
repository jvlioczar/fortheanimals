I8

"Rodeos, bullfights and circuses are culture"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's the appeal to tradition (D1) applied to suffering imposed for entertainment. Many countries have already decided that being cultural doesn't put a practice beyond the reach of laws against cruelty: England and Wales banned hunting with dogs in 2004 and wild animals in circuses in 2020; Brazil's Supreme Court banned the farra do boi, a bull-chasing festival, and cockfighting, and in 2016 ruled the vaquejada, a rodeo in which riders pull bulls down by the tail, unconstitutional for cruelty. Tradition explains why a practice exists; it doesn't justify the suffering in it.

Short answer

Being a tradition doesn't put a practice beyond laws against cruelty. England and Wales banned hunting with dogs in 2004 and wild animals in circuses in 2020. Brazil's Supreme Court banned the farra do boi and cockfighting, and in 2016 ruled the vaquejada unconstitutional for cruelty; a constitutional amendment then tried to protect it. Festivals can keep their music, food and gathering, without the animals' suffering.

For children

At some festivals, people pull bulls down by the tail, fight bulls in an arena, or ride animals that buck out of fear and pain. This is called tradition. But hurting animals for fun doesn't become right just because it has gone on for a long time. Festivals can carry on with music, food and dancing, without hurting anyone.

Ask a question back

"If other traditions involving animals have been banned for cruelty, why should this one be different?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Rodeo is cultural heritage. They want to end the tradition. The bull doesn't even feel it."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Rodeos, bullfights and other festivals with animals are part of many communities' identity.
  2. P2These practices are recognized as cultural heritage.
  3. P3Banning them would destroy part of the culture and harm those who make a living from them.
  4. CSo these practices should be kept.

P1 and P2 are true. P3 has a point about economic impact. The problem lies in the conclusion: cultural value doesn't authorize the suffering imposed on the animals.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails because cultural value doesn't answer the question of cruelty.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument uses cultural value to close the discussion about cruelty:

    PracticeWhat happens to the animalLegal situation
    Hunting with dogs (England and Wales)Foxes and other mammals chased and killed by dogsBanned by the Hunting Act 2004
    Wild animals in circuses (England)Confinement, transport and training for performancesBanned since 2020
    Bullfighting (Spain)The bull is wounded and usually killed in the arenaBanned in Catalonia in 2010; the ban was overturned by Spain's Constitutional Court in 2016
    Farra do boi and cockfighting (Brazil)Bulls chased and beaten; birds fighting to injury or deathBanned
    Vaquejada (Brazil)The bull is pulled down by the tailRuled unconstitutional in 2016; later protected by Constitutional Amendment 96
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's the appeal to tradition.

    The age of a practice says nothing about whether it's just (D1).

    2. The law has already recognized the cruelty.

    Parliaments and courts in several countries have banned traditional practices with animals because of the suffering involved. Where they were later protected, as with the vaquejada in Brazil or bullfighting in Spain, the reason given was culture, not the absence of suffering; Brazil's amendment even made the practice conditional on animal welfare.

    3. "The bull doesn't even feel it" is false.

    Cattle are sentient, and being pulled down by the tail can cause fractures, dislocations and serious injuries. In bullfighting, wounding the bull is part of the spectacle.

    4. Cultures change.

    Many traditional festivals keep their value without using animals, and many places have already replaced cruel practices.

    5. The suffering is imposed for entertainment.

    Unlike food, here there isn't even a claim of necessity.

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: communities live off these festivals

    The most serious version points out that many communities depend economically on these festivals, and that banning them without alternatives would harm real people.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Economic impact calls for a transition, not preservation.

    Festivals can transform and keep their audience.

    Economics doesn't justify cruelty.

    The same argument would defend dogfighting.

    There are examples of change.

    Festivals that replaced cruel practices and kept drawing crowds.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Here there isn't even a claim of necessity: the suffering is imposed for entertainment. The moral question becomes even clearer: does the pleasure of a spectacle justify hurting and frightening animals?

What the argument gets right

What is true

Traditional festivals have real cultural and social value, and many communities identify deeply with them. Changes imposed without dialogue can breed resentment and harm people who make a living from these festivals.

But

But the value of a festival lies in the gathering, the music, the food and the history, not in the animals' suffering. Traditions can change and still be traditions.
