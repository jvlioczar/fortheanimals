N9

"Helping wild animals isn't veganism; it's a different cause"

The answer in one sentence

it's true that veganism, as defined, is about not exploiting animals. But the foundation of veganism is sentience: animals matter because they can suffer and have a good or bad life. That foundation doesn't change depending on who caused the suffering. An antispeciesism that cares only about the human victims of nature, and not the animal ones, keeps exactly the kind of exclusion it claims to fight.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

I agree that veganism, by definition, is about not exploiting. But why not exploit? Because animals feel. And an animal with hunger, disease or parasites feels it the same way, even if no one caused it. No one has to give up the fight against animal farming: we can advocate both, and both come from the same idea.

For children

Imagine you learned that you mustn't hurt others. Great! But if a classmate falls and hurts themselves on their own, wouldn't you want to help too? Not hurting is the beginning. If we really care about others, we also want to help when they need it, even if it isn't our fault.

Ask a question back

"If the reason for being vegan is that animals feel, why would an animal's suffering stop mattering just because nature caused it?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Veganism is about not exploiting animals. Wild animals aren't our business. First end animal farming, then we'll talk."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Veganism is a position against the exploitation of animals by humans.
  2. P2The suffering of wild animals doesn't result from human exploitation.
  3. P3Mixing the two causes confuses the public and diverts resources from the fight against exploitation.
  4. CSo helping wild animals isn't part of veganism and shouldn't be advocated by vegans.

P1 and P2 are true as a matter of definition. P3 is a legitimate strategic concern. The problem is the moral conclusion: that something isn't in the definition of veganism doesn't show it isn't a consequence of the same principle.

The argument is valid as to the definition, but invalid as to what matters: from "it isn't veganism" it doesn't follow that "it isn't a duty".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument depends on confusing a movement's definition with the reach of its foundation:

    LevelWhat it saysReach
    Definition of veganismTo exclude, as far as possible and practicable, exploitation of and cruelty to animalsHuman practices of use
    FoundationSentient beings have interests that matter, whatever their speciesEvery sentient being, in any situation
    Consequences of the foundationNot exploiting; and also taking into account the suffering we can preventIncludes, but isn't limited to, exploitation

    Veganism can keep its definition. What doesn't hold is saying the foundation stops there.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The foundation doesn't ask who caused it.

    If animals matter because they feel, their suffering matters wherever it comes from. An ethics that counts only suffering caused by humans measures harm by the agent's guilt, not by what the victim feels (N2, N4).

    2. The human test.

    A human rights movement can focus on violations committed by governments. But no one would say that, because of this, helping victims of floods or epidemics doesn't matter morally. Choosing a movement's focus is one thing; saying the rest isn't a problem is another.

    3. It isn't welfarism.

    Welfarism regulates exploitation; the proposal to help wild animals regulates no exploitation at all. Abolitionists also care about animals' wellbeing, and the proposal is compatible with rejecting all exploitation.

    4. Different causes don't have to compete.

    A person can fight animal farming and support research on wild animals. And teaching that every sentient being matters, whoever caused the harm, strengthens antispeciesism as a whole.

    5. It's a hierarchy that survives among vegans.

    Giving more weight to exploited animals than to wild ones, just because of the origin of the harm, is one of the forms of speciesism that persist even among those who have rejected exploitation.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: strategy matters

    The most serious version says: even if the foundation reaches wild animals, talking about it now harms the movement. The public already thinks veganism is radical; talking about intervening in nature will make it look absurd, and that delays the end of exploitation.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's an argument about when and how to speak, not about what's right.

    It can justify care in communication, but not denying the problem exists.

    Hiding the foundation weakens the argument.

    If veganism is defended only as "not exploiting", without explaining why, it loses the basis that makes it coherent and becomes more vulnerable to objections (F3).

    The public can understand.

    Most people already approve of rescuing animals from fires and floods. The idea of helping wild animals isn't strange; what's strange is treating it as absurd.

Contradictions

And then says some animals' suffering doesn't matter because no one caused it.

But keeps a hierarchy among animals according to the origin of the harm.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that veganism has a precise definition worth preserving, and that a movement's communication requires choices. It's also right that exploitation is harm we cause, which we can stop now, and that this has practical priority.

But

But the foundation of veganism goes beyond it. If what matters is sentience, the animals suffering in nature matter too. There's no need to change the definition of veganism to recognize this; just don't use the definition as an excuse.
