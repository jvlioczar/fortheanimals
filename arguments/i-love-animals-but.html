E11

"I love animals, but..."

The answer in one sentence

the "but" reveals the contradiction. Loving some animals while paying for others, just as able to suffer, to be killed is what psychology calls the meat paradox. The sentence isn't an argument: it's the description of a conflict between what the person values and what they do. And that conflict is actually the best starting point for change.

Short answer

Loving animals is real and a great start. But pigs, cows and chickens feel pain and fear like the dogs and cats you love. The difference between them isn't in the animals, but in the place habit has given each one. Psychology calls this the meat paradox. If the love of animals is sincere, it's the best reason to extend that care to those on the plate.

For children

Imagine someone who says: "I really love my classmates, but I push some of them." Strange, right? If we love animals, it makes sense to think about whether the pig and the chicken also deserve that love. They feel fear and pain just like the dog at home.

Ask a question back

"What does a pig have that's different from your dog that justifies eating one and loving the other?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I have three dogs. I'm an animal lover, but I won't give up meat."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1I love and care for several animals.
  2. P2Eating meat is a separate practice, tied to food rather than affection.
  3. P3I can love animals and eat meat without contradiction, as many people do.
  4. CSo eating meat doesn't contradict my love of animals.

P1 is sincere. The problem lies in P2 and P3: the split between animals "for affection" and animals "for food" doesn't correspond to any difference in the animals themselves.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2, which treats a category created by habit as a real difference.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The sentence puts together a value and a practice that pull in opposite directions:

    What the person valuesWhat the person does
    That animals don't sufferPays for practices that make animals suffer
    Caring for the animals at homeFunds the slaughter of others with similar capacities
    Being outraged by crueltyAccepts practices that would be cruelty if done to dogs
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The difference is in the habit, not the animal.

    Pigs, cows and chickens feel pain and fear like dogs and cats. Comparisons of intelligence between species are imprecise, and not even needed: what matters is the capacity to suffer. The split between "pet" and "food" is cultural: in other countries, different animals sit on each side.

    2. It's the meat paradox.

    Psychology has studied this conflict for over a decade. Research shows people attribute less mind and less moral status to the animals they eat, and do so more when reminded of the link between meat and suffering.

    3. The category changes perception.

    In one experiment, classifying an animal as food was enough to lower the moral status attributed to it. The animal doesn't change; the way we see it does.

    4. Love is a starting point.

    The sentence isn't a defense; it's the recognition of a conflict. Those who love animals already accept the most important premise: that they matter.

    5. The "but" usually hides another argument.

    After the "but" usually comes taste (J5), custom (D1) or priority (A12). Those are the arguments that need examining.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010Eating meat reduces the moral status and mind attributed to animalsA classic study on the meat paradox
    Bastian et al., 2012Being reminded of the link between meat and suffering increases the denial of mind to animalsShows the defense mechanism
    Bratanova, Loughnan and Bastian, 2011Classifying an animal as food lowers the moral status attributed to itThe category changes perception
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: love is always partial

    The most serious version says all love is partial: we love our family more than strangers, and no one finds that contradictory. Loving one's own animals more than others would be equally natural.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Loving more isn't licensing harm.

    We love our family more than strangers, but we don't pay for strangers to suffer (A12).

    Partiality explains care, not exploitation.

    Not caring for everyone is different from funding the suffering of some.

    The counter-reply concedes the central point.

    If the issue is just how intensely one loves, the other animals still deserve, at least, not to be harmed.

Contradictions

Would be horrified if someone ate their dog.

But accepts eating animals with similar capacities.

Condemns cruelty to dogs.

And funds practices that would be cruelty if done to dogs.

Says they love "animals", not "some animals".

The sentence already contains the contradiction.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Loving animals is sincere and valuable. People who care for dogs and cats, rescue animals or are outraged by cruelty already show that animals matter to them. That affection is a real force, not hypocrisy.

But

What the sentence reveals isn't an excuse but a conflict. And conflicts like this are often the beginning of change: all it takes is extending the same love to the animals still on the other side of the line.
