K9

"Radical veganism pushes people away"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about effectiveness and individual responsibility, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended only by each person's impact, anyone who proved their purchase changes nothing would be let off. But no one accepts this for other harms: taking part in an injustice is still wrong even when each person's share is small.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

there may be some strategic truth here: certain tones and tactics put some people off. But that isn't a moral refutation, nor a personal excuse. That a messenger is unpleasant doesn't make the message false. Besides, radical isn't the same as extremist: a radical is someone who goes to the root of a problem and calls for deep change, while an extremist is, typically, someone who rejects pluralism and accepts violence. Radicalism has been part of other social movements, and research on the so-called radical flank effect shows that radical voices can either increase or decrease support for moderate ones. What matters, for the animals, is the overall effect of each strategy.

Reply

It may be that some tones and tactics put some people off, and it's worth discussing the best way to communicate. But does that change whether it's right to eat animals? An unpleasant messenger doesn't make the message false. Radical also isn't the same as extremist: a radical is someone who calls for change that goes to the root of the problem, which doesn't imply violence. Movements such as abolitionism and the fight for women's votes had radical wings, and research shows they can help or hurt, depending on the case. What matters is the overall effect for the animals. And the question for you remains: do animals deserve to be spared, regardless of who speaks for them?

For children

Sometimes a person speaks in a way that makes us angry, even when they're right. But the way they speak doesn't change whether something is right or wrong. If someone rudely warns us that we're hurting someone, it's still worth stopping. And wanting a big change isn't the same as being violent: many people who changed the world were called radicals.

Ask a question back

"If a vegan were kind to you, would the answer about the animals change?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"You scare people off. Why don't you just promote reduction? Toxic veganism."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Messages perceived as radical or judgmental provoke rejection.
  2. P2Rejection reduces people's willingness to change.
  3. P3A more moderate approach, such as encouraging reduction, would reach more people.
  4. CSo radical veganism harms its own cause.

P1 and P2 have support in some contexts. P3 is a plausible but uncertain strategic hypothesis. The problem is that the conclusion, even if correct, is about strategy, not about whether it's right to eat animals. Besides, the argument tends to call both the goal (not using animals) and the tactics (confrontational protests) "radical", and research suggests the two have different effects.

The argument is valid as a strategic criticism. It fails when used as a justification for not changing.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument mixes a strategic question with a moral one, and often also confuses two concepts:

    QuestionTypeDoes the argument answer it?
    What's the best way to persuade people?StrategicPartly
    Is it right to eat animals?MoralNo
    Is being radical the same as being extremist?ConceptualIt assumes so, but it isn't
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The form doesn't change the content.

    That a message is delivered unpleasantly doesn't make it false.

    2. It isn't a personal excuse.

    Other people's tone doesn't excuse anyone from examining the question for themselves.

    3. Radical isn't the same as extremist.

    The word "radical" comes from the Latin radix, root: in political use, since the 19th century, it has described those who advocate a thorough reform that goes to the root of the problem. In the literature on radicalization, researchers such as Alex Schmid distinguish the two terms: radicals aren't violent by definition and have historically tended to be more open to debate and compromise, while extremists tend to reject pluralism, disregard the rule of law and favor the use of force. In this sense, advocating that animals not be used is a radical position, because it calls for fundamental change, but it isn't extremism. In practice, though, more confrontational strategies, such as protests that disrupt events, are often labeled radical, and that's usually what the argument is about (L5).

    4. "Radical" often just means consistent.

    Saying animals shouldn't be killed without need is called radical, but it applies a principle almost everyone accepts.

    5. Radicalism has always been part of other social movements.

    Abolitionists such as William Lloyd Garrison, who from 1831 demanded the immediate, not gradual, emancipation of enslaved people, were seen as radicals. In the United Kingdom, the suffragettes of the Women's Social and Political Union adopted militant tactics, such as smashing windows and going on hunger strike, alongside the moderate suffragists. In the US civil rights movement, the sociologist Herbert Haines, who coined the term "radical flank effect", found in 1984 that the rise of more radical Black groups coincided with an increase, not a fall, in funding for moderate organizations. Historians still debate how much each wing contributed to the victories.

    6. The radical flank effect can be positive or negative.

    In 2022, two experiments with about 2,770 participants, one of them on the animal movement, found that the presence of a group using radical tactics increased support for moderate groups in the same movement, who came to seem more reasonable by comparison; the effect came from the tactics, not from radical goals, which changed support little. A study with representative samples in the United Kingdom, published in 2024, found a similar effect after road blockades by Just Stop Oil, a climate group. But there are negative effects too: a 2020 study of Black protests in the United States estimated that nonviolent protests increased the Democratic vote in nearby counties, while violent ones shifted white voters toward Nixon in 1968. For animals, a Social Change Lab study of the Animal Rising protest at the Grand National, a British horse race, in 2023 found more donations to and sign-ups for the group, but also less agreement with criticisms of animal use among those who knew about the protest; six months later, these negative effects had largely faded. The evidence is limited and mixed.

    7. What matters is the overall effect for the animals.

    If a form of radicalism tends to have negative effects for the animals, less radical or different strategies would be needed. Likewise, if it tends to have positive effects, it should be kept. The strategic question isn't settled by how uncomfortable a tactic makes people, but by what it produces overall, and the answers may vary by tactic, country and moment.

    8. Eating more meat out of irritation is a curious effect.

    If irritation with vegans leads someone to harm animals more, the problem isn't with the animals.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: strategy matters for the animals

    The most serious version says that if the goal is to help animals, the effectiveness of communication is a moral question, because it determines how many animals are spared. And there's evidence that some confrontational tactics provoke rejection.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's valid for activists.

    Animal advocates should think about the best strategy and look at the research on what works, including when it shows a tactic is counterproductive.

    It doesn't apply to whoever receives the message.

    For that person, the question is still about their own plate.

    Different strategies can coexist.

    Moderate and firmer voices can complement each other, and the radical flank effect shows that the presence of some can change how the others are seen.

    The radical goal isn't the problem.

    In the experiments, what changed support was the tactics, not the goal of ending animal use.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument is about vegans, not animals. But animals don't choose who speaks for them. The moral question stays the same, whoever the messenger: is it fair to make them suffer and die when we don't need to?

Contradictions

Accepts ideas defended by unpleasant people on other subjects.

Doesn't change opinion on other matters because of tone.

Says they'd support a moderate approach.

But doesn't change when they meet one.

Admires movements that were called radical.

Such as abolitionism or the fight for women's votes.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The way we communicate matters, and some animal advocates do push people away with judgment, aggression or contempt. Some confrontational tactics can also provoke rejection, and research shows the radical flank effect isn't always positive. Discussing communication strategies is legitimate and necessary, and the movement should learn from criticism and from the evidence.

But

But criticism of the messenger doesn't answer the message. Whatever the tone of whoever speaks, the question about the animals is still waiting for an answer.
