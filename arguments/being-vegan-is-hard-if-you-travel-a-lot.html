M4

"Being vegan is hard if you travel a lot"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the experience of someone who tried to be vegan and gave up, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism depended on being easy for everyone, one person's difficulty would be enough to make it stop counting. But someone's difficulty, which deserves to be heard and helped, doesn't change what happens to the animals: it calls for practical support, not a new moral conclusion.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

traveling takes more planning for vegans, and sometimes the options are few. But almost every place has something without animal products: rice, beans, bread, fruit, vegetables, nuts. Restaurant-finder apps, supermarkets and a snack in the bag solve most cases. The difficulty justifies, at most, a rare and genuine exception, not giving up veganism at home, where most meals are eaten.

Short answer

Traveling does take more planning. But almost every place has something vegan, even if it's simple: rice, beans, bread, fruit, salad. A snack in the bag, a restaurant app and a supermarket solve most cases. And if somewhere there really is no option, that justifies an exception there, not dropping veganism at home.

For children

When we travel, we can bring a snack in our backpack: fruit, nuts, a sandwich. And almost everywhere there's rice, beans, bread or fruit. With a little planning, you can travel without eating animals.

Ask a question back

"In how many of your meals this week do you really have no option at all?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I travel a lot for work. Being vegan on the road, in airports, in small towns is impossible. I gave up."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1People who travel a lot depend on what they find along the way.
  2. P2In many places, vegan options are few or nonexistent.
  3. P3No one is obliged to go hungry or to eat poorly.
  4. CSo people who travel a lot aren't obliged to be vegan.

P1 and P3 are true. P2 is sometimes true, but it's usually exaggerated. And the conclusion goes too far: even if some meals on a trip were difficult, that wouldn't justify eating animals at home or in places where there are options.

The argument is valid, but not sound: it extends the difficulty of a few situations to a whole life.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument uses the hardest case to decide all the others:

    SituationIs it really hard?What to do
    At homeNoEat vegan
    In a big cityRarelyRestaurants and supermarkets
    On the roadSometimesSnacks in the bag, simple dishes, supermarkets
    In a remote place, with no option at allYesAs little harm as possible, as an exception
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The difficulty concerns some meals, not all of them.

    Even people who travel a lot eat a good share of their meals in places with options. The conclusion of giving everything up doesn't follow from a few difficult meals.

    2. Almost every place has basic vegan food.

    Rice, beans, bread, pasta with tomato sauce, fruit, vegetables, nuts. It may not be a special meal, but it's food.

    3. Planning solves most cases.

    Bringing snacks, looking up restaurants in apps, choosing accommodation with a kitchen, asking for adapted dishes. That's what people with allergies, religious restrictions or celiac disease do.

    4. The options are growing.

    Restaurants, fast-food chains and supermarkets offer more and more vegan products, including on highways and in airports.

    5. A genuine exception is different from giving up.

    If, in a remote place, there's nothing without animal products, eating the minimum needed there is an exception. That doesn't license eating animals at home.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Faunalytics, 2014Inconvenience was a reason for quitting for 13% of former vegetarians and vegansSurvey carried out in 2014 in the United States
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: it's exhausting to live always planning

    The most serious version says: the problem isn't one meal, but the fatigue of always having to plan, search, ask and explain. For someone who travels every week, that weighs on them.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Planning becomes a habit.

    After a few trips, a person knows what to bring, where to stop and what to order. The effort shrinks over time.

    Fatigue justifies simplifying, not giving up.

    Eating the simplest dish, always bringing the same snack, having a few trusted places.

    The cost to animals doesn't shrink with fatigue.

    The discomfort of planning is still much smaller than the harm avoided.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Even if traveling made veganism more work, the question would remain the same: is it just to make animals suffer and die to avoid the work of planning a meal? Most of the time, the answer is a snack in the bag.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that traveling takes more planning, that in some places the options really are few and that the fatigue of always planning weighs on people. It's also right that no one should go hungry because of a rule.

But

But the difficulty of a few meals doesn't justify giving up veganism in all of them. The good answer is to share travel strategies, and to remember that genuine exceptions are different from giving up.
