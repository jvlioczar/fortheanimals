N10

"Sentientists are against preserving species and biodiversity"

The answer in one sentence

the sentientist answer is "it depends on how this affects the animals". Species and biodiversity don't feel; individuals do. So the sentientist supports preservation when it is good for sentient beings, which is often, and questions it when it harms them, as in the mass killing of "invasive" animals. That isn't being in favor of biodiversity loss. It is refusing to treat biodiversity as a value above those who live within it, something no one does when the ones affected are human.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

I'm not against preservation. I just ask who the preservation is good for. A species feels nothing; its animals do. When protecting a habitat prevents hunger, fire and death, I support it, and that's the most common case. When "preserving" means killing millions of animals because they were born in the wrong place, I question it. It's the same with people: we value the diversity of cultures, but not at the cost of people's suffering.

For children

A forest doesn't feel pain, but the animals who live in it do. So when someone wants to protect the forest, we ask: is this good for the animals who live there? Almost always it is, and then we help. But if, to "protect" the forest, someone wants to kill lots of animals, we ask them to look for another way. Caring about animals one by one isn't being against the forest.

Ask a question back

"Would you accept preserving a human culture by forcing its members to suffer in order to keep it going? If not, why does the rule change when they're animals?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"You don't care whether a species goes extinct. Sentientists only think about the individual animal, and nature can go hang."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Sentientism says that only beings capable of feeling matter morally in themselves.
  2. P2Species, ecosystems and biodiversity don't feel.
  3. P3So, for the sentientist, species and biodiversity have no value.
  4. P4Anyone who sees no value in biodiversity is against preserving it, or indifferent to its loss.
  5. CSo sentientists are against preserving species and biodiversity.

P1 and P2 are correct. The error lies in P3, which confuses "having no value in itself" with "having no value at all", and in P4, which confuses "not being the supreme value" with "being something to fight".

The argument is invalid: from "biodiversity isn't an end in itself" it doesn't follow that "biodiversity doesn't matter".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The phrase assumes there are only two possible positions. There are at least three:

    PositionWhat it says about biodiversityWho holds it
    Supreme valueIt must be preserved even at the cost of the suffering and death of individualsPart of environmental ethics and conservation practice
    Value for those who feelIt matters a great deal, to the extent that it affects sentient beingsSentientism
    No valueIt makes no difference whether it is lostThe position attributed to sentientists

    The argument assigns sentientists to the third row, when they are in the second.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Having no value in itself isn't having no value.

    Public health, peace and clean water don't feel anything either, and no one concludes that they don't matter. They matter a great deal, because of what they mean for those who feel. The same goes for habitats and for the variety of life forms.

    2. Most of the time, the goals coincide.

    Habitat loss and ecological collapse cause suffering and death on a huge scale: WWF estimated that nearly 3 billion animals were killed or displaced by the Australian bushfires of 2019 and 2020. And the main cause of biodiversity loss in the world is the food system: according to a report by the British institute Chatham House, supported by the UN Environment Programme, agriculture is the identified threat to 24,000 of the 28,000 species assessed as at risk of extinction, and one of the ways out it points to is a more plant-based diet. The main practical demand of those who defend sentient beings, the end of animal farming, is also one of the measures most favorable to biodiversity.

    3. The conflict appears in the method.

    The sentientist questions preservation when it is carried out against the animals. Three examples: the mass killing of "invasive" animals (in 2015 the Australian government set a target of killing 2 million feral cats in five years, and an estimated 1.5 million were killed); the reintroduction of predators without anyone assessing what it means for the animals who will be hunted; and captive breeding programs that keep individuals confined for life in the name of the species (I6). N3 covers these programs in more detail.

    4. The human test.

    We value the diversity of cultures, languages and peoples. Even so, no one accepts preserving a culture by forbidding its members to leave it, to receive medical care or to change their lives, and no one would accept eliminating "outsiders" to maintain an "original" population. When the ones affected are human, diversity is valued for what it means to people, never above them. The sentientist simply applies the same rule to the other beings who feel.

    5. "So you don't care if a species goes extinct?" Not so.

    Extinctions almost always happen through hunger, hunting, disease and habitat destruction, which are harms to individuals, and they can affect many other sentient beings, including people who care about that species. What the sentientist denies is that there is, on top of all this, a harm to the species as if it were someone (A10).

    6. The proposal is to study wellbeing, not to abandon ecology.

    In 1995 the economist Yew-Kwang Ng proposed welfare biology: the study of living things and their environment with respect to their welfare. Organizations such as Animal Ethics and Wild Animal Initiative work for that study to exist in universities. The latter states that "identifying more humane ways to control populations has great potential to improve the lives of many wild animals". It's a research agenda that adds one question to conservation: how are the individuals doing?

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: extinction is forever, and without biodiversity everything falls apart

    The most serious version says: an extinct species doesn't come back. Ecosystems poor in species are more fragile, and their collapse harms all beings, sentient ones included. Faced with an irreversible loss, it's prudent to treat biodiversity as if it were untouchable. Besides, there are sentientist philosophers, such as Catia Faria and Eze Paez, who hold that animal ethics and environmental ethics are incompatible in their foundations.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    That is a sentientist argument.

    Preserving because collapse would harm those who feel is exactly the reason the sentientist accepts. The counter-reply confirms the position it set out to refute.

    Prudence doesn't license any method.

    Irreversibility calls for caution and also for looking for means that don't kill: contraception, fences, relocation, habitat protection. The question "is there another way?" is the one we would ask if the ones affected were human.

    Different foundations aren't opposition on everything.

    Faria and Paez discuss what has value in itself. Saying that the two ethics start from different points isn't being against every conservation measure, still less hoping for extinctions. In practice, the two often agree.

False dichotomy

Equivocation

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Double standard
Accepts limits on preservation when it hurts humans, but not when it hurts animals

Contradictions

Wouldn't sacrifice people for biodiversity.

Which shows they don't treat it as the supreme value either.

Accuses the sentientist of threatening species.

And often eats the products of the sector that threatens them most.

Says they defend nature out of love for animals.

But accepts killing them by the million when they belong to the "wrong" species.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that sentientism and part of environmental ethics start from different values, and that this difference has practical consequences: there are conservation programs a sentientist doesn't support. It's also right that some sentientist texts treat biodiversity coldly, as if it were irrelevant, and that this grates on people who have devoted their lives to protecting habitats. The loss of species is a real problem, and extinction can't be undone.

But

But disagreeing with some methods isn't being against the goal. The sentientist wants forests standing, rivers clean and habitats protected whenever that is good for those who live in them, and it almost always is. What the sentientist refuses is the idea that an abstraction can be worth more than the beings who feel.
