I21

"Horses like working and being ridden"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's a convenient projection by those who benefit. Horses are prey animals, and most riding teaches them to obey through pressure, discomfort and repetition; their docility is trained, not chosen. Many suffer from bits, spurs, excessive loads and work on hot asphalt, like cart and carriage horses in cities. That an animal gets used to something doesn't mean it likes it.

Short answer

Horses are prey animals, and most riding teaches them to obey through pressure, discomfort and repetition. Seeming calm isn't consenting: it's having learned. Many suffer from bits, spurs, excessive loads and work on hot asphalt, like city cart and carriage horses. Saying they were "born for this" is attributing to the animal the purpose we gave it (C10).

For children

Horses learn to obey because they're trained, sometimes with things that bother them, like the bit in their mouth. They can get used to it, but that doesn't mean they chose it. Horses that pull carts in the streets often work in the hot sun and carry a lot of weight. Loving horses means taking good care of them, not making them work for us.

Ask a question back

"How do you know the horse likes it, if it never had the option to refuse?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Horses were born to be ridden. The carriage driver depends on it."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Horses were domesticated thousands of years ago for work and riding.
  2. P2Many horses seem calm and cooperative while working.
  3. P3Well-treated horses have a partnership with their carers.
  4. CSo horses like working, and using them isn't exploitation.

P1 is true. P2 and P3 are true in some cases. The problem lies in the conclusion: seeming calm isn't consenting, and behavior is shaped by training.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails because it reads trained obedience as preference.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument reads trained behavior as preference:

    BehaviorConvenient interpretationMore careful interpretation
    The horse accepts the saddle"It likes it"It learned that resisting brings discomfort
    The horse obeys the bit"Partnership"Pressure in the mouth is uncomfortable
    The horse works all day"It's in its nature"It has no choice
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Trained obedience isn't consent.

    Horse training uses pressure and release: the animal learns to yield so the discomfort stops.

    2. Horses are prey animals.

    They often hide signs of pain and fear. Seeming calm doesn't guarantee wellbeing.

    3. There's real suffering in many uses.

    Cart horses in cities work with excessive loads, on hot asphalt and without adequate rest. Several cities have banned or restricted horse-drawn carts and carriages for this reason, from Brazilian cities such as Belo Horizonte and Recife to Chicago.

    4. "Born for this" is an imposed purpose.

    It's the purpose we assign, not a trait of the animal (C10).

    5. Economic dependence is real, and calls for a transition.

    Cart drivers need alternatives, and programs replacing carts with motor vehicles already exist in some cities.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    McGreevy and McLean, *Roles of learning theory and ethology in equitation* (2007)The horse learns to yield to remove discomfortJournal of Veterinary Behavior
    Municipal laws on animal-drawn vehicles (in Portuguese)Several Brazilian cities have banned or restricted cartsExample: Esteio (Rio Grande do Sul), 2021; Belo Horizonte, Recife and Curitiba have also passed laws
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: some horses seem to seek contact

    The most serious version points out that some horses approach their carers spontaneously and seem to enjoy activities, and that there are more respectful forms of riding.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Liking the carer isn't liking the work.

    The bond can be real without riding being wanted.

    More respectful forms are the exception.

    Most use of horses is commercial and intensive.

    The relationship can exist without exploitation.

    It's possible to live with horses without riding them or making them work.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument attributes to the horse the desire that suits whoever uses it. The moral question is whether it's fair to use an animal who can't consent, especially when the use causes suffering and there are alternatives.

What the argument gets right

What is true

There are real bonds of affection between horses and humans, and many carers treat their animals with devotion. The economic dependence of cart drivers and ranch hands is real and must be considered with respect.

But

But affection doesn't prove consent, and obedience is learned. Loving horses can mean caring for them without requiring them to work for us.
