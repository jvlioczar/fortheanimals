G19

"Fruitarianism isn't healthy, so veganism isn't either"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about health and nutrition, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on health grounds, anyone who manages to be healthy eating animals, and many people do, would have no reason to change. And if an animal product turned out to be demonstrably healthier, the same argument would start defending exploitation. The eater's health says nothing about whether what is done to the animal is just.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

veganism isn't fruitarianism. An all-fruit diet is genuinely restrictive and risky, especially for children, and it's honest to say so. But a vegan diet includes beans, lentils, grains, nuts, seeds, vegetables, fortified foods and a vitamin B12 supplement, and nutrition bodies consider it, when well planned, adequate at every stage of life. Criticizing the most extreme diet in the group says nothing about the others.

Short answer

I agree that eating only fruit isn't healthy, especially for children. But that isn't veganism. A vegan diet has beans, lentils, chickpeas, rice, nuts, vegetables and a B12 supplement, and nutrition bodies consider it, when well planned, adequate at every stage of life. It would be like saying eating meat is harmful because a strict carnivore diet is risky.

For children

Imagine someone who eats only bread, nothing else. Their body would be missing a lot, wouldn't it? That doesn't mean bread is bad, or that people who eat bread are wrong. It means eating just one thing isn't enough. It's the same with vegans: eating only fruit isn't good, but vegans eat beans, rice, vegetables, nuts and lots more.

Ask a question back

"If someone ate only meat and got sick, would you conclude that every diet with meat is dangerous?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Those people who only eat fruit get sick. That's veganism; it isn't healthy."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1All-fruit diets cause deficiencies in protein, vitamin B12, calcium, iron, zinc and other nutrients.
  2. P2Fruitarianism is a form of plant-based eating, advocated by some vegans.
  3. P3If one form of plant-based eating is dangerous, the others probably are too.
  4. CSo veganism isn't healthy.

P1 is true, and vegans shouldn't deny it. P2 is true only for a minority. The problem lies in P3, which generalizes from the most restrictive diet to all of them.

The argument is invalid: from the flaws of one specific diet, the flaws of every diet without animal products don't follow.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument takes the part for the whole:

    DietWhat it includesAssessment
    FruitarianismMostly fruit, sometimes nuts and seedsRestrictive; risk of deficiencies, especially in children
    A planned vegan dietLegumes, grains, nuts, seeds, vegetables, fruit, fortified foods, B12 supplementConsidered adequate at every stage of life by nutrition bodies
    Veganism (as an ethical position)Not exploiting animals, as far as possible and practicableNot a specific diet
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Veganism isn't a specific diet.

    It's an ethical position against the exploitation of animals. There are many ways to eat without animal products, and most vegans aren't fruitarians.

    2. The criticism of fruitarianism is valid, and doesn't reach the rest.

    All-fruit diets can cause deficiencies in protein, B12, calcium, iron, zinc and other nutrients, and they're unsuitable for children. That says something about that diet, not about eating beans and rice.

    3. The position of nutrition bodies.

    The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, the largest association of nutrition professionals in the United States, states that appropriately planned vegetarian diets, including vegan diets, are healthful and nutritionally adequate at every stage of life. The crucial point is planning, including B12 (G4).

    4. The same logic would apply to meat eaters.

    A strict carnivore diet, meat only, is also restrictive and has risks. No one would conclude from it that every diet with meat is dangerous (G16).

    5. Vegans shouldn't romanticize fruitarianism either.

    Saying "humans are frugivores by nature" is a weak argument this guide asks people to stop using (V30). Defending animals doesn't require defending extreme diets.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Melina, Craig and Levin, 2016Appropriately planned vegetarian, including vegan, diets are healthful, nutritionally adequate and appropriate for all stages of lifePosition of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics
    NIH, Office of Dietary Supplements, vitamin B12Natural sources of B12 are limited to animal foods; vegans need fortified foods or supplements
    Wikipedia, "Fruitarianism"Gathers sources on the risks of fruitarianism: deficiencies in protein, B12, calcium, iron, zinc and fatty acids, and unsuitability for childrenUsed as an overview, with the sources cited in the article
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: plant-based diets demand too much care

    The most serious version says: even if veganism isn't fruitarianism, any diet without animal products requires planning and supplementation, and many people don't do it right. In practice there would be more risk than with the usual diet.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Every diet requires some care.

    Usual diets also have common problems, such as too much salt and ultra-processed food and too little fiber.

    Supplementing is normal.

    Iodized salt, fortified flour and the supplementation of farm animals themselves show that fortifying and supplementing are part of many diets.

    Difficulty isn't impossibility.

    That some people plan poorly doesn't show the diet is inadequate for those who plan well.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Even if a vegan diet required more attention, the question would remain the same: does the effort of planning one's meals justify breeding and killing animals that feel? Health can correct facts about diet, but it doesn't decide whether it's just to exploit someone.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that fruitarianism is a risky diet, which can cause serious deficiencies and is unsuitable for children. Vegans shouldn't promote it as a model or use the idea that humans are "frugivores by nature".

But

But veganism isn't fruitarianism. A varied, well-planned vegan diet with B12 is considered adequate by nutrition bodies. And even if it required more care, that wouldn't answer the question of what we do to animals.
