G1

"Eating animals is necessary to survive"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about health and nutrition, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on health grounds, anyone who manages to be healthy eating animals, and many people do, would have no reason to change. And if an animal product turned out to be demonstrably healthier, the same argument would start defending exploitation. The eater's health says nothing about whether what is done to the animal is just.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the word "necessary" only means something with a complement: necessary for whom, where and when. For most people who say it, it isn't: they are choosing between products in a shop, and the alternative is on the same shelf. For people living where the alternative doesn't exist, it genuinely is, and in that case nobody is being accused of anything — the standard definition of veganism already says "as far as is possible and practicable". The argument swaps a real necessity, belonging to a few, for a general justification, used by many. And that is exactly why it turns against whoever uses it: if necessity justifies, the absence of necessity condemns.

Note: this chapter is about necessity. The specific nutrients are in G2, G3 and G5, and the extreme hypothetical is F1

Short answer

Necessary for whom? Whoever says it is usually standing in a supermarket with beans, lentils, chickpeas, rice, nuts and tofu on the next shelf. That isn't necessity, it's preference. Where the alternative really doesn't exist, it is necessity, and nobody is accusing those people: the definition of veganism itself speaks of doing what is possible and practicable. The argument takes the exception and uses it as a general permission.

Question back: "If one day it became easy and cheap to live without using animals, would you stop? Because for you that day has already arrived."

For children

In some very cold or very poor places, people have no other food, and then they really do need it. But in a shop full of rice, beans, lentils, chickpeas and nuts, nobody needs it: you can choose. And when you can choose without hurting anyone, that's the right choice.

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Human beings have always eaten meat. That's how we survive. Nobody lasts without it."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Causing harm is justifiable when it is necessary for survival.
  2. P2Eating animals is necessary for human survival.
  3. CSo eating animals is justifiable.

P1 is accepted by practically everyone, including those who defend animals. The problem is P2, which is false for most people and true for some, and the argument depends on applying to everyone what holds only for those few.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2. And where P2 is true, the conclusion was already accepted: nobody holds that a person should starve.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    Everything depends on who is speaking. The same sentence is true in one person's mouth and false in another's.

    SituationIs it necessary?What follows
    A person with access to a shop, a market or a varied food basketNoThe choice exists, and it needs justifying
    A person in food insecurity, eating whatever turns upIt isn't a moral choice of theirs, it's a lack of optionsThe problem is poverty, and blaming them is unfair
    Arctic peoples and communities without viable agricultureYes, todayNobody proposes otherwise
    A rare medical condition that rules out a planned plant-based dietDepends on the case, and is between the person and their clinicianOutside what is being discussed here

    The argument rolls all of this into one sentence, and uses the last two rows to release the first. It is the same move as the desert-island case (F1): a scenario where the rule genuinely doesn't apply is used to dispense with the rule where it does.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The exception doesn't become the rule.

    That someone, somewhere, needs it says nothing about those who don't. It is like saying drink-driving is acceptable because somebody once drove drunk to get an injured person to hospital. Necessity justifies where it exists, and only there.

    2. Veganism already has that clause written in.

    The most-used definition, from the Vegan Society, speaks of excluding animal exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable". It isn't a concession made under pressure: it has been in the text from the start. Anyone raising the Arctic case is describing exactly the situation the definition already covers.

    3. Well-planned plant-based eating is considered adequate by major nutrition bodies.

    The position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics in the United States, and of other national bodies, is that well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets are appropriate for all stages of life, including pregnancy, lactation, childhood and old age, with attention to specific nutrients, above all vitamin B12. Some bodies are more cautious: Germany's DGE, in its 2024 review, makes no recommendation for pregnant women, children and older adults, judging the evidence insufficient — a caveat about the state of the research, not a claim that the diet is unworkable.

    4. The argument turns against whoever uses it.

    It concedes the principle: causing harm requires necessity. Anyone who accepts it has already accepted that, where there is no necessity, there is no justification. And for the person saying it in a supermarket, there isn't.

    5. "Surviving" gets swapped for "this is how we live".

    Nobody in a city with shops is at risk of death from not eating meat. What is at stake is habit, taste, convenience and identity — real things, but not things called survival.

    6. Where the necessity is real, the target is someone else.

    A person who eats whatever turns up because they have no alternative isn't who sustains the industry, and blaming them is wrong on its own account (J2). The argument ends up using the situation of the poorest to protect the consumption of those who do have a choice.

    7. History doesn't establish present necessity.

    That the species ate animals over the course of evolution explains how we got here, not what is obligatory now. Otherwise any old practice would be necessary (C4, D1).

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The best reply: necessity isn't only biological

    The serious version isn't about immediate survival. It says "necessary" includes what is feasible in a real person's life: price, time, access, food culture, health conditions, family support. For many people a planned plant-based diet is theoretically possible and practically difficult, and demanding it would be demanding what they have no way of delivering.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It changes the thesis, and for the better.

    It moves from "eating animals is necessary" to "for some people giving it up is hard", which is true and already in the definition.

    Difficulty comes in degrees, and so does the answer.

    Someone who can't do everything can do part, and that counts.

    It doesn't apply to those who invoke it most.

    The objection usually comes from people with access and choice, on behalf of people who have neither.

    What it asks for is changing the conditions, not the principle.

    If the obstacle is price and access, the conclusion is to make the alternative accessible, not to declare the harm justified.

Contradictions

It accepts that vegetarians exist and are alive.

Which already refutes universal necessity.

It picks the cut, the brand and the price.

Behaviour of someone with options, not of someone without.

It says it's necessary and eats for pleasure.

It praises the taste, not the survival.

It agrees that causing harm needs justification.

Which is exactly the principle that condemns it when the necessity isn't there.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets right something that can't be brushed aside: there are people and peoples for whom the alternative doesn't exist, because of climate, geography, income or lack of access, and demanding of them what is demanded of someone in a supermarket would be unfair and foolish. It also gets right that "possible" on paper and "possible" in someone's life aren't the same thing, and that a cause which ignores this forfeits the right to be taken seriously.

But

What it gets wrong is turning that truth into a general permission. The person saying the sentence is almost never the person the sentence is about. And the argument concedes the principle that undoes it: if harm needs necessity to be justified, then where there is no necessity, there is no justification.
