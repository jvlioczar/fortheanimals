I27

"Guide and assistance dogs are well cared for and do important work"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's true that many guide dogs are very well treated and that they help blind and disabled people gain independence. That's why this is a borderline case, and vegans disagree about it. What's at issue isn't affection for the dog, but planned breeding for the job, the fact that the dog doesn't choose, and what happens to those who don't qualify. And none of this works as an argument for factory farming: a hard case doesn't make the easy ones easy.

Short answer

Many guide dogs are very well treated and help blind people gain independence; it's a borderline case, and vegans disagree about it. The serious discussion is about planned breeding, the fact that the dog doesn't choose, and what happens to those who don't qualify: in a study of almost 11,000 dogs from a British school, about 4 in 10 were withdrawn from the program for behavior. And a hard case doesn't make factory farming acceptable, where none of this is at stake.

For children

A guide dog helps a person who can't see to walk down the street safely, and many are very loved. Even so, there are important questions: did the dog choose this job? And the puppies that can't become guide dogs, where do they go? You can love the guide dog and still ask these questions.

Ask a question back

"If a well-treated guide dog is a hard case, what does that say about a chicken who lives in a shed for 45 days?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"So vegans are against guide dogs too? The dog is treated like royalty and helps a blind person. That shows how extreme veganism is."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Guide and assistance dogs are well cared for and help disabled people.
  2. P2If veganism condemns even this, it's extreme.
  3. P3If veganism accepts this, it accepts using animals.
  4. CSo either veganism is extreme, or using animals can be acceptable, and then so can eating them.

P1 is true in most cases. The problem lies in the dilemma: veganism doesn't need to condemn every guide dog to be consistent, and accepting a hard case doesn't license the easy ones.

The argument is a false dilemma, and the final conclusion is a leap.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The objection uses a borderline case to try to bring down the central one:

    QuestionGuide dogFactory farming
    Is there an important benefit for a human?Yes, independence for disabled peopleTaste, for those who have alternatives
    Is the animal treated well?Usually, yesUsually, no
    Is the animal killed at the end of its use?NoYes, young
    Are there alternatives?Cane, technology, human guides, with limitsYes, for most people
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's a false dilemma.

    Veganism speaks of avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable". Cases where there is a person's real need and genuine care for the animal are discussed with nuance, not condemned wholesale (L12).

    2. The real questions exist and deserve to be said.

    The dogs come from breeding programs, with females carrying several litters, and the dog doesn't choose the job. In a study of 10,968 dogs from Guide Dogs, in the United Kingdom, born between 2000 and 2011, about 4 in 10 were withdrawn from the program for behavioral reasons. These dogs are usually moved to other roles or adopted.

    3. No killing or confinement is involved.

    A guide dog lives with the person, is cared for and isn't killed at the end of its service. The comparison with animal farming shows the difference, not a similarity.

    4. A borderline case doesn't decide the central case.

    Even if guide dogs were fully acceptable, that would say nothing about breeding and killing billions of animals for food when there are alternatives.

    5. The motive behind the objection is usually something else.

    The question "are vegans against guide dogs?" rarely comes from concern for guide dogs; it serves to make veganism look absurd and end the conversation.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the dog enjoys the work

    The most serious version says that guide dogs are trained with positive reinforcement, bond with their person, seem to enjoy the routine and have a better life than many stray dogs.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It may be right about many dogs.

    And that's why the case is borderline, not an example of serious exploitation.

    But it doesn't answer the questions about breeding and the withdrawn dogs.

    The welfare of those who work says nothing about those who didn't qualify.

    And it doesn't apply to animal farming.

    None of these factors, bond, care, a long life, exists in breeding for slaughter.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The guide dog case shows exactly the criterion veganism uses: real need, the animal's interests taken into account, no killing for convenience. Applied to breeding animals for food, the same criterion gives the opposite answer.

Contradictions

Uses the care for guide dogs as an argument.

And accepts the lack of care for the animals they eat.

Values the guide dog's long life.

And accepts that farmed animals are killed at weeks or months old.

Accuses veganism of being extreme.

And demands from it a purity they demand from no other ethics.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that guide and assistance dogs make a huge difference in disabled people's lives, that many are loved and well cared for, and that vegans disagree about this case. It's also true that the question deserves an honest answer, not an automatic "yes, it's exploitation".

But

But a borderline case, discussed carefully, doesn't dissolve the central question: is it fair to breed and kill animals when there's no need?
