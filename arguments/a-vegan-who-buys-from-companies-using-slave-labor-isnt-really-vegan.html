L13

"A vegan who buys from companies using slave labor isn't really vegan"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the argument gets an important point right: veganism rests on not causing avoidable harm to sentient beings, and humans are sentient beings too. Vegans have the same reasons to avoid illegal products or products from companies convicted of slavery-like labor, as far as possible. But the argument gets the conclusion wrong: failing one duty doesn't cancel the other, and the same demand applies to non-vegans, who usually consume the same products and also eat animals. In Brazil, cattle farming is the activity with the most workers rescued from such conditions.

Short answer

You're right on one point: veganism is about not harming those who can feel, and humans can feel. Vegans have the same reasons to avoid products made with slave labor whenever they can. But failing at that doesn't make exploiting animals acceptable, and the same demand applies to non-vegans: in Brazil, cattle farming is the activity with the most workers rescued from slavery-like conditions. Avoiding the exploitation of animals and of humans are duties that go together.

For children

Someone who doesn't want to harm animals shouldn't want to harm people either, and that's right. Sometimes a piece of clothing or a food was made by people who were treated very unfairly. The right thing is to try to avoid both: harming animals and harming people. Getting one wrong doesn't make the other stop mattering.

Ask a question back

"If human exploitation is the problem, will you also avoid meat from farms caught using slave labor?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"A vegan who buys clothes from a brand caught using slave labor, or who buys drugs from traffickers, isn't vegan. They contribute to human exploitation all the same."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Veganism rests on the idea of not causing avoidable harm to sentient beings.
  2. P2Humans are sentient beings.
  3. P3Someone who buys products from companies convicted of slavery-like labor, or illegal products linked to violence, contributes to harming humans.
  4. CSo a vegan who does this is inconsistent, and their veganism doesn't count.

P1, P2 and P3 are true, which is why the argument deserves to be taken seriously. The conclusion, however, mixes two things: showing that someone is inconsistent doesn't show that their position is wrong, or that exploiting animals becomes acceptable.

The argument is valid as to the inconsistency. Its soundness fails in the step from "inconsistent" to "veganism doesn't count".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    What the argument saysWhat's trueWhat doesn't follow
    Veganism asks us not to harm sentient beingsHumans are sentient tooThat an inconsistent vegan should go back to exploiting animals
    Buying from companies using slave labor causes harmIt contributes to human exploitationThat non-vegans are exempt from this demand
    An inconsistent vegan "isn't vegan"The inconsistency exists and should be fixedThat veganism is wrong
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The starting point is right.

    If the reason not to exploit animals is that they can suffer, the same reason applies to humans. A consistent vegan should avoid, as far as possible, products of slavery-like labor and of supply chains linked to violence.

    2. Inconsistency doesn't refute the position.

    Showing that someone fails to follow a principle doesn't show that the principle is wrong. Someone who defends human rights and buys a product of slave labor is inconsistent, but that doesn't make human rights false.

    3. The two duties don't compete.

    Avoiding the exploitation of animals and of humans are parts of the same idea. Failing at one doesn't license abandoning the other.

    4. The demand applies to everyone.

    Non-vegans usually consume the same products, and also eat animals. According to data from Brazil's Observatory for the Eradication of Slave Labor, cattle farming is the activity with the most workers rescued from slavery-like conditions in the country.

    5. Knowing the origin is hard, but there are tools.

    Brazil's Ministry of Labor publishes the "Dirty List" twice a year, the register of employers caught using slavery-like labor. No one controls the whole supply chain, but you can avoid what you know about.

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: veganism is selective about human suffering

    The most serious version says some vegans mobilize a lot for animals and little for workers, and that this reveals an inverted hierarchy in which animals would matter more than people.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Some people's selectivity doesn't define the position.

    Consistent veganism includes all sentient beings, humans too.

    The criticism works as an invitation, not a refutation.

    It shows where vegans can improve, not that exploiting animals is right.

    Those who make the criticism are held to it too.

    Concern for workers is welcome, and should lead those who raise it to look at the meat they eat as well.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question is whether it's fair to exploit those who can suffer when there is an alternative, whether animal or human. The argument helps broaden that question, but doesn't answer it in favor of exploitation.

Contradictions

Holds the vegan to account for slave labor.

And doesn't look at the meat they eat.

Says humans matter.

And uses that to justify exploiting animals.

Demands total consistency.

And accepts inconsistency in their own consumption.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that veganism rests on not harming sentient beings, that humans are included, and that vegans should avoid products of slave labor and of supply chains linked to violence whenever possible.

But

But failing at that doesn't make exploiting animals acceptable, and the same demand applies to non-vegans. The two duties go together.
