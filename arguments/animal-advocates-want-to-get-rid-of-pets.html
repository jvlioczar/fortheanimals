L33

"Animal advocates want to get rid of pets"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the accusation mixes up different positions and swaps "stop breeding" for "take them out of homes". Many animal advocates live with rescued dogs and cats, and PETA, the organization most often named in this accusation, says on its own website that it doesn't oppose people who take good care of the animals they live with. What many reject is treating animals as property and breeding them for sale, because every animal bred for the trade competes for a home with those waiting in shelters. Some do argue for ending the breeding of dogs and cats, such as the legal scholar Gary Francione, but even that position asks that the animals who already exist be well cared for; other currents, such as that of Zoopolis, see domesticated animals as members of the community. And whatever the answer about companion animals, it says nothing about whether it's right to breed and kill animals for food.

Reply

Many animal advocates live with adopted dogs and cats, and not even PETA proposes taking animals out of homes. What many criticize is breeding for sale: every puppy bred for the trade competes for a home with those waiting in shelters, and in the United States alone, in 2024, about 748,000 dogs and cats left shelters dead or euthanized. Some argue for no longer breeding new pets, but even that position asks that the ones who already exist be well cared for. And whatever you think about that, it doesn't change what happens to the animals bred for food.

For children

Many people who stand up for animals have a dog or a cat at home, usually adopted from a shelter. What they don't think is right is making puppies and kittens be born just to be sold, while lots of animals are waiting for a family at shelters. Nobody wants to take your dog away from you: what they want is for every dog to have a home and be well cared for.

Ask a question back

"Is adopting a shelter dog and caring for them until the end of their life what you call getting rid of pets?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"These vegans want to take your dog away. Next they'll ban having a cat."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Those who defend animal rights want to end the companionship between people and pets.
  2. P2This companionship is good for humans and, often, for the animals themselves.
  3. P3A movement that wants to end something good doesn't deserve to be taken seriously.
  4. CSo the case for animal rights doesn't deserve to be taken seriously.

P1 has a true part: some theorists, such as Gary Francione and Anna Charlton, argue that we should stop bringing new domesticated animals into the world. But the sentence swaps "stop breeding new animals" for "take animals out of homes" and attributes to the whole movement the position of one part of it. P2 is plausible, as long as the animal's interests are met. The central problem lies in P3: even if a proposal about companion animals were bad, it would say nothing about what the movement claims about farming or laboratories.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1, which generalizes and distorts, and at P3, which judges all of a movement's theses by a single one.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The sentence lumps together positions that animal advocates themselves keep apart:

    What the sentence saysWhat animal advocates usually argue for
    "They want to take your dog"That animals already living in homes be well cared for; many advocates live with rescued animals
    "They're against having pets"The criticism is of the idea of the animal as property, which is why many prefer to speak of guardians rather than owners
    "They'll ban dogs"The laws the movement supports restrict the sale of animals bred for the trade, not companionship
    "They want to end domestic animals"It's the position of some abolitionists, and it's about no longer breeding new animals, not removing the ones who exist
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Many advocates live with adopted animals.

    The philosopher Nathan Nobis notes that the accusation comes from ignorance or manipulation: many animal advocates, including philosophers, live with companion animals and mention them in their own writings (Animals & Ethics 101, 2016, p. 69). Even Gary Francione and Anna Charlton, the authors of the most radical critique of pets, live with rescued dogs.

    2. The criticism is of property and trade, not of care.

    If an animal is property, the owner may generally do as they please with it, including killing it for no reason. That's what many advocates reject, because it assumes the animal's interests don't count in their own right (Nobis, 2016, p. 69; see A24). They also criticize breeding for sale, because each new animal takes the place of one waiting in a shelter who may never be adopted (pp. 69–70). In the United States, in 2024 alone, about 748,000 dogs and cats left shelters without their lives, according to Shelter Animals Count (see I33).

    3. Even the most radical position doesn't take animals out of homes.

    Francione and Charlton argue that we should stop bringing domesticated animals into the world, but care well for those who already exist. PETA says it opposes all breeding of pets, and also that it doesn't oppose people who live with animals they love, treat well and properly care for. A proposal about animals not yet born isn't a threat to those already living with someone.

    4. The movement isn't a bloc.

    Besides the abolitionists, there is the relational position of Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka, for whom domesticated animals can be members of the community, with rights, and companionship can be just if their interests come first. And there is the welfare-centred position, common among vets, which accepts responsible companionship and criticizes commercial breeding and breeds with health problems. The map of these positions is in I35.

    5. The laws the movement supports restrict trade, not companionship.

    In 2017, California passed a law, in force since 2019, requiring pet stores to sell only dogs, cats and rabbits from shelters or rescue organizations. The law was backed by animal protection groups, opposed by the American Kennel Club, and doesn't stop breeders from selling directly. It's the kind of change the movement usually asks for: less trade, more adoption.

    6. Even if it were true, the question about the other animals remains.

    If every animal advocate wanted pets to end, that would be a reason to discuss that proposal, not to ignore what they say about the pigs, chickens and fish bred for food.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the best-known organization wants pets to end and euthanizes animals

    The most serious version says: the accusation isn't made up. PETA states that it opposes all breeding of pets, and the figures it publishes itself show that its shelter in Virginia euthanizes more than a thousand animals a year and sends few to adoption. Francione and Charlton argue, in so many words, that we should stop breeding domesticated animals, which, taken all the way, would make dogs and cats disappear as companion animals. So "they want to get rid of pets" would be literally true of an influential part of the movement.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Ending breeding isn't taking animals out of homes.

    The abolitionist proposal is about the future, not a confiscation: if it were adopted, every animal who exists would live out their life with the person caring for them. Calling it "taking your dog" swaps one thesis for another.

    Shelter euthanasia is debated within the movement itself.

    Supporters of shelters that avoid euthanasia criticize PETA, and PETA replies that it takes in sick, old or aggressive animals that other shelters refuse, and that its mobile clinics have spayed or neutered more than 181,000 animals. Whatever one thinks of this policy, it belongs to one organization, not to the whole movement.

    Disagreeing with the abolitionists about pets doesn't refute animal rights.

    One can reject Francione's proposal, as Donaldson and Kymlicka do, and still think it's wrong to breed and kill animals for food.

    None of this touches the central question.

    Even if ending pets were everyone's goal, the question about animals bred for food, who are the vast majority of animals used, would still stand.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question of how we should live with dogs and cats is real and deserves discussion. But it isn't the question veganism puts on everyone's plate: is it just to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to? Adopting a dog and refusing a pig's meat aren't conflicting positions; they come from the same care.

Contradictions

Accuses animal advocates of wanting to take pets away.

And usually supports adoption and spay-and-neuter campaigns, which are exactly what they ask for.

Says the dog is part of the family.

But family isn't property, which is exactly the criticism many advocates make.

Defends the dog at home.

And doesn't ask why a pig, who feels and learns like a dog, can be bred to die.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that there is, within the movement, a position that argues for no longer breeding domesticated animals, and that the debate about domestication is real and difficult (I35). It's also right that some organizations have controversial policies, such as PETA's euthanasia policy, which are criticized even by other animal advocates.

But

But wanting no animal to be born to be sold isn't wanting to take animals from those who care for them. And no answer about companion animals decides what to do about the billions of animals bred for food.
