J9

"I swapped red meat for chicken, which is healthier"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the swap may make sense for the eater's health, and some health guidelines suggest replacing red meat with poultry or fish. But for animals it usually makes things worse. For the same amount of meat, swapping beef for chicken involves about 210 times more animals, and swapping pork for chicken about 55 times more; swapping for fish tends to raise the count even further (J11). The count measures individuals, not suffering, but the difference per animal would have to be huge to offset that factor. And even if each cow or pig suffered more than each chicken, that wouldn't justify eating only the large animals. The swap that is good for health and neutral for animals is a different one: replacing meat with legumes.

Short answer

For your health, the swap may make sense. But for animals it usually makes things worse. For the same amount of meat, swapping beef for chicken involves about 210 times more animals, and swapping pork for chicken about 55 times more. And broiler chickens live only about six weeks, often with serious welfare problems. If the idea is to eat more healthily, you can swap meat for beans, lentils or chickpeas without raising the number of animals killed.

For children

If someone stops eating beef and eats only chicken, many more animals die for the same amount of food: more than two hundred chickens in place of each cow. Each chicken is a little animal who feels. Swapping meat for beans, lentils or chickpeas doesn't need any animal at all.

Ask a question back

"Do you know how many chickens die to put on the plate the same amount of meat that comes from a single cow?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"I've stopped eating red meat. Now it's just chicken and turkey breast, which is lighter and healthier. I'm already doing my part."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1There is evidence that eating a lot of red and processed meat raises some health risks (see G17).
  2. P2Some health guidelines suggest replacing red meat with poultry or fish.
  3. P3Eating less red meat also cuts beef consumption, which has a large environmental impact.
  4. CSo swapping red meat for chicken is a good, responsible choice.

P1 has support, and P2 describes recommendations that exist. P3 is true for emissions. The problem lies in the conclusion: it looks at the eater's health and at the climate, and leaves out the number of animals killed, which rises sharply with the swap.

The argument is valid as a health argument. It fails as a moral justification, because the choice that looks lighter on the plate can be the heaviest for animals.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    What the argument saysWhat's trueWhat's left out
    Red meat is worse for your healthThere is evidence of risk with high intake of red and processed meatThe eater's health isn't the only thing at stake
    Chicken is the recommended swapSome guidelines suggest poultry or fish instead of red meatThey don't take the number of animals into account
    So the swap is responsibleIt may cut emissionsIt multiplies the number of animals killed
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The count starts from how much meat comes from each animal killed.

    According to Faunalytics data compiled by Our World in Data, and used by the philosopher Daniel Dorado in a paper published in Food Ethics in 2026, the average is about 364.4 kg of meat per cow, 96.1 kg per pig and 1.74 kg per chicken.

    2. Kilo for kilo, the swap multiplies the animals.

    With these figures, replacing beef with chicken involves about 210 times more animals, and replacing pork with chicken about 55 times more (rounding the chicken to 1.7 kg, as some presentations do, gives about 214 and 57). The ratios assume a complete swap, but even partial swaps add up quickly. Someone who eats 1 kg of beef consumes a small fraction of a cow; 1 kg of chicken is more than half a bird.

    3. Fish would make the count even larger.

    Dorado leaves fish out because sizes vary too much between species. Since far less meat comes from most fish eaten than from a cow or a pig, swapping red meat for fish tends to raise the number of animals even further (J11). Feed can also contain fishmeal, but poultry farming, once the largest consumer of fishmeal, now uses little of it, according to IFFO, the industry's international association; this effect weighs far more in fish and shrimp farming than in chicken farming.

    4. The count measures individuals, not suffering.

    A chicken and a cow don't go through the same things, and the number of animals doesn't say how much each one suffers. But broiler chickens aren't better off: they are slaughtered at about six weeks old (see B2), and the Welfare Footprint Project, which estimates animals' pain in each production system, documents serious welfare problems in broiler chickens, many linked to fast growth. And even if each cow's or pig's suffering were greater than each chicken's, that wouldn't make eating the large animals acceptable: it would only show that both choices cause harm, and that swapping one for the other doesn't solve it.

    5. The link between health advice and more chicken hasn't been tested.

    Dorado argues that messages like "cut down on red meat", without saying what to eat instead, may lead people to chicken, because "healthy" menus and products tend to feature chicken. But he himself says this causal pathway hasn't yet been studied directly. What is certain is the arithmetic: whoever makes the swap raises the number of animals.

    6. There is a swap that is good for health and neutral for animals.

    Dorado suggests that instead of recommending poultry in place of red meat, health advice could use neutral wording, such as "replace meat with legumes". Beans, lentils, chickpeas and peas provide protein without choosing which animal dies.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: a chicken doesn't suffer like a cow

    The most serious version says counting heads is misleading. A chicken would have a simpler mental life than a cow or a pig, and so would suffer less. If each chicken's suffering weighs much less, the swap may not be worse.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The difference would have to be enormous.

    For the swap from beef to chicken not to be worse, each chicken's suffering would have to weigh about 210 times less than each cow's. There's no evidence of a difference that large, and chickens feel pain and fear (see A5).

    Living conditions weigh against the chicken.

    Broiler chickens live for weeks, in sheds with thousands of birds, with health problems linked to accelerated growth. That doesn't suggest a better life than a cow's.

    Under uncertainty, prudence avoids multiplying victims.

    As Dorado argues, anyone who can't reliably compare suffering across species has reason to avoid choices that sharply increase the number of victims.

    The choice doesn't have to be between beef and chicken.

    Swapping for legumes addresses the health concern without this dilemma.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The swap shows a real concern for one's own health, and that is legitimate. But the moral question is a different one: who pays for the meal? When the answer is "dozens or hundreds more animals", the choice that seemed lighter becomes heavier. If the reason to change is health, there is a change that looks after health without raising the number of deaths.

Contradictions

And picks the swap that most increases the number of animals killed.

Says they've already done their part for animals.

And for them, the change was for the worse.

Accepts that food has consequences.

But only counts those that fall on the eater.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that there is evidence of risk in high intake of red and processed meat, that some health guidelines suggest replacing it with poultry or fish, and that cutting beef reduces emissions. Whoever made the swap acted out of a real concern for health.

But

But for animals, the swap to chicken almost always multiplies the number of deaths. The swap that serves health without exacting that price is the one to legumes. Genuinely eating less meat, without swapping it for smaller animals, is better than doing nothing, and can be a step toward veganism; but for the animals who are still killed, the harm is still terrible.
