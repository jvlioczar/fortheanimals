J14

"Fishers and ranchers would lose their identity"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

professional and cultural identities matter, and change can be painful. But an identity doesn't justify harm to others: many trades and ways of life have changed throughout history without their communities ceasing to exist. What the situation calls for is a just transition, with respect and support, not the indefinite continuation of the harm.

Short answer

Identities matter, and changing is hard. But an identity doesn't justify harm to others: many trades and ways of life have changed throughout history, and communities carried on, with their stories, festivals and bonds. What the situation calls for is a just transition, with respect and support for those who depend on these activities, not the indefinite continuation of killing animals.

For children

Some families have worked in fishing or with cattle for a long time, and it's part of their story. Changing is hard. But many families have changed jobs over time and stayed themselves. The important thing is to help those who need to change, with respect.

Ask a question back

"Should an identity built on harm to others be kept at any cost?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"My family has always lived from fishing. I'm a rancher's son."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1For many communities, fishing or ranching isn't just a job, but an identity and a way of life.
  2. P2Losing that identity causes suffering and social breakdown.
  3. P3Asking these communities to abandon their practices is disrespectful.
  4. CSo veganism threatens legitimate identities and should be rejected.

P1 and P2 are true. P3 has a point about how the subject is approached. The problem lies in the conclusion: an important identity doesn't make acceptable the harm it involves.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails because identity isn't a moral justification for harm.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument treats identity as a justification:

    ClaimStatus
    Identity mattersTrue
    Changing is painfulTrue
    So the harm tied to the identity is acceptableDoesn't follow
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Identity doesn't justify harm.

    Many historical identities included practices now rejected, and communities transformed.

    2. Identities change.

    Trades, traditions and ways of life transform over time, and people keep their history and their bonds.

    3. Those who use the argument often don't live from it.

    That doesn't invalidate it, but it's a reminder that for whoever buys the product, the question is still their own: what justifies the harm?

    4. The transition can preserve what matters.

    Knowledge of the land and sea, community bonds and cultural traditions can carry on in other activities.

    5. Respect isn't inaction.

    It's possible to respect people and argue for change with support and dialogue.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    ILO, *Guidelines for a just transition* (2015)Principles for supporting workers and communities in changing sectorsAdopted by the ILO Governing Body
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: traditional peoples have rights

    The most serious version points out that traditional communities, such as small-scale fishers, have recognized rights, and that imposing change on them repeats colonial patterns.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Veganism is addressed to those who can choose.

    Communities without alternatives are in a different situation (D3).

    The argument doesn't apply to the urban consumer.

    Someone buying meat at the supermarket has no such identity at stake.

    Change can be built together with communities.

    Without imposition.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The identity of those who work with animals deserves respect, but it doesn't answer the question about the animals. For those with a choice, the question remains: what justifies causing the death of other beings?

What the argument gets right

What is true

Identities tied to work and the land run deep, and change can cause real pain. Small-scale fishers and farmers often live in hard conditions, and deserve respect and support. The animal movement shouldn't treat them as enemies.

But

But respect for people is compatible with arguing for change. The question is how to make the transition fairly, not whether it should happen.
