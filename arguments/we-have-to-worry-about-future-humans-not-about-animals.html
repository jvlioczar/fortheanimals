N17

"We have to worry about future humans, not about animals"

The answer in one sentence

anyone who says this has already accepted the hard part, which is taking seriously beings who do not yet exist. What is missing is why, once that future arrives, only the humans in it would count. The reason a future harm matters — someone will feel it — does not sort by species. And by the numbers, most sentient beings the future will contain probably won't be human: today's decisions about technology, agricultural expansion, ecosystems and perhaps artificial minds will determine how many will exist and under what conditions.

Note: denying that future beings matter at all is N16. This chapter is about those who accept the future and leave animals out of it

Short answer

Anyone worried about future humans has already accepted the hard part: that beings who don't exist yet count. What's missing is why, when that future arrives, only one species in it would count. The reason a future harm matters is that someone will feel it, and that doesn't change with species. On top of that, most of those who will feel in the future probably won't be human.

Question back: "If you care about a child who'll be born a hundred years from now, but not about an animal who'll be born a hundred years from now, what changed: the time or the species?"

For children

When we take care of the planet, we're taking care of whoever will live on it later. And whoever lives on it later isn't only people: it's also the animals who haven't been born yet. They will feel heat, hunger and fear just the same. Leaving the world better means leaving it better for everyone who will feel.

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I care about the world I'm leaving my grandchildren. About the animals of the future, frankly, I don't."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1We have duties toward future generations.
  2. P2Those duties come from a specific relationship: they are our descendants, they will inherit our institutions, they will carry on our projects.
  3. P3Future animals are not part of that relationship.
  4. CSo concern for the future is concern for future humans.

P1 is true and already costs something to whoever accepts it. The problem is P2, which swaps the reason the future matters for something else, and P3, which only holds if P2 does.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2: if duties came only from lineage and institutional inheritance, we would have no duties toward future humans in other countries and cultures, and we do.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    This argument is speciesism carried across time. It accepts temporal impartiality and denies it in the same move, because it applies it only within one species. Two kinds of neglect add up, and the Senciência piece on temporal impartiality names both: speciesism leads people to consider only future humans, and temporal bias leads those who defend animals to ignore future animals. Whoever uses N17 has corrected the second and kept the first.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The reason given doesn't select a species.

    Saying that a future harm matters because someone will feel it is a reason about who feels, not about who descends from us. To leave animals out you would need a second premise the argument never states: that the interests of non-humans don't count. That premise is speciesism, and it has chapters of its own (A8, A10).

    2. The lineage criterion proves too much and too little.

    Almost everyone accepts that we have duties toward humans who will be born in other countries, in other cultures, with no relation to us. If the duty came from institutional inheritance or family ties, those cases would fall outside. Since they don't, the real reason is another one: they will feel.

    3. The numbers run the other way.

    If the future contains many sentient beings, most of them probably won't be human. Already today, the number of animals farmed, fished and living in the wild is orders of magnitude larger than the number of people. Current decisions about agricultural expansion, aquaculture, the occupation of ecosystems and eventually artificial sentient entities will set how many will exist and under what conditions.

    4. The same decisions affect both groups.

    Climate, land use, technology and institutions don't come with a separate switch for humans. A policy that protects the human future can multiply animal suffering, and the reverse. Pretending only one side exists doesn't spare you the choice: it only hides it.

    5. The veil of ignorance reaches both time and species.

    If you didn't know in what era or as which being you would be born, what rule would you choose? It is the same question that grounds temporal impartiality, and there is no way to answer it excluding non-humans without already assuming they don't count.

    6. The animal-advocacy version of the same mistake also exists.

    Anyone who defends animals and treats the distant future as irrelevant commits the other half of the neglect. The chapter cuts both ways.

  4. d

    The best reply: special duties

    The serious reply doesn't deny that future animals may count. It says we have special duties toward those who inherit our institutions, our language and our projects, as we do toward children and fellow citizens, and that these duties are stronger and more enforceable than general ones.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Special duties add, they don't subtract.

    Having an extra obligation toward your own children doesn't cancel the obligation not to harm strangers.

    They don't cover the case.

    The harm under discussion is caused by us; it isn't a failure to help. The prohibition on harming depends on no tie at all.

    The tie invoked doesn't exist yet.

    People born in 2200 are fellow citizens of no one alive today, and almost everyone still accepts duties toward them.

    It isn't used as a reply but as a cut.

    In practice it doesn't serve to weigh anything: it serves to take animals out of the count before the weighing starts.

Contradictions

It accepts duties toward humans distant in space.

While denying duties toward animals at the same kind of moral distance.

It worries about the climate.

Whose effects also fall, in far greater numbers, on animals.

It protects ecosystems with the future in mind.

And describes the animals living in them as irrelevant.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets right that the future should be taken seriously, which is no small thing: most people discount the future automatically, and whoever says this sentence has stopped doing that. It also gets right that special duties exist and matter, and that a political agenda needs focus or it turns into a list of good intentions.

But

What's missing is a single step. The reason that makes the future matter — there will be someone who feels — doesn't come with a species clause. Adding one takes an argument this whole chapter looks for and doesn't find, and it takes ignoring that most of those who will feel in the future probably won't be human.
