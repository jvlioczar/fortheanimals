L18

"You don't feel empathy for every animal in the world either"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

that's true, and no one does. But the question was never empathy. Empathy is great at making us notice others, yet it's limited and biased: it favors those who are close, similar and identifiable, and it fades in the face of large numbers. Where it fails, we use reason and principles, as we already do with millions of distant humans we've never seen. No one needs to cry over an animal to recognize that it shouldn't be hurt without need.

Short answer

I don't, and no one does. I don't feel empathy for each of the billions of people in the world either, and I still know I shouldn't harm any of them. Empathy helps us notice others, but it's biased and can't count: research shows it drops when the victims go from one to two. That's why we use principles. Mine is simple: don't cause suffering and death to those who feel, when it can be avoided. It doesn't require tears.

For children

You don't know every child in the world, and you don't feel sad for each one of them. Even so, you know you shouldn't hit any of them. You don't need to feel sorry for someone to know it's wrong to hurt them. It's the same with animals: we don't need to cry for all of them to know that none should be mistreated for no reason.

Ask a question back

"Do you need to feel sorry for a stranger to think it's wrong to harm them?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"You cry over a pig but not over a mosquito. And nobody can feel sorry for billions of animals. Your compassion is selective too."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1The defense of animals rests on empathy for their suffering.
  2. P2No one, not even vegans, feels empathy for all animals: it's selective and doesn't keep up with numbers.
  3. P3A morality based on a selective feeling is arbitrary and can't be demanded of those who feel differently.
  4. CSo there's no obligation to follow vegan morality.

P2 is true and well documented. The problem lies in P1: the defense of animals doesn't depend on feeling empathy for each of them. It rests on a principle (don't cause avoidable harm to those who feel), and principles exist precisely because feelings are selective.

The argument is valid, but not sound: P1 is false.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses what motivates us with what justifies a rule:

    QuestionAbout whatAnswer
    Do we feel empathy for all animals?About our psychologyNo, nor for all humans
    Do animals feel pain and have an interest in living?About themYes, and that doesn't depend on what we feel
    Should we avoid causing harm to those who feel?About what to doThat's the moral question, and empathy doesn't settle it
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The premise about empathy is true, and it applies to everyone.

    The psychologist Paul Slovic, of the University of Oregon, calls psychic numbing our difficulty in feeling more when the victims are many. In a 2014 study on donations to children, affect and donations were already lower for two children than for one. Empathy also favors our own group: a 2011 review gathers evidence that we respond less to the suffering of those we see as outsiders.

    2. That's why morality can't depend on it alone.

    The psychologist Paul Bloom, in his book Against Empathy (2016), compares empathy to a spotlight: it lights up one spot and leaves the rest in the dark, follows our biases and is insensitive to numbers. Bloom doesn't defend coldness; he defends compassion guided by reason. Some think he underrates the role of empathy, but the diagnosis of its limits is widely accepted.

    3. We already live this way with humans.

    No one feels empathy for every person in a distant country, and we still accept that none of them may be tortured or killed for convenience. Human rights, laws and professional codes are ways of protecting those who don't stir our feelings.

    4. The criterion is sentience, not sympathy.

    What gives a being an interest in not suffering is the capacity to feel, and that doesn't change according to whether it moves us. The difference between a pig and a mosquito, if there is one, should be discussed on the evidence of what each feels (A15, F9), not on the size of their eyes.

    5. Empathy's failure counts against the argument.

    If our feeling fades before billions, that's a sign it underestimates the problem. Insensitivity to numbers explains why it's so easy to ignore the scale of animal farming; it doesn't justify it.

    6. What's asked is abstention, not feeling.

    No one asks you to cry for every animal. What's asked is not to pay for it to be confined and killed. Ceasing to cause harm requires no emotion at all.

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: without empathy, reason moves no one

    The most serious version says: abstract principles are fine, but it's feeling that makes someone act. If not even vegans can feel for fish, insects or billions of chickens, the principle of equal consideration has no engine. And a rule no one feels is a rule no one will follow. Besides, reason without feeling can also go wrong and serve to justify coldness.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Motivation and justification are different things.

    It may be harder to act without feeling, but that doesn't change what is right. The difficulty of being moved by the billions of victims of a famine doesn't make the famine less serious.

    Empathy and reason work together.

    Empathy for one concrete animal can open the door; reason extends the conclusion to those we don't see. That's how we've widened protection among humans.

    We follow many rules without emotion.

    We pay taxes, respect queues and don't steal from strangers without needing to be moved.

    The risk of cold reason calls for more care, not less.

    The answer to that risk is to check the arguments, not to hand the decision to a feeling we know is biased.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The selectivity of empathy is a fact about us. The animal's pain is a fact about the animal, and it exists even when no one is moved. If the question is what we should do, what counts is what happens to each individual who feels: is it fair to make them suffer and die when we don't need to? That answer doesn't depend on our managing to cry for all of them.

Contradictions

Doesn't feel empathy for all humans.

And doesn't conclude they may harm the ones who don't move them.

Criticizes selective empathy and relies on it.

Eating pigs and protecting dogs is the selectivity the argument claims to condemn.

Accepts laws that protect strangers.

They exist because feeling doesn't reach everyone.

Demands from the vegan an emotion no one has.

And takes its absence as evidence against a rule that doesn't depend on emotion.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets the facts right. Anyone's empathy, vegan or not, is selective: it favors the animal with a name and a face, the mammal, the baby. Vegans who talk as if they felt the pain of every being on the planet exaggerate, and campaigns that depend only on emotion stay stuck on the most charismatic animals. The argument is also a reminder that a cause needs more than feeling to stand.

But

But it doesn't follow that animals may be used. What follows is that empathy is a poor starting point for deciding who matters, and that we need principles to cover what it doesn't reach. That's what we do with the humans we'll never see.
