C4

"We're at the top of the food chain"

The answer in one sentence

the sentence is false as ecology and irrelevant as morality. By the measure ecologists use, the average human sits at trophic level 2.21, the same as an anchovy and close to a pig, not at the top. And even if we were at the top, a position in a food chain describes who eats whom, not who has the right to do what.

Short answer

In ecology, trophic level measures what a creature eats. A 2013 study calculated the average human level at 2.21, the same as an anchovy and close to a pig, well below predators like orcas and lions. And even if we were at the top, that would describe a diet, not grant rights. Being higher in a food chain isn't being higher morally.

For children

In nature, each animal has a place based on what it eats: some eat plants and some eat other animals. But that isn't a ladder of who matters more. It's just a way of explaining who eats what. And when scientists measured it, they found that people, on average, eat so many plants that they end up in the middle of the ladder, close to a pig, not at the top.

Ask a question back

"If the top of the chain gave rights, would an orca have more rights than you?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"We're at the top of the food chain for a reason. We're predators."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Humans are at the top of the food chain.
  2. P2Whoever is at the top of the food chain may, naturally, feed on those below.
  3. CSo it's legitimate for humans to eat other animals.

P1 is false by the ecological measure, and P2 is an appeal to nature.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at both premises.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses two ideas of "top":

    IdeaWhat it meansMoral implication
    Trophic levelPosition in the food chain, measured by what one eatsNone: it describes a diet
    Hierarchy of valueWho is worth more or is in chargeIt's what the argument wants to conclude, without proof
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Humans aren't at the top.

    In 2013, researchers calculated the human trophic level for the first time using consumption data from 176 countries. The average was 2.21, on a scale where plants are at level 1 and top predators reach 5.5. It's the same level as an anchovy and close to a pig's. National values ranged from 2.04 to 2.57, and the authors concluded that humans should not be considered top predators.

    2. Trophic level isn't a hierarchy of value.

    Position in a food chain describes who eats whom. It says nothing about who has more value or more rights, just as being prey doesn't make anyone less important.

    3. It's an appeal to nature and to power.

    The idea that being "above" licenses using whoever is "below" is the same as might makes right (C6) and superiority (A2).

    4. Eating more meat raises the level, not the merit.

    The study showed the human trophic level has risen as meat consumption increased. Climbing the chain, in this sense, is just changing one's diet.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Bonhommeau et al., 2013, *PNAS*Average human trophic level of 2.21, equal to an anchovy's; national values between 2.04 and 2.57; humans aren't top predatorsData from 176 countries; checked in the abstract and in coverage
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the top is power, not diet

    The most serious version drops ecology and says "top of the food chain" is a metaphor for human dominance over nature: we can hunt, breed and control any species, and none can do that to us.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It becomes might makes right.

    Being able to dominate isn't a right to dominate (C6, A2).

    Power brings responsibility.

    Precisely because we can affect every other species, the question of how to use that power becomes more urgent.

    Among humans, we reject the criterion.

    No one accepts that the most powerful have the right to exploit the weakest.

Absurd

Contradictions

Doesn't accept the strong exploiting the weak among humans.

Doesn't eat top predators.

The human diet is based on herbivores and plants.

Wouldn't give an orca more rights.

If the top of the chain gave rights, it would.

What the argument gets right

What is true

There's something true here: humans have unmatched power over other species and over ecosystems, and occupy a unique place in nature. The ecology of food chains is a useful tool for understanding that place.

But

But the same ecology shows we aren't at the top of the food chain, and ethics shows power isn't a title to rights. What sets us apart isn't what we can eat, but the capacity to ask whether we should.
