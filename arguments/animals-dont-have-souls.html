A4

"Animals don't have souls"

The answer in one sentence

the existence of a soul can't be publicly verified and, even in the traditions that affirm it, it doesn't authorize causing suffering. The word "animal" itself comes from the Latin anima, soul; classical philosophy attributed souls to animals; and the great religions condemn cruelty. What matters morally, the capacity to suffer, exists with or without an immortal soul.

Note on tone: this chapter doesn't discuss whether God or the soul exists. It discusses whether belief in an exclusively human soul justifies causing suffering to other beings, and shows that religious traditions themselves usually answer no.

Short answer

No one can prove who has a soul and who doesn't, so that can't be the criterion for deciding whom we may make suffer. The word "animal" itself comes from the Latin anima, meaning soul. And religions usually condemn cruelty: the Catechism of the Catholic Church says "it is contrary to human dignity to cause animals to suffer or die needlessly". Finally, if an animal has no afterlife, this life is all it has, which makes taking it even more serious.

For children

Some people believe only humans have souls, others believe animals do too, and every family can talk about this. But there's one thing everyone can see: when a dog gets hurt, it feels pain, and when it's left alone, it can get sad. To know whether it's wrong to hurt someone, we don't need to know whether they have a soul. It's enough to know they feel. And almost all religions teach us to take good care of animals.

Ask a question back

"If only the soul matters, why does your own religion condemn cruelty to animals?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Animals don't have souls, so there's no problem killing them."

Strongest version (steelman). In its most elaborate theological form, inspired by Thomism:

  1. P1A being's full moral value comes from having a rational, immortal soul, created by God.
  2. P2Only humans have a rational, immortal soul.
  3. C1So only humans have full moral value.
  4. P3Beings without full moral value were entrusted by God to human use.
  5. C2So using animals for food and other purposes is permitted.

There's also a Cartesian version, not religious in the same sense: without a soul (mind), animals would be complex machines that react but don't feel. That version denies sentience and is dealt with in A5.

The theological argument is valid. Its soundness problems are of three kinds: P1 and P2 can't be assessed by public criteria; "soul" is used in different senses; and P3 doesn't authorize what is practiced today, not even according to the religious traditions that hold it.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    "Soul" has at least three senses, and the argument only seems to work when they're mixed together.

    Sense of "soul"What happens to P2What happens to P1
    Principle of life and sensation (Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas)False: in this tradition, animals have a sensitive soul, which perceives, desires and feelsLoses its meaning: having a soul doesn't separate humans from animals
    Mind or consciousness (popular usage)False in light of the evidence of sentience (see A5)Comes close to sentientism
    Rational, immortal soul (theology)Can't be verified or refuted by public evidenceDoesn't explain why the pain of a being without immortality wouldn't matter

    In the Aristotelian tradition, which shaped medieval Christian thought, there are three levels of soul: nutritive (plants), sensitive (animals) and rational (humans). In other words, the philosophy that gave rise to the argument attributed souls to animals. What it denied was the rational soul.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The premise can't be examined by everyone (P1 and P2).

    Who has an immortal soul is a matter of faith, not shared evidence, and traditions disagree among themselves. In Jainism and strands of Hinduism, all living beings have a spiritual principle; in Buddhism, rebirth crosses species. Choosing one specific theology's answer to authorize harm to others is special pleading. This isn't an attack on faith: it's the demand that a criterion used to justify suffering be accessible to those who don't share the same belief.

    2. Even in traditions that affirm a uniquely human soul, cruelty is condemned.

    The table in (c) shows that Christianity, Judaism, Islam, Hinduism and Buddhism have explicit teachings against making animals suffer. Those who use A4 usually attribute to religion a license it doesn't grant.

    3. Immortality isn't what makes pain bad.

    Pain is bad while it's felt, here and now. Having or not having an afterlife doesn't change what an animal feels in confinement or at slaughter. There's even reason to think the opposite: if there's no compensation after death, this life is the only one the animal has, and the harm of taking or ruining it is final. C. S. Lewis, one of the most widely read Christian apologists of the 20th century, treated animal suffering as a serious problem precisely for this reason (The Problem of Pain, 1940).

    4. The criterion ends up reduced to species.

    In practice, the rational soul is attributed to every human and denied to every animal, regardless of each one's capacities. So "having a soul" works as another name for "being human", and the argument becomes A10 ("they're just animals") in theological vocabulary.

    5. The Cartesian version is empirically refuted.

    The idea that soulless animals would be automata that don't feel has been abandoned by neuroscience and veterinary medicine (see A1, A3 and A5).

  3. c

    Data and sources: what the traditions say

    The table doesn't claim religions are vegan. It shows they don't authorize unnecessary suffering, and honestly records where they allow the use of animals.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: human dignity and religious permission

    The most solid version doesn't say animals don't matter. It says humans have a unique dignity, grounded in the rational soul and the image of God, and that God permitted the use of animals, provided there's no cruelty. On this reading, eating meat would be compatible with faith, and veganism would demand more than religion does.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The permission's own limit condemns current practice.

    The permission comes with the condition of not causing unnecessary suffering. When there are food alternatives and the dominant system involves confinement, mutilation and slaughter on an industrial scale, most of that suffering is unnecessary by the tradition's own standard.

    Dignity creates duties, not license.

    In the Abrahamic traditions, humans receive animals as stewards caring on another's behalf, not as absolute owners. Laudato Si' itself rejects absolute domination. There's even a strand of Christian theology that defends animals on the basis of faith (Linzey, 1994).

    Permission isn't obligation.

    Even if religion permits eating meat, none requires it. Choosing not to cause suffering remains available within all these traditions.

    Faith and sentientism are compatible.

    It's possible to believe only humans have immortal souls and, at the same time, recognize that animal suffering matters. There's no contradiction between the two beliefs; the contradiction lies in condemning cruelty while funding it when it can be avoided.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Equivocation
"Soul" alternates between principle of life, mind and immortal soul
Non sequitur
From "has no immortal soul" it concludes "its suffering doesn't matter"
Begging the question
The soul is attributed exactly to the members of the species one wants to protect
Special pleading
One specific theology is treated as a valid criterion for everyone

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Unfalsifiable premise
No possible evidence could show who has a soul
Selective reading of texts
The permission to use is quoted and the prohibitions on cruelty are omitted
Not knowing one's own tradition
Ignores that Aristotle and Thomas Aquinas attributed a sensitive soul to animals

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Irrelevant criterion
Immortality has no bearing on the pain felt now
Dignity as license
Turns a special position into permission to harm
Lack of public justification
Justifies harm to others with a reason that only holds for those who share the belief

Biases

Psychological essentialism

The tendency to think categories have an invisible essence makes the "soul" seem a natural boundary between humans and animals

Medin and Ortony, 1989; Gelman, 2003

Motivated reasoning

Religion is searched for a justification of a practice one already wants to keep

General literature on motivated cognition

Confirmation bias in reading texts

The passages that permit use are remembered and those that condemn cruelty are forgotten

A known pattern in interpreting authoritative texts

Deference to authority

"Religion says so" closes the analysis before it starts

A known pattern of appeal to authority

Motivated denial of mind

Denying a soul to eaten animals is a way of denying them a mind

Bastian et al., 2012; replicated by Jacobs et al., 2024

Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "whoever lacks a (rational) soul may be used".

If it applied to…Peoples considered "without reason" or "without a full soul"Tap to see

It would be enough to claim a human group lacks a full rational soul to justify using them. Since possession of a soul can't be verified, those with power decide whom it belongs to

In the 16th century, some argued that the Indigenous peoples of the Americas should be treated "as dumb brutes created for our service". The bull Sublimis Deus (1537) replied that they were "truly men" and could not be enslaved

If it applied to…Human physical sufferingTap to see

Bodily pain would be morally secondary. If value lies only in the immortal soul, and the soul isn't hurt by physical pain, relieving a sick person's pain would matter less than it seems

Religious traditions do the opposite and treat care for the suffering body as a duty

If it applied to…Different religious traditionsTap to see

Each religion could impose its own answer about souls on everyone. If the soul decides, a Jain could require everyone to treat insects as ensouled beings, with the same right as those who deny souls to cattle

Shows why the criterion for authorizing harm must be public, not dependent on a specific faith

If it applied to…Conscious beings without a recognized soulTap to see

Sentient extraterrestrials or a conscious artificial intelligence could be tortured. If they lack the soul a certain tradition attributes to humans, their suffering wouldn't count

Whoever feels this would be wrong is appealing to sentience, not to the soul

Contradictions

Condemns cruelty to animals.

Almost everyone who uses A4 thinks it wrong to torture an animal, and so do their religions. That only makes sense if animal suffering matters, soul or no soul.

Treats their own pet differently.

Many people who say animals have no souls hope to meet their dog or cat again "in a better place" or speak of its "good soul", but don't extend that idea to the pig or the cow.

Uses the religion of care to dispense with care.

The traditions speak of responsibility and compassion; the argument uses them to close the conversation about responsibility.

Doesn't apply the criterion to the human body.

If only the immortal soul mattered, human physical pain would also be secondary. Yet the same religions run hospitals and teach the relief of bodily suffering.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The argument expresses an intuition that deserves respect: that there's something special about human life and that we have a responsibility no other animal has. For billions of people, this intuition is lived through faith, and an honest book needn't attack it. It's also true that the ultimate nature of consciousness remains an open question in philosophy and science: no one can fully explain why subjective experience exists.

But

But none of these points leads to the conclusion that we may make animals suffer needlessly. The special responsibility traditions attribute to humans is, in almost all of them, a responsibility to care. And uncertainty about the nature of consciousness doesn't erase the evidence that animals feel.
