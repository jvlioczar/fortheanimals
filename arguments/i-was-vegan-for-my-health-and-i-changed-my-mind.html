M2

"I was vegan for my health, and I changed my mind"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the experience of someone who tried to be vegan and gave up, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism depended on being easy for everyone, one person's difficulty would be enough to make it stop counting. But someone's difficulty, which deserves to be heard and helped, doesn't change what happens to the animals: it calls for practical support, not a new moral conclusion.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

someone who adopted a plant-based diet for health or for the environment is right on one point: if that was the only reason and the person changed their mind about the facts, no reason was left. That is why indirect arguments are fragile. But there is more than one path. Many people arrive with several reasons, and the animals may be among them; what tends to vary is how much weight that reason carries and whether it stays strong over time. For veganism, the reason concerning animals is the center, and it is the one that does not depend on any nutrition study. In the 2014 Faunalytics survey, health was the only motivation cited by a majority of those who gave up (58%), which is compatible with the idea that, for many, the reasons tied to animals were not the strongest or did not last.

Short answer

That makes sense: if the reason was only health and you changed your mind, the diet lost its footing. It may also be that you had more than one reason and that the one about animals existed but was not the strongest, or lost strength over time. That is human and common. Veganism itself is the reason about animals, and it does not change with revisions in nutrition. On the facts: a well-planned plant-based diet is adequate, according to the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics in the United States, but it isn't a miracle cure or the only way to be healthy. If the diet no longer convinces you today, it is worth asking what is left of the reason about animals and whether there is something of it you want to keep.

For children

Imagine someone who stopped pulling the cat's tail because they heard that cats bring bad luck. One day they find out the bad luck story is nonsense. Can they go back to pulling the cat's tail? No, because the real reason was always something else: it hurts the cat. With animals that become food it's similar. Even if a person changes their mind about health, the animals' reason is still there.

Ask a question back

"Besides health, did the animals ever become part of your reasons? How did that reason hold up over time?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I went vegan because of a health documentary. Then I read more and saw it wasn't quite like that. It was never about the animals. Now I eat everything, in balance."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1I adopted a plant-based diet because I believed it was the healthiest (or the best for the environment).
  2. P2After informing myself better, I changed my mind about those facts.
  3. P3Without that reason, there is no other reason to keep the diet.
  4. CSo I have no reason to continue.

P1 and P2 are sincere accounts, and P2 may even be partly correct: many health promises made in the name of veganism are exaggerated. The problem lies in P3: there may be a reason that was left out of the count, the animals, and health facts do not refute it. In some cases it was never examined; in others it existed and was one of several reasons, but it was not strong enough or lost strength over time.

The argument is valid, but not sound for everyone: P3 is only true if the reason concerning animals did not exist or stopped existing, and that is a question for each person that health facts do not answer.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The same plate can have very different reasons behind it, and they hold up differently:

    Reason for the dietWhat it depends onWhat knocks it down
    HealthNutrition studies and how the person feelsA new study, a new fad diet, feeling unwell (M1)
    EnvironmentCalculations of emissions and land useThe belief that local or "regenerative" meat solves it (H12)
    AnimalsTheir feeling pain and having a life to loseOnly proof that they don't feel, which doesn't exist (A1)
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The argument is coherent, and it shows how fragile indirect reasons are.

    Someone who stops eating animals only for a benefit to themselves can turn back when the benefit looks smaller. This guide keeps indirect arguments separate for exactly that reason: health and the environment are good additional motives, but they aren't the heart of the matter.

    2. A diet and veganism can look the same.

    Veganism is the refusal, as far as is possible and practicable, to exploit animals: in food, clothing, entertainment and testing (L24). Someone who adopted a plant-based diet only for health or for the environment was following a diet, and that is not a criticism: it is a description. Someone who had more than one reason, including the animals, was on the path to veganism, even if that was the weakest of their reasons. In that case the person may have left the diet without re-examining whether the reason about animals still stood. So "I used to be vegan and I know what it's like" (M10) describes, depending on the case, the experience of a diet or of a conviction that did not last.

    3. The data fit the account.

    In the 2014 Faunalytics survey, the only motivation cited by a majority of former vegetarians and vegans was health (58%). Among those who continued, five motivations passed the halfway mark: health (69%), animal protection (68%), disgust at meat (63%), the environment (59%) and taste (52%). In a 2013 study of 292 vegetarians, published in the journal Appetite by Hoffman and colleagues, those with ethical motivation had stronger conviction and had kept the diet longer than those motivated by health. There is a caveat: in a 2022 Faunalytics study that followed 222 people for six months, the type of main motivation made no difference to success over that period. What counted most was the level of commitment.

    4. The health facts, with no exaggeration either way.

    The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics in the United States stated in 2016 that well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets are healthful and adequate at all stages of life. In its 2025 update, which covers only adults who aren't pregnant or lactating, it maintained that these patterns can be nutritionally adequate and offer long-term benefits. "Well planned" includes supplementing B12. None of this makes a plant-based diet a cure for everything, and no one needs it in order to be healthy: anyone who heard those promises in a documentary is right to be wary (L26). The mistake is to conclude the opposite from the exaggeration (G16).

    5. The environmental facts.

    Buying local meat changes little: transport accounts for less than 10% of emissions for most foods. In the data compiled by Our World in Data, the lowest-impact beef emits about 9 kg of CO2 equivalent per 100 g of protein, against 0.8 kg for the highest-impact peas (H12).

    6. The reason about animals does not depend on health facts.

    A diet can be swapped when the science changes. The animal's situation in the slaughterhouse doesn't change with any nutrition review. Someone who had this reason as one among several, or as a reason that lost strength, can reassess its weight now, apart from the others.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: I thought about the animals and wasn't convinced

    The most serious version says: it's not that I never thought about it. I considered the argument about animals and it didn't convince me. My reason was health, that's legitimate, and no one can force me to have another.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's legitimate as far as the diet goes.

    No one should keep a health diet they have stopped believing in. But in that case the conversation moves elsewhere: it becomes about the animals, and the chapters are different ones (A1, B2, B1, B3).

    "I wasn't convinced" calls for an argument.

    Animals feel pain and fear. For that not to count, one has to point to a relevant difference between their suffering and ours. Species alone isn't that difference.

    Changing your mind about nutrition doesn't change the facts about animals.

    The two questions are independent. You can think a plant-based diet isn't the healthiest and still conclude that one shouldn't kill someone who feels when there is an alternative.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Health and the environment are reasons about the one who eats and about the planet. The third party involved is the animal: they have a life that goes well or badly for them, and that life ends at slaughter, whether the meat is healthy or not, local or not. The moral question isn't "which diet is best for me?". It is: do I have a good enough reason to do this to someone who feels? Someone who had that reason but it grew weaker, or who had not yet considered it, can come back to it now, calmly.

Contradictions

The data show that the type of food weighs far more than the distance.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that a diet adopted for health should be judged by health, and that changing your mind in the face of new information is a sign of honesty. It's right that a lot of vegan outreach exaggerates: it promises cures, weight loss and boundless energy, and people who come in through those promises leave when they fail. That is a fair criticism of the movement: promoting veganism as a miracle diet produces people who give up.

But

But this shows the health reason was weak, not that every reason was. Veganism has at its center the reason about animals, the body of the one who is eaten, and it still stands for anyone who wants to examine or strengthen it.
