L30

"I'll eat double to make up for you"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it isn't an argument; it's an emotional reaction. Psychology calls it reactance: when people feel their freedom is threatened, they tend to do the opposite of what's asked. The phrase says nothing about the animals; it says something about the discomfort of whoever says it. And the one who pays the "make-up" isn't the vegan, it's the animal.

Short answer

That isn't an argument; it's a provocation. Psychology calls it reactance: when we feel our freedom is threatened, we tend to do the opposite. But the one who pays the "make-up" isn't me; it's the animals. If the goal is to annoy me, there are ways that don't involve anyone's death. And whenever you want to talk about the animals for real, I'm here.

For children

Sometimes, when someone tells us not to do something, we feel like doing it even more, just to provoke them. But in this case, the one who gets hurt isn't the person who asked, it's the animals. Provoking doesn't change what's right.

Ask a question back

"If it weren't to provoke me, what do you think of what happens to the animals?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I'll eat some bacon in your honor. For every vegan, one more steak."

Strongest version (steelman):

There's no strong version. The phrase is a provocation, not an argument. It can express:

  1. Irritationat feeling judged.
  2. Defenseof one's freedom of choice.
  3. Humorto ease an uncomfortable conversation.

None of these readings offers a reason to eat animals.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The phrase has no argumentative structure:

    What the phrase expressesWhat it says about the animals
    IrritationNothing
    Defense of freedomNothing
    HumorNothing
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It isn't an argument.

    There are no premises or conclusion about the moral question.

    2. It's reactance.

    Feeling one's freedom threatened produces a tendency to do the opposite of what's suggested.

    3. The victim is the animal.

    The provocation is aimed at the vegan, but the harm falls on the animals.

    4. It reveals discomfort.

    Feeling the need to "make up" for it shows the question bothered the person, which is a starting point for conversation.

    5. Do-gooder derogation explains part of the reaction.

    Those who feel judged tend to react against the group that seems to judge them (L1).

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Minson and Monin, 2012Meat eaters disparage vegetarians when they anticipate being judged by themHelps explain the reaction
    Brehm, theory of psychological reactance (1966)Threats to freedom produce a motivation to restore it, often by doing the oppositeAcademic Press; the link is to an open-access review by Steindl and colleagues (2015)
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: it's just a joke

    The most serious version says the phrase is only humor, with no real intention to eat more meat, and that taking it seriously shows a lack of humor.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    If it's a joke, it isn't an argument.

    And the moral question is still unanswered.

    Humor can close the conversation.

    Often, that's exactly what it's used for.

    The joke has a real target.

    The animals.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The provocation is a way of not talking. But the question remains: setting aside irritation with vegans, is it fair to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to?

Contradictions

And makes it a response to someone else.

By feeling the need to make up for it.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The phrase reveals something real: many people feel pressured or judged when the subject is meat, and react with humor or provocation. Animal advocates should understand this reaction and avoid feeding it.

But

But provocation isn't an answer. And when the irritation passes, the question about the animals is still there, waiting.
