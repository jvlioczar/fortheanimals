I29

"Buying a pedigree dog or cat doesn't hurt anyone"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

commercial breeding of dogs and cats treats the animals as a product: breeding females spend their lives producing litters, puppies are selected for looks, and several breeds have been shaped in ways that harm their health. A Royal Veterinary College study of more than 4,000 pugs found almost twice the odds of having a disorder in a year compared with other dogs, and 54 times the risk of obstructive airway syndrome. Meanwhile, shelters are full of animals waiting to be adopted.

Short answer

Commercial breeding treats dogs and cats as a product: breeding females spend their lives producing litters, and several breeds have been shaped in ways that harm their health. A Royal Veterinary College study of more than 4,000 pugs found almost twice the odds of having a disorder in a year and 54 times the risk of serious breathing problems compared with other dogs. Buying keeps that demand going, while shelters are full of animals waiting to be adopted.

For children

Some dogs were bred to have very flat little faces because people find it cute. But that face makes it hard for them to breathe. And the mother dogs at breeders often spend their lives having puppies. Meanwhile, many dogs and cats at shelters are waiting for a family.

Ask a question back

"If the animal is part of the family, why does it have to be chosen from a catalogue of breeds?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I bought from a responsible breeder, with a pedigree. Adopting is lovely, but I wanted a purebred. What's the harm?"

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Responsible breeders take good care of their animals.
  2. P2Breeds have predictable temperament and size, which helps people choose the right animal.
  3. P3Whoever buys gives the animal a good home.
  4. CSo buying a purebred animal doesn't hurt anyone.

P1 holds for some breeders. P2 holds in part. P3 is true for the animal bought, but it leaves out the breeding females, breed-related health problems and the animals waiting in shelters.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails by omission: it only looks at the animal that comes home.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument only looks at the animal that comes home:

    Who is affectedWhat happens
    The puppy boughtUsually gets a good home
    The breeding femaleSuccessive litters; in commercial breeding, a life in kennels
    Breeds with extreme traitsHealth problems tied to the selected looks
    Shelter animalsLeft without the home that went to the bought animal
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Selected looks come at a price.

    The Royal Veterinary College study, using veterinary records of 4,308 pugs and 21,835 dogs of other breeds in the UK, found about 1.9 times the odds of having at least one recorded disorder in a year, and about 54 times the risk of brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome. The authors concluded that the pug can no longer be considered a typical dog from a health perspective.

    2. The breeding female is the invisible part.

    In commercial breeding, females are used to produce litter after litter and replaced when they stop being productive. The buyer rarely sees them.

    3. "Responsible breeder" is a variable label.

    There are careful breeders, but there is no single standard, and the same demand that sustains the good ones sustains the bad ones.

    4. Buying competes with adopting.

    Each animal bought is one home fewer for a shelter animal, and shelters have dogs and cats of every size and temperament, including purebreds.

    5. The animal isn't a product.

    Choosing by looks, as from a catalogue, follows the same reasoning as breeding animals for consumption: the animal exists to satisfy a preference.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    O'Neill et al., 2022, *Canine Medicine and Genetics*Pugs had about 1.9 times the odds of a disorder in a year and about 54 times the risk of brachycephalic obstructive airway syndromeVetCompass program, Royal Veterinary College; 4,308 pugs and 21,835 other dogs
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: buying from an ethical breeder avoids the worst problems

    The most serious version says that ethical breeders run health tests, limit the number of litters, avoid extreme traits and take the animal back if the owner can't keep it.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It improves breeding, but doesn't answer the shelters.

    While animals wait to be adopted, breeding more to sell is still a choice.

    It doesn't reach breeds with extreme traits.

    No health test fixes a face selected to be flat.

    It still treats the animal as a product.

    The breeding happens because someone pays for a look.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question isn't whether the animal bought will be loved, but whether it's fair to breed animals to sell while others wait for a home, and to shape their bodies to the point of harming their health. Those who love animals can start at the shelter.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Omission
Considers only the animal bought

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Trust in the label
"Pedigree" and "responsible breeder" are taken as a guarantee of health and welfare

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Animal as product
The desired look outweighs health and the other lives involved

Biases

Baby schema

Infant-like traits, such as a short muzzle and big eyes, attract and hide the health cost

Glocker et al., 2009

Motivated reasoning

Wanting the breed makes the breeding female and the shelter invisible

General literature on motivated cognition

Contradictions

Says the animal is family.

And chooses it by breed, like a product.

Finds flat faces cute.

And wouldn't accept a trait that made a child's breathing hard.

Loves dogs and cats.

And ignores the ones waiting at the shelter.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that there are careful breeders, that the animal bought usually gets a good home, and that knowing an animal's temperament and size helps people choose well. It's also true that many shelters have rules that make adoption hard.

But

But none of these points answers the breeding females, the traits that harm health and the animals waiting at the shelter. Adopting solves all three at once.
