H3

"Plant monoculture destroys as much as industrial animal farming"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the environment and agriculture, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on environmental grounds, low-impact exploitation would be acceptable, even if it killed more sentient beings. And it is already being promoted: insect farming, "sustainable" hunting, "sustainable" leather. It is possible to reduce environmental impact and increase exploitation at the same time.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

monocultures cause real damage, but the argument gets the address wrong: most of the world's soy and a large share of its grain feed farmed animals, not vegans. Animal farming takes up about 80% of agricultural land and supplies 17% of calories. A plant-based diet would cut agricultural land by about 75%, cropland included. Comparing the worst plant agriculture with the best animal farming is an unfair comparison, and food sovereignty doesn't require raising animals.

Short answer

Monoculture does damage, I agree. But most of it exists to feed animals: about 77% of the world's soy becomes feed, and only about 7% becomes tofu, soy milk and similar foods. Animal farming uses about 80% of agricultural land and delivers 17% of calories. With a plant-based diet, the world would need about 75% less agricultural land, and less cropland too. A vegan world would have less soy, not more.

For children

Growing just one thing in a huge field can be bad for the land. That's true. But do you know who eats most of the soy and corn from those fields? The cows, pigs and chickens on farms. If people ate the plants directly, we would need to grow less, and there would be more room left for nature.

Ask a question back

"If eating plants directly would mean the world needs about 75% less agricultural land, would there be more or less monoculture than today?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Soy and corn monocultures destroy as much as industrial animal farming. A vegan world would be a soy desert. It's regenerative, local systems, with animals integrated into the crops, that look after the land."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Large-scale monocultures degrade soil, depend on pesticides and reduce biodiversity.
  2. P2A diet without animal products would depend even more on those monocultures.
  3. P3Regenerative, local systems that integrate animals and plants restore the soil and strengthen food sovereignty.
  4. CSo swapping animal farming for plants only swaps one harm for another, and the way out lies in systems with animals.

This is a typical argument from advocates of regenerative agriculture. P1 is true. The problem lies in P2, which the data contradict (animal farming is the biggest customer of monocultures), and in P3, which describes real local benefits but leaves out the land these systems need and how little food they produce.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2 and P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument compares the worst of one side with the best of the other:

    ComparisonWhat the argument doesFair comparison
    Worst plant system vs. best animal farmingIndustrial monoculture against a model regenerative farmNot done this way
    Typical vs. typicalLeft outCrops eaten directly against animal farming that depends on feed crops
    Best vs. bestLeft outPlant-based agroecology against regenerative animal farming

    In both fair comparisons, the plant-based diet uses less land.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Monoculture largely belongs to animal farming.

    According to Our World in Data, with 2018 data, 77% of the world's soy goes to animal feed (37% to poultry alone, about a fifth to pigs). Tofu, soy milk, tempeh and other direct foods add up to about 7%. With cereals the picture is similar: 41% become feed and 48% are eaten by people (H1).

    2. Fewer animals means less cropland.

    Animal farming takes up about 80% of the planet's agricultural land, counting pasture and feed crops, and supplies 17% of calories and 38% of protein. Based on the study by Poore and Nemecek (2018), Our World in Data calculates that, in a plant-based world, agricultural land would fall from 4.1 to 1 billion hectares, about 75%. Cropland would shrink too, because what is no longer grown for feed is more than what would newly be grown for people.

    3. The comparison is unfair.

    Putting the worst plant agriculture next to the best animal farming shows little. Chicken, pork and eggs almost always come from systems that depend on grain, and the regenerative farm in the photo accounts for a small fraction of what is eaten (B4, H11).

    4. Regenerative systems with animals use more land and don't scale.

    The report Grazed and Confused (2017), coordinated at the University of Oxford, calculated that solely grass-fed animals supply about 1 gram of protein per person per day worldwide, against 49 grams from plants. It also concluded that carbon sequestration from grazing is small, time-limited and reversible: even under very generous assumptions, it would offset 20% to 60% of the emissions of the grass-fed animals themselves (H8, H9).

    5. "Soy desert" describes today's world better.

    Today most soy and a large share of cereals are already grown to become feed. A vegan world would free up about three quarters of agricultural land, which could go back to being forest, savanna or native grassland.

    6. Food sovereignty doesn't require raising animals.

    The Declaration of Nyéléni (2007), a reference text for the movement, defines food sovereignty as the right of peoples to healthy and culturally appropriate food, produced by ecologically sound methods, and to define their own food systems. The declaration counts pastoralists and fisherfolk among producers, and the movement isn't vegan. But the definition is about who decides, not about using animals: beans, cassava, corn, vegetable gardens, orchards and agroforestry fit in it entirely, with green manures and compost in place of manure (H10).

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: where crops can't grow, animals are the autonomy of those who live there

    The most serious version speaks of dry, mountainous or very cold regions, and of traditional communities that live from their herds. There, animals turn grass into food, and asking those people to depend on grain brought in from outside would take their autonomy away.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It holds for those in that situation.

    The definition of veganism says "as far as is possible and practicable". Those who repeat the argument generally buy their food at a store and have alternatives (D7).

    These systems produce a small fraction.

    One gram of protein per person per day shows they can't sustain current meat consumption or serve as a model for cities (H9).

    The special case doesn't justify the general one.

    That a herder in a dryland depends on goats says nothing about supermarket factory-farmed chicken.

    The moral question remains.

    Even where there are few alternatives, it's worth seeking those that exist. Where there are many, the justification is missing.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Suppose it were proven that a farm with integrated animals looks after the soil better than any cropland. We would still have to answer for what happens to each animal there, raised to be killed. The discussion about monoculture and soil is important, and in it the numbers favor plants. But it treats animals as parts of a farming system. For those who feel, being part of a well-managed system doesn't change how it ends.

Contradictions

Criticizes monoculture and eats those who consume it.

Store-bought chicken, pork, eggs and milk depend on soy and corn.

Rejects monoculture, but not feed.

If the problem is monoculture, the first step would be to stop feeding animals with it.

Asks for food sovereignty with the system that uses the most land.

The more land per calorie, the less food each territory can produce for itself.

Doesn't apply the soil criterion to plants.

If what matters is the health of the land, plant-based agroecology should be the first option.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that large-scale monoculture does real damage: impoverished soil, pesticides, loss of biodiversity, concentration of land. It's right that a vegan plate can come from a bad farming system, and that vegans would do well to care more about that. It's also right that diversified, local systems have value, and that food sovereignty is a serious cause.

But

But the limit of the concession lies in the numbers: animal farming is the main reason so many monocultures exist, and the plant-based diet is the one that needs the least cropland. The path the argument itself asks for, with less monoculture and more well-tended land, runs through eating fewer animals, not more. And no farming system, however good, answers for what happens to them.
