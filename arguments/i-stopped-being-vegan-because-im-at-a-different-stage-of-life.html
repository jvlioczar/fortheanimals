M5

"I stopped being vegan because I'm at a different stage of life"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the experience of someone who tried to be vegan and gave up, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism depended on being easy for everyone, one person's difficulty would be enough to make it stop counting. But someone's difficulty, which deserves to be heard and helped, doesn't change what happens to the animals: it calls for practical support, not a new moral conclusion.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

stages of life change what is easy, but they don't change what happens to animals. A stage with less time, more stress or other priorities is a good reason to ask for practical help, simplify the menu or do whatever is possible, not to treat a moral question as if it were a passing fad. No other moral commitment expires because a person has entered a new stage.

Short answer

It makes sense that your life has changed, and that things are harder now. But the animals are in the same situation as before. If this stage has less time or more pressure, you can simplify: fewer recipes, more ready-made vegan food, and doing whatever is possible. A hard stage calls for adjustments, not for the question to stop mattering.

For children

When we grow up, our tastes change: toys, music, clothes. But some things don't change with age, like not hurting others. Animals still feel pain and fear, whatever stage of life we're in.

Ask a question back

"What changed in your life, and did it also change the weight the animals had for you? Can you adjust what became hard without giving up what is still important?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I was vegan at one point in my life. Now I'm at a different stage; I have other priorities."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1A person's priorities and availability change over the course of life.
  2. P2It's reasonable to adjust personal choices to the priorities and availability of each stage.
  3. P3Veganism is a personal choice, like any other.
  4. CSo it's reasonable to leave veganism when entering a new stage.

P1 is true. P2 holds for personal tastes and projects. The problem lies in P3: veganism is about what is done to others, not a personal taste. Choices that affect third parties don't expire with the stages of life.

The argument is valid, but not sound: P3 treats a moral question as a preference.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument mixes up two things that change in different ways:

    What changes with the stage of lifeWhat doesn't change
    Time, money, energyWhat happens to the animals
    Routine, family, workThe reasons that led the person to veganism
    What is easy or hardWhat is right or wrong
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Stages change tastes, not duties.

    No one would say they stopped paying child support, being honest or not mistreating their dog because they entered a new stage. When a choice affects third parties, a change of stage can change how it's carried out, but it doesn't cancel it.

    2. A hard stage calls for practical help.

    With less time, there are shortcuts: frozen food, packed meals, simple menus with rice, beans and vegetables. Real difficulty is a good reason to simplify, not to give up on everything.

    3. "It was a phase" treats the reasons as a fad.

    If the person became vegan because of the animals, the reasons are still the same. What changed was the willingness to act on them, and that deserves to be said honestly (E15).

    4. The most common cause is lack of support, not a change of values.

    In the Faunalytics survey, 63% of former vegetarians and vegans didn't like standing out from others, and 58% didn't see the diet as part of their identity. This suggests that many people give up because of social pressure and lack of a network, not because they started thinking differently.

    5. Doing less is better than doing nothing.

    If the current stage really doesn't allow full veganism, keeping up whatever is possible (E10) still spares animals, and leaves the door open to coming back.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Faunalytics, 201463% of former vegetarians and vegans didn't like standing out; 58% didn't see the diet as part of their identity; 84% weren't part of groupsSurvey conducted in 2014 in the United States

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: everyone has a limit, and no one should push themselves beyond it

    The most serious version says: there are stages of life, such as illness, grief, a small child or unemployment, when a person can barely take care of themselves. Demanding the same of them as before is cruel.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Recognizing a limit is different from abandoning the question.

    In a very hard stage, it makes sense to do less, without guilt. That doesn't make exploiting animals acceptable.

    Hard stages pass.

    The question is what to do when it passes: go back to what one did before, or keep the giving up as if it were permanent.

    The limit is rarely on the plate.

    In most cases, eating vegan takes little extra time when you have a simple menu.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Even if the current stage made everything harder, the question would remain the same: is it just to make animals suffer and die when it can be avoided? The answer doesn't depend on the stage of life of whoever is asking.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Equivocation between taste and duty
Treats a moral question as the preference of a stage
False dichotomy
All or nothing, without considering doing what's possible

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Forgetting the one affected
Talks about the eater's stage of life, not the animal

Biases

Social conformity

Going back to the norm relieves the discomfort of being different

Faunalytics, 2014

Status quo bias

The old habit seems like the natural state

Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988

Dissociation

Once they stop, the person goes back to not thinking about the animal

Benningstad and Kunst, 2020

Contradictions

Keeps other commitments at every stage.

Honesty, caring for one's children, not mistreating the dog.

The reasons are still the same.

But the action changes as if they had disappeared.

Says they've grown up.

But growing up usually means keeping commitments even when they get harder.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that life changes, that some stages are genuinely hard and that no one should be held to account as if they always had the same availability. It's also right that guilt alone doesn't help anyone change.

But

But the stage changes what is easy, not what is right. The good answer is to help the person do what's possible at the stage they're in, and to remind them that the door is still open.
