L23

"Vegans are against religion"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's a false generalization. There are vegans and vegetarians in every religion, and whole religious traditions teach not harming animals. Veganism answers a single question, how to treat animals, and so it is compatible with faith and with the absence of faith. The objection is right on one point: some vegans attack religions, and criticism of halal or kosher slaughter sometimes serves as a pretext for Islamophobia and antisemitism. That is wrong. The consistent criterion criticizes all slaughter, not only other people's.

Short answer

I have nothing against your faith, and neither does veganism. It only answers one question: how to treat animals. There are Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu and Buddhist vegans, and vegans with no religion. In India, 92% of Jains say they are vegetarian, because of a religious principle of nonviolence. If a vegan disrespected your faith, they were wrong, and that's on them.

For children

Some people who don't eat animals go to church, to the mosque, to the synagogue or to the temple. And some people who don't eat animals have no religion. Caring for animals goes with any of those choices. In some religions, not hurting animals is even one of the most important teachings.

Ask a question back

"Does your religion ask you to be indifferent to the suffering of animals, or does it ask for compassion?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Vegans are all atheists. You don't like Christians or Jesus, and you want to put veganism in the place of religion."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Some vegans treat faith with disdain and attack religious practices, such as ritual slaughter and the meals of holy days.
  2. P2Veganism proposes rules for living, talks about guilt and conversion, and so competes with religion.
  3. P3A movement hostile to my faith doesn't deserve a hearing from believers.
  4. CSo religious people can reject veganism.

P1 is true of some vegans. P2 confuses a moral position on one issue with a religion (L7). The bigger problem is the move from "some" to "veganism": millions of religious people don't eat animals precisely because of their faith.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2 and in the generalization of P1.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument treats veganism as if it were a competing religious worldview:

    QuestionDoes a religion answer it?Does veganism answer it?
    Does God exist? What comes after death?YesNo
    What is the meaning of life? How should we pray?YesNo
    How should we treat animals?Yes, each in its own wayYes

    Veganism and religions only meet on the third row. And there, many traditions reach answers close to the vegan one.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. There are vegans and vegetarians in every religion.

    A Pew Research Center survey published in 2021 found that 39% of adults in India say they are vegetarian: 92% of Jains, 59% of Sikhs, 44% of Hindus, 25% of Buddhists, 10% of Christians and 8% of Muslims. Outside India, the Seventh-day Adventist Church doesn't require it, but many of its members follow vegetarian or entirely plant-based diets.

    2. There are religious organizations dedicated to animals.

    Among Christians, the Christian Vegetarian Association and the UK-based Sarx; among Jews, the Jewish Vegetarian Society; among Muslims, the Vegan Muslim Initiative; among Buddhists, Dharma Voices for Animals. The Animal Interfaith Alliance brings together organizations of several faiths around the same issue. None of them sees a contradiction between believing and not eating animals.

    3. Veganism has no doctrine about God.

    The Vegan Society's definition speaks of excluding exploitation of and cruelty to animals, as far as is possible and practicable. There is nothing in it about religious beliefs. That's why veganism has room for believers and atheists, like any moral cause.

    4. Criticizing a practice isn't being against the faith.

    Traditions themselves change and debate their customs: the guide shows, in D10, D11 and D12, that Christianity, Judaism and Islam have teachings against the unnecessary suffering of animals, and that permitting meat is not requiring it.

    5. Selective criticism of religious slaughter is something else, and it's wrong.

    Bans on kosher slaughter have been a tool of antisemitic campaigns: Switzerland banned it in 1893, after a referendum preceded by an antisemitic campaign, and Germany banned it in 1933, three months after Hitler came to power. Today, a person who is outraged only by halal or kosher slaughter and eats meat from an ordinary slaughterhouse is using animals as a pretext. The debate over slaughter with or without stunning is legitimate, but the antispeciesist criterion goes beyond it: all slaughter kills an animal who didn't want to die (B1), and the majority's slaughter is what kills almost all of them.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: veganism demands changing practices my faith holds sacred

    The most serious version says: this isn't about atheism. In some traditions, sacrifice, the Passover lamb or the Feast of Sacrifice are part of worship. An ethic that condemns those practices condemns, in practice, the religion of those who keep them.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Disagreeing with a practice isn't rejecting the whole faith.

    Believers disagree among themselves about practices of their own tradition all the time, and remain believers. D12 covers this point in more detail.

    The disagreement applies to everyone, religious or not.

    Veganism asks the same question of the barbecue of someone with no religion. There is no religious target.

    Ritual consumption is a small part.

    Almost everything of animal origin that people eat comes from daily consumption, which none of the major religions requires.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The faith of the person asking and the lack of faith of the person answering change nothing for the animal. The animal feels pain and fear all the same. The moral question can be asked inside any religion and outside all of them: is it right to make someone who feels suffer and die, when it can be avoided? Many traditions have already answered no.

The same mistake

Contradictions

Says veganism is against religion.

But lives alongside whole religions that teach not harming animals.

Defends compassion as a religious value.

And refuses it when someone applies it to animals.

Doesn't say eating meat is an atheist thing.

Although there are also atheists who eat meat and attack religions.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that some vegans treat other people's faith with contempt, use sacred texts as a weapon or mock believers. That is disrespectful, and it pushes away people who would have, in their own religion, good reasons to care about animals. It's also right that criticism of halal and kosher slaughter is sometimes used by people who only want to attack Muslims and Jews, and that animal advocates need to refuse that company.

But

But this describes the mistakes of people, not veganism. Not harming animals is an ancient teaching of several religions, and a conclusion reached today by believers in all of them. People of faith don't have to choose between it and the animals.
