F7

"Oysters and mussels don't feel"

The answer in one sentence

the argument is partly plausible, and that is exactly why it confirms the criterion. Oysters and mussels have very simple nervous systems, with no centralized brain, and some philosophers who defend animals admit they may not feel. But by saying "they don't feel, so it's fine", the person accepts that what matters is sentience. And that criterion condemns eating every animal who does feel, including fish, octopuses and crabs.

Short answer

You may be right about oysters and mussels: they have very simple nervous systems, and even some animal advocates admit they may not feel. But by saying that, you accept that what matters is whether the animal feels. And that criterion condemns eating fish, octopuses, crabs, chickens and pigs, who do feel. The exception, if it exists, proves the rule.

For children

Some animals, like oysters and mussels, are very simple and may not feel pain. Scientists are still studying this. But if we decide that only those who feel matter, then we need to look after everyone who does feel, like fish, octopuses, chickens and pigs.

Ask a question back

"If what matters is whether the animal feels, why eat the ones who do?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Oysters have no central nervous system. Bivalves are almost plants."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Oysters and mussels have no centralized brain and have very simple nervous systems.
  2. P2Without that kind of system, they're unlikely to be sentient.
  3. P3If they aren't sentient, eating them causes no suffering.
  4. CSo eating oysters and mussels is acceptable.

P1 is true. P2 is plausible, but uncertain. P3 is true if P2 is. The most important point is that the argument accepts sentience as the criterion.

The argument is valid and perhaps sound for bivalves. But it can't be used to defend eating animals who feel, and the uncertainty about P2 is a reason for caution.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument accepts the sentience criterion, and that has consequences:

    AnimalEvidence of sentienceWhat the criterion implies
    Mammals and birdsStrongDon't eat
    FishStrong and growingDon't eat
    Octopuses and crabsStrong, recognized even in UK lawDon't eat
    Oysters and musselsWeak and uncertainPerhaps acceptable; caution advised
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It confirms the criterion.

    If the problem is the lack of sentience, sentience is what matters. That's exactly the criterion of veganism and animal ethics.

    2. The exception doesn't extend.

    Octopuses, squid, crabs and lobsters show strong evidence of sentience. In 2021, a London School of Economics report recommended treating them as sentient, and the United Kingdom included them in its animal sentience law in 2022.

    3. Uncertainty calls for caution.

    Bivalves have nerve ganglia and react to stimuli. The absence of strong evidence isn't strong evidence of absence. When in doubt, the cost of not eating them is very small.

    4. The exception doesn't cover the rest of the plate.

    Some people eat only bivalves for this reason, but most of the time the argument comes from people who eat many other animals, for whom it doesn't hold.

    5. Harvesting and farming bivalves also have effects.

    Collection and production can affect other animals and ecosystems, which should be taken into account.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: veganism should then accept bivalves

    The most serious version says that if bivalves probably don't feel, veganism should accept them, and that refusing them shows the movement is dogmatic.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Some animal advocates do accept them.

    Some philosophers and activists consider eating bivalves acceptable. The debate is honest and open.

    Caution isn't dogma.

    Faced with uncertainty, preferring not to take the risk is a reasonable position.

    Nothing changes for the other animals.

    Accepting bivalves or not doesn't affect the conclusion about animals who feel.

Contradictions

Accepts sentience as the criterion.

And eats clearly sentient animals.

Doesn't eat only bivalves.

The argument doesn't describe their own consumption.

Would ask for caution in other cases of uncertainty.

In medicine, safety, the environment.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The argument is largely right. Bivalves are genuinely hard cases, the science about them is uncertain, and some animal advocates admit they may not feel. Recognizing this is a sign of honesty and shows that veganism rests on criteria, not dogma.

But

And it's precisely that criterion that settles the rest. Whoever accepts that what matters is feeling already has the answer for every other animal on the plate.
