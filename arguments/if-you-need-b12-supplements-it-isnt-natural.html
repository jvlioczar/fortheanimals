G3

"If you need B12 supplements, it isn't natural"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about health and nutrition, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on health grounds, anyone who manages to be healthy eating animals, and many people do, would have no reason to change. And if an animal product turned out to be demonstrably healthier, the same argument would start defending exploitation. The eater's health says nothing about whether what is done to the animal is just.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

vitamin B12 is produced by bacteria, not by animals. Farmed pigs and chickens receive B12 in the vitamin premixes added to their feed, because they don't get it from plants. In cattle, B12 is produced by bacteria in the rumen, and cobalt is often added to the feed so this can happen. So much of the B12 meat eaters get also comes from supplementation, just indirectly. And "natural" is no criterion of health or morality: we use vaccines, glasses and treated water.

Short answer

B12 is produced by bacteria, not by animals. Farmed pigs and chickens receive B12 in their feed precisely because they don't get it from plants, and ruminants often receive cobalt so the bacteria in their stomachs can produce it. Much of the B12 meat eaters get also comes from supplementation, just indirectly. And "natural" isn't the criterion: we use vaccines, treated water, iodized salt and fortified flour without thinking this makes our lives wrong. Supplementing isn't a flaw in the diet; it's looking after your health, ideally with professional guidance.

For children

Vitamin B12 is made by tiny bacteria, not by animals. Farm animals also get this vitamin in their food, because plants don't have it. So people who eat meat also get the vitamin from a supplement, it just passes through the animal first. Taking the vitamin directly is simpler.

Ask a question back

"If the animals you eat get B12 in their feed, doesn't your diet also depend on a supplement?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"A diet that needs pills is wrong. No natural diet needs supplements."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Vitamin B12 is essential, and lacking it causes serious harm.
  2. P2Vegan diets don't provide B12 without supplements or fortified foods.
  3. P3A diet suited to the human species should provide all nutrients without supplements.
  4. CSo the vegan diet isn't suited to the human species.

P1 and P2 are true: vegans need a reliable source of B12. The problem lies in P3: no scientific criterion says a healthy diet must do without supplements, and the B12 in meat also comes, in large part, from supplementation.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument ignores where B12 comes from:

    WhoWhere the B12 comes from
    BacteriaThey produce B12
    Farmed pigs and poultryThey receive B12 in their feed
    Cattle and sheepRumen bacteria produce B12 from cobalt, which is often added to feed or mineral licks
    Meat eatersThey get the B12 the animals received
    VegansThey get B12 from supplements or fortified foods, directly
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. B12 comes from bacteria.

    Neither plants nor animals produce B12. Animals get it from bacteria, from soil, water or their own gut flora.

    2. Farmed animals receive supplements.

    In modern livestock farming, pigs and poultry receive B12 in the vitamin premixes in their feed, and ruminants often receive cobalt. Part of the B12 in meat is thus a supplement that has passed through an animal.

    3. "Natural" isn't a criterion.

    Chapter C1 shows that natural isn't synonymous with good. We use many life-saving technologies that aren't natural.

    4. Supplementing and fortifying are common in every diet.

    Salt is iodized; wheat and corn flour are fortified with iron and folic acid in many countries; many milks and cereals have added vitamins; farmed animals receive vitamin and mineral premixes in their feed. And much of what we eat is already a blend of ingredients. There's nothing wrong with supplementing, as long as it's to look after health, ideally with guidance from a doctor or dietitian. No one thinks iodized salt makes everyone's diet inadequate.

    5. Meat eaters also have B12 deficiency, but at a much lower rate.

    An estimated 10% to 15% of people over 60 are deficient, mainly because they absorb less B12 from food; that's why supplementation is recommended for them too. Among vegetarians and vegans, the deficiency found in studies ranged from 11% to 90%, depending on the criterion, at a time when many didn't supplement. The comparison shows both things: eating meat doesn't guarantee enough B12, and those who eat no animal products run a much greater risk if they don't take the supplement.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the risk of forgetting the supplement

    The most serious version grants all this, but says depending on a supplement creates a risk: whoever forgets or doesn't know can develop serious deficiencies, while meat eaters are protected without thinking about it.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The risk is solved with information.

    A cheap supplement or fortified foods solve the problem.

    Meat eaters can be deficient too.

    Meat is no guarantee, especially in older people.

    Manageable risks don't justify the harm.

    The inconvenience of remembering a supplement doesn't outweigh the animals' lives.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    B12 is the most serious point in the nutrition debate, and it has a simple solution. If a cheap supplement is enough to make raising and slaughtering animals unnecessary, the moral question remains: what justifies their suffering?

What the argument gets right

What is true

B12 is the point where the argument is most right: vegans need a reliable source of this vitamin, and ignoring that can cause serious harm. Animal advocates should always say so clearly.

But

But needing a supplement doesn't make a diet wrong. The B12 in meat also comes from supplements and bacteria, and the vegan solution is simple, cheap and safe.
