H20

"If everyone goes vegan, we'll have to clear forests to grow crops"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the environment and agriculture, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on environmental grounds, low-impact exploitation would be acceptable, even if it killed more sentient beings. And it is already being promoted: insect farming, "sustainable" hunting, "sustainable" leather. It is possible to reduce environmental impact and increase exploitation at the same time.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's the other way round. Most of the world's farmland is used to raise animals, as pasture or to grow feed, and that land produces a small share of the calories we eat. According to the largest study of food's impacts, by Poore and Nemecek (2018), animal products occupy 83% of farmland and provide 18% of calories and 37% of protein; a global diet without animal products would reduce farmland by about 76%. Eating plants directly needs far less land than feeding plants to animals and then eating them.

Short answer

It's the other way round. Animal products occupy 83% of the world's farmland and provide only 18% of the calories we eat, because much of what is grown becomes feed. A global diet without animal products would reduce farmland by about 76%, according to the study by Poore and Nemecek (2018). Eating plants directly frees up land instead of requiring more.

For children

To have meat, you have to grow lots of food for the animals to eat their whole lives, and leave lots of space for them too. If we eat the plants directly, we need much less land. That leaves room for forests to grow back.

Ask a question back

"If most soy becomes feed, who needs more land: the person who eats the soy or the person who eats the animal that ate it?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"If everyone goes vegan, there won't be enough land. They'll have to cut down the forest to grow all that soy and beans."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1A vegan diet depends on growing more plants for human consumption.
  2. P2Agriculture needs land, and agricultural expansion is one of the causes of deforestation.
  3. P3To feed billions of people on plants alone, new areas would have to be cleared.
  4. CSo mass veganism would cause more deforestation.

P1 and P2 are true. P3 is false: it ignores that most farmland is already used for animals today, including to grow their feed. Without animal farming, that land is no longer needed.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    Use of the world's farmlandShare of areaShare of caloriesShare of protein
    Animal products (pasture and feed)83%18%37%
    Plants for direct human consumption17%82%63%
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Most land already serves animals.

    Pastures and feed crops occupy most farmland. That's where the pressure on forests lies.

    2. Animals turn plants into food inefficiently.

    An animal eats far more plant calories over their life than they provide as meat, milk or eggs. That's why the same food takes much more land when it passes through an animal.

    3. A plant-based diet frees up land.

    According to Poore and Nemecek (2018), a global diet without animal products would reduce farmland by about 76%, an area equivalent to the United States, China, Australia and the European Union combined.

    4. Pasture on land unfit for crops doesn't change the math.

    Some pastures are on land that couldn't be farmed (see H9). But that land wouldn't need to be used: it could regenerate as native vegetation.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Poore and Nemecek, 2018, *Science*Animal products occupy 83% of farmland and provide 18% of calories; diets without animal products would reduce farmland by about 76%Dataset of about 38,000 farms in 119 countries
    Our World in Data, land useLivestock uses most of the world's agricultural land, between pastures and feed cropsSynthesis of FAO data
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the transition wouldn't be perfect

    The most serious version says that in practice, a shift to plant-based diets could come with more monocultures and new land cleared in some regions, even if total area fell.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The problem would be management, not land.

    If the land needed falls sharply, clearing new areas would be a political choice, not a necessity.

    Monocultures already exist, and mainly serve feed.

    Reducing demand for feed also reduces pressure for monocultures (see H3).

    The right comparison is with the current system.

    And the current system is the one that takes up the most land.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Even if a plant-based diet needed the same amount of land, the question about animals would remain: is it fair to breed and kill them when we can live well without doing so? The land argument doesn't answer that question, and it gets the facts wrong too.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that agriculture causes deforestation, that monocultures have serious impacts, and that a food transition needs to be well planned.

But

But most farmland already serves animals, and a plant-based diet would free up most of it. Veganism reduces pressure on forests rather than increasing it.
