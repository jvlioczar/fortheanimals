N14

"Sentientists support population control and antinatalism for wild animals"

The answer in one sentence

this is partly true: many sentientists judge that, in species where almost all young die soon after birth, fewer births may mean less suffering, and fertility control is already used in some populations. But there is no ready-made answer. Everything depends on which animals would be harmed and which would benefit, case by case: fewer prey can mean hunger for predators, and fewer animals of one species can favor another. That's why the serious position is to research before acting, not to go around applying a rule.

Note: this chapter is about birth control. Predation is N12, and parasitism is N13

Short answer

It's partly true: in species where almost all the young die soon, many sentientists judge that fewer births may mean less suffering, and fertility control without killing is already used in some populations, such as the wild horses of Assateague Island, in the United States. But there's no ready-made answer: everything depends on which animals would be harmed and which would benefit in each case. That's why the serious position is to research before acting.

For children

In nature, many animals have hundreds of babies, and almost all of them die soon from hunger, cold or illness. Some people think that if fewer babies were born, fewer animals would suffer. But nature is full of connections: changing one thing changes others. That's why we need to study very carefully before deciding anything.

Ask a question back

"If reducing a population can either lessen or increase suffering, isn't that exactly why it has to be studied case by case?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"These sentientists want to sterilize the animals in the forest. They're antinatalists: they think it's better for animals not to be born at all."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Sentientists claim that, in the wild, most animals die soon after birth, suffering.
  2. P2If being born mostly leads to suffering, they conclude it's better for fewer animals to be born.
  3. P3A position that wants less life in nature is antinatalist and absurd.
  4. CSo sentientism should be rejected.

P1 describes a real, well-grounded thesis about population dynamics. P2 is a conclusion some authors draw, with caveats. P3 treats an empirical, case-by-case question as if it were a general doctrine.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3: the serious position isn't "less life, always", but "assess who is harmed and who benefits".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The objection lumps together three different things:

    IdeaWhat it isWho holds it
    Many species produce far more young than surviveA fact of population dynamicsBiologists and ethicists
    In some cases, fewer births may reduce sufferingA hypothesis to assess case by caseSome sentientist authors
    All wild life should endA general doctrinePractically no one in the debate
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The premise about nature is serious.

    In many species, each female produces hundreds or thousands of young, and in a stable population only a few reach adulthood. The philosopher Oscar Horta argued in 2010 that, for this reason, suffering and early death are the rule rather than the exception (N1).

    2. Controlling fertility isn't killing.

    Wildlife contraception is already practiced. On Assateague Island, in the United States, experimental use of a contraceptive vaccine (PZP) on wild mares began in 1988 and went on to stabilize the population without culling.

    3. But the result depends on who is affected.

    Fewer young of one species can mean fewer animals dying of hunger and disease, and also less food for predators, which may then go hungry, or more room for other species, with their own young. The right question isn't "less or more life?", but "which animals would be harmed and which would benefit?".

    4. That's why the serious position is one of research.

    The proposal of a welfare biology exists precisely to measure these effects before acting at scale. What's defended today are forms of help with known effects: vaccinating, treating the injured, rescuing in disasters, controlling fertility in small, well-studied populations.

    5. "Antinatalist" is an imprecise label.

    Philosophical antinatalism is the thesis that coming into existence is always a harm. Someone who assesses case by case whether a smaller population would suffer less doesn't have to accept that thesis.

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: tampering with wild species' reproduction is arrogant and risky

    The most serious version says that ecosystems are too complex for us to predict the effects of reducing a population, and that history is full of interventions that went wrong.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It reinforces the answer.

    That's exactly why the serious position calls for research and small cases before any action at scale (N6).

    We intervene all the time already.

    Cities, farming, hunting and "pest" control change wild populations every day, without asking about the animals' suffering. The difference is taking it into account.

    The risk doesn't make the question illegitimate.

    Asking which animals would suffer more or less is the opposite of acting blindly.

Logical

Is the form valid?

Straw man

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Oversimplifying complex systems
Assumes the effect of reducing a population is obvious, for good or ill

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Omission bias
Not acting seems neutral, even in the face of much avoidable suffering

Contradictions

Accepts population control when it serves humans.

"Management" hunting, pest control and spaying stray dogs and cats are seen as normal.

Supports spaying strays.

It's birth control to reduce suffering, by the same reasoning.

Says nature is too complex to intervene in.

And accepts huge interventions when they bring economic benefit.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that some sentientists conclude that, in certain cases, fewer births would mean less suffering, and that this conclusion sounds strange at first. It's also true that intervening in wild animals' reproduction carries serious risks, and that ecosystems are complex.

But

But that calls for what the serious position already proposes: assessing case by case which animals would be harmed and which would benefit, researching first, and starting with forms of help whose effects are known. There's no ready-made answer, either for intervening or for never intervening.
