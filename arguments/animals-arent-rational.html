A15

"Animals aren't rational"

The answer in one sentence

rationality isn't what makes someone capable of being harmed. The argument fails on three levels: it uses a criterion morally irrelevant to what's at stake (suffering), it's inaccurate about animal minds and, applied consistently, it would strip moral protection from millions of human beings.

Short answer

The moral question isn't whether a being can reason, but whether it can suffer. A pig's pain doesn't hurt less because the pig can't solve equations. If rationality were the criterion, babies and people with profound intellectual disabilities could also be used, and no one accepts that. Besides, the divide "rational humans, irrational animals" is false: crows make hooks to get food, and fish work out social hierarchies just by watching other fish.

For children

Imagine your friend falls and scrapes their knee. They cry because it hurts, not because they can do sums. It's the same with a dog, a pig or a chicken: they also feel pain, fear and joy, even though they can't read. A baby can't do sums yet either, and no one thinks it's right to hurt them. To know whether we can hurt someone, the question isn't "are they clever?" but "can they feel?".

Ask a question back

"If there were a human being as little rational as a pig, would it be acceptable to raise them for food? If not, what really protects that human?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Animals aren't rational, so there's no problem using them."

Strongest version (steelman). In the philosophical tradition, the argument comes from Aristotle, Thomas Aquinas, Descartes and Kant; today it appears, for example, in the contractualism of Peter Carruthers. Charitably reconstructed, it goes like this:

  1. P1Only rational beings (capable of abstract reasoning, deliberation and self-reflection) have full moral status, that is, interests that create direct duties for us.
  2. P2Non-human animals aren't rational in this sense.
  3. C1So non-human animals don't have full moral status.
  4. P3If a being lacks full moral status, it's permissible to use it for important human ends, provided there's no gratuitous cruelty.
  5. C2So it's permissible to use animals for food, clothing and research.

There's also a moderate version: rationality wouldn't be a condition for having some moral status, but would give human interests much greater weight. Both versions are dealt with in this chapter.

The argument is valid: if the premises were true, the conclusions would follow. The problem lies in its soundness: P1, P2 and P3 are false or lack sufficient justification.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    As we've seen, the argument is formally valid. So it has to be assessed premise by premise. Three points give way: the normative criterion (P1), the empirical claim (P2) and the bridge between status and use (P3). Before them comes an earlier problem: the word "rational" is ambiguous, and the ambiguity creates a dilemma.

    The dilemma of ambiguity. "Rational" can have two senses:

    • Strong sense: abstract reasoning, propositional language, the ability to reflect on one's own reasons.
    • Weak sense: learning, making inferences, solving new problems and adjusting behavior to goals.

    Rational in what sense?

    • Strong: abstract reasoningP2 becomes true, but P1 excludes babies and people with profound intellectual disabilities
    • Weak: learning, inferring, solving problemsP1 now includes those humans, but P2 becomes false for many animals

    There's no sense of "rational" that makes P1 and P2 true at the same time without excluding humans whom the speaker protects. Those who use the argument usually switch between the two senses without noticing: the strong one to exclude animals, the weak one to include all humans.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The criterion is irrelevant to what's at stake (P1).

    A characteristic only justifies different treatment if it relates to the interest affected. Requiring political understanding in order to vote can be debated; requiring political understanding in order not to be beaten is absurd. Rationality is relevant to some interests, such as political autonomy and moral responsibility, but not to the interest in not feeling pain, fear or deprivation. Jeremy Bentham summed this up in 1789: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

    From here comes the sentientist foundation. Having interests means being able to be affected, for better or worse, from the point of view of whoever lives the experience. A stone has no interests; a pig does. That's why sentience is a necessary and sufficient condition for moral inclusion, and the principle of equal consideration of interests (Singer) requires that similar pains count similarly. The criterion is also the most parsimonious: a single trait explains why we protect adults, babies, people with dementia and, already today, dogs and cats.

    2. Species overlap (P1 against humans themselves).

    The argument is old: it appears in Porphyry, in the third century, and in Bentham. Today it's called the argument from species overlap (Horta, 2014), a name replacing the older "argument from marginal cases". In the form of modus tollens:

    1. Q1. If rationality is necessary for full moral status, then humans without rationality don't have full moral status.
    2. Q2. Humans without rationality (newborns, people with profound intellectual disabilities, people with advanced dementia) have full moral status.
    3. C. So rationality isn't necessary for full moral status.

    Q2 is accepted by the speaker. The argument doesn't propose downgrading these humans: it shows the criterion is wrong and that the moral community should be widened, not narrowed (Naconecy). The usual escape routes have their own chapters: the "species norm" (A18) judges individuals by what others have; "potential" (A19) doesn't cover those who will never have rationality; "special relationships" (A12) make protection depend on someone caring.

    3. The empirical premise is a caricature (P2).

    As early as 1871, Darwin held that the mental difference between humans and other animals is one of degree, not kind. Research in animal cognition has confirmed this gradual picture (see the table in c). An honest caveat is needed here: citing animal intelligence helps undo the false "rational versus irrational" divide, but using it as the foundation is a trap. It would mean accepting the speaker's criterion and concluding that chickens or fish, supposedly less intelligent, matter less. The argument against exploitation doesn't depend on animals being intelligent; it depends on their being sentient.

    4. The bridge between status and use doesn't hold (P3).

    Even in the moderate version, where rationality would give human interests more weight, the argument doesn't reach its conclusion. First, that extra weight would have to be enormous to outweigh intense, prolonged suffering, plus death, in exchange for taste and convenience, since there are alternatives (J5). Second, the "important human ends" of P3 are, in practice, mostly preferences, not needs. Third, in the Kantian version, duties toward animals are only indirect: cruelty would be wrong because it hardens human character. It follows that torturing a dog in secret, with no effect on anyone, wouldn't be wrong in itself, a conclusion almost no one accepts.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: contractualism

    The philosophically strongest version comes from contractualism, defended on this subject by Peter Carruthers (1992). Morality would be the set of rules that rational agents would accept in order to live together. Only those who can understand, accept and follow rules are party to the contract. Animals would be left out and would have, at most, indirect protection, because cruelty reveals bad character. Non-rational humans would be included for two reasons: the contractors care about them, and excluding them would create social insecurity, since anyone could come to lose their rationality. A close variant, Carl Cohen's, grants rights through membership of a species of moral agents and is dealt with in chapter A18.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply also fails

    It confuses who makes the rules with whom the rules protect.

    Moral agents are those who can have duties; moral patients are those who can be harmed. Babies are patients without being agents, and no one concludes that their protection is therefore a favor.

    It makes the protection of vulnerable humans contingent.

    If a baby is protected only because the contractors care about them, then torturing an orphan nobody cares about wouldn't be wrong in itself. The theory fails precisely the test of our firmest moral judgments.

    The contract's own device points the other way.

    Behind Rawls's veil of ignorance, rules are chosen without knowing what position one will occupy. Mark Rowlands (1997) observed that if we also didn't know whether we'd be human or some other sentient being, we'd choose rules protecting all sentient beings. Excluding animals only appears when the contract is designed by those who already have power, and power isn't justice (A9).

    Even if accepted, it doesn't justify the real practice.

    Contractualism itself condemns cruelty; large-scale farming and slaughter systems, with confinement and mutilation, would hardly pass even that minimal filter.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Equivocation
"Rational" changes meaning between P1 and P2
Non sequitur (irrelevance)
From the absence of abstract reasoning, permission to cause pain is inferred, though pain doesn't depend on that trait
Ad hoc criterion
The group to protect (humans) is chosen first and then the trait separating it, adjusting the criterion whenever a counterexample appears
Proving too much
If valid, it also authorizes using humans without rationality

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

False dichotomy
Treats as binary (rational or irrational) something the evidence shows to be gradual
Outdated science
Relies on the Cartesian image of the animal-machine, abandoned by neuroscience
Reversed burden of proof and misapplied parsimony
Assumes no mind until proven otherwise, when evolutionary continuity and shared neuroanatomy make the hypothesis of mind the simplest
Anthropocentric tests
Measures "rationality" with tasks designed for humans and ignores each species' own capacities

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Criterion irrelevant to the interest
Uses a trait linked to autonomy to decide about pain and death
Violating equal consideration
Weighs similar pains differently because of species
Only indirect duties
Makes cruelty wrong only because of its effect on humans, not because of the victim

Biases

Motivated denial of mind (meat paradox)

Those about to eat an animal tend to attribute less mind to it, which reduces moral discomfort

Bastian et al., 2012; effect replicated, somewhat smaller, by Jacobs et al., 2024

Effect of consumption on moral status

Eating meat leads to attributing less moral status to the animal eaten

Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010

Functional categorization

Classifying an animal as "food" reduces the perception that it suffers

Bratanova, Loughnan and Bastian, 2011

Motivated reasoning

A trait is sought that justifies a practice one already wants to keep

General literature on motivated cognition

Self-serving bias

The criterion chosen is exactly the one adult humans score best on

A known pattern in judgments of desert

Anthropocentric measurement

Judges animal minds by human tasks, not by each species' capacities

Frans de Waal, Are We Smart Enough to Know How Smart Animals Are? (2016)

Extrapolations

The universalization test applies the principle "whoever isn't rational may be used" to other cases. If the person rejects the consequences, they must abandon or reformulate the principle.

If it applied to…NewbornsTap to see

They could undergo painful procedures without anesthesia. A newborn doesn't reason, speak or reflect on themselves. If protection from pain depends on rationality, they'd have no right to it, and the fact that they feel pain would be irrelevant

Not just hypothetical: until the 1980s, surgery on newborns was performed with minimal anesthesia in the belief that they didn't really feel pain, a practice revised after Anand and Hickey (1987)

If it applied to…People with profound intellectual disabilities or advanced dementiaTap to see

They could be used as test subjects or organ sources. These people will never have, or have lost, the rationality the criterion demands. The criterion offers no reason to protect them, and the care we give them would stop being a duty and become a favor

A conclusion rejected by practically everyone

If it applied to…Hierarchies among humansTap to see

The appeal to capacities wouldn't only decide who deserves respect, but also how much. If what matters is rationality or intelligence, and it varies in degree, there'd be a moral scale among humans: a person with a doctorate would be worth more than someone who can barely read, and their interests would take priority in a conflict

This is exactly what happened: Aristotle attributed incomplete reason to "natural slaves" and women, and colonial discourse called whole peoples "irrational" to justify domination

If it applied to…Artificial intelligence or extraterrestrialsTap to see

A being far more rational than us could use us as we use animals. If superior rationality gives the right to use the less rational, the distance between us and that being would justify the same treatment we give a pig today, and we'd have nothing to complain about

Whoever rejects this conclusion is, in practice, appealing to sentience

If it applied to…Cruelty in secretTap to see

Torturing an animal without witnesses would only be bad because of the habit it creates. In the Kantian version, the animal isn't a victim: the duty is only to humanity. If no one knew and the torturer went on treating people well, there'd be nothing wrong at all

Contradicts the common intuition that the wrong lies in what is done to the victim

Contradictions

They protect dogs and cats

, which are no more rational than pigs, and are outraged at cruelty to them. Laws in the UK, the US, the EU and Brazil punish cruelty to animals, which presupposes that their suffering matters in itself.

They grant full rights to babies

and to people with profound intellectual disabilities, without requiring rationality of them.

They reject degrees of rationality among humans.

No one accepts that a Nobel Prize winner has more right not to be tortured than an ordinary person. If rationality doesn't grade protection within the species, it remains to be explained why it eliminates protection outside it.

They say animals "understand nothing"

, but treat their own dog as someone who feels longing, fear and joy.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Rationality does have moral importance, but elsewhere. It's relevant to goods such as autonomy, political rights and responsibility: animals aren't moral agents, and a lion commits no crime by hunting. Cognitive differences can also change what harms each being; a being with long-term plans may lose more by dying (McMahan's time-relative interest account, 2002). Finally, equal consideration isn't identical treatment: a pig doesn't need the right to vote.

But

What the argument can't show is the decisive step: rationality can adjust the content of our duties, but it doesn't decide who is in the moral circle. That role belongs to sentience.
