I28

"Sport hunting funds conservation"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the money sport hunting provides for conservation is smaller and more unevenly spread than claimed. In trophy hunting, the most visible form of sport hunting, a 2013 report on Africa, citing research by pro-hunting bodies, found that hunting companies passed on about 3% of their revenue to communities. In the United States, where licenses and taxes on firearms and ammunition fund wildlife agencies, an estimate linked to the industry itself suggests only about a fifth of that tax comes from hunting purchases, and the money tends to favor hunted species. And even if the money did arrive, the argument instrumentalizes individuals: it kills an animal to protect the species, as if they were just a specimen.

Reply

Have you looked at how much of that money reaches conservation? It's less than it seems. In trophy hunting, a 2013 report on Africa, citing research by pro-hunting bodies, found that hunting companies passed on about 3% of their revenue to local communities. In the United States, most of the tax on firearms and ammunition that funds wildlife comes from purchases that aren't for hunting. And wildlife-watching tourism generates far more money and jobs without killing. But even if the money did arrive, would you accept killing an individual to save their species? That animal isn't a specimen: they have a life of their own, and it matters to them.

For children

Some people hunt for sport, and some pay lots of money to hunt lions or elephants and take the head home. They say the money protects the other animals. But little of that money reaches the people and parks that look after them. And animals can be protected through wildlife-watching tourism, without killing any.

Ask a question back

"If wildlife-watching tourism generates more income without killing, why would conservation need hunting?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"Hunters are the biggest conservationists. The license pays the park rangers."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Wildlife conservation needs money.
  2. P2Sport hunters pay for licenses and fees, and trophy hunters pay a great deal.
  3. P3That money funds habitat protection and the fight against poaching.
  4. CSo sport hunting, including trophy hunting, benefits conservation.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3: the evidence suggests that a small share of the money reaches conservation and communities, and there are ways of funding that don't involve killing.

The argument is valid if P3 is true. Its soundness fails because P3 is largely false.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument promises benefits the data don't confirm:

    PromiseWhat the data show
    The revenue funds conservationA small share reaches protected areas and communities
    Hunters' money sustains wildlife (the North American model)Licenses count, but most of the tax on firearms and ammunition comes from non-hunters, and the funds tend to favor hunted species
    Hunting is important for the economyThe revenue is a tiny fraction of the GDP of the countries studied
    Without hunting, animals lose their valueWildlife-watching tourism generates more revenue
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Little money reaches communities.

    The 2013 report The $200 million question cited research according to which hunting companies passed on about 3% of their revenue to local communities.

    2. The revenue is small for the economies.

    The same report estimated trophy hunting revenue as a very small fraction of the GDP of the African countries studied.

    3. Outside Africa, the numbers are also smaller than they seem.

    In the United States, sport hunting in general is often presented as the backbone of wildlife funding: state agencies receive license sales and a share of the federal tax on firearms, ammunition and archery equipment (the Pittman-Robertson Act of 1937). The money is real, but the tax applies to all sales, and an estimate by Southwick Associates, a consultancy that works for the industry, suggests only just over a fifth of it comes from hunting-related purchases; the rest comes from people who shoot for sport or buy guns for self-defense. Moreover, half of the money apportioned to the states is proportional to the number of hunting licenses sold, which, critics argue, encourages agencies to prioritize hunted species over non-hunted ones. A tax or fee on other activities could fund conservation without anyone having to kill.

    4. Hunting removes important individuals.

    Hunters seek the biggest males, with the largest horns or manes, which can affect the social and genetic structure of populations.

    5. Wildlife-watching tourism is an alternative.

    It generates ongoing revenue from the same living animals. Not every area attracts tourists, and that's the strongest point for those who defend hunting; the answer is to fund conservation by other means, not to kill animals to pay for it.

    6. The argument instrumentalizes individuals.

    Killing an animal to "save the species" treats them as a specimen, not as someone (A10).

    7. Even for those who accept swapping one animal for another, the numbers don't add up.

    Peter Singer, who grants with caveats the replaceability of animals without self-awareness (B7), concludes in Practical Ethics that it doesn't justify hunting: the death of the animal shot doesn't bring another into being, except when the population is at the limit of the available food, and the instant death that is usually assumed is far from guaranteed (ch. 5; see H19). The money hunting brings in doesn't change that calculation: it pays for the death, but doesn't make up for it to the one who dies.

    8. If the end is the pleasure of hunting, killing is effective, but the end doesn't justify it.

    The four questions about ends and means proposed by the philosopher Nathan Nobis (see I15) help separate two ends the argument mixes up. If the end is conservation, sport hunting is a debatable means (points 1 to 4) and there are better means (point 5). If the end is the pleasure of hunting and killing, killing is indeed the most effective means, as Nobis acknowledges; but that end hardly justifies the death of an animal who feels, and no one would accept it as a reason to kill a human (Animals & Ethics 101, 2016, pp. 70–71). Separating the two ends shows which one actually sustains the practice: if conservation could be funded without killing, those who hunt for pleasure would still want to hunt.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: in some areas, hunting is the only income

    The most serious version points out that in remote areas unattractive to tourists, hunting may be the only source of income that gives wildlife value and prevents the habitat being turned into pasture.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    That calls for other forms of funding.

    International funds and payments for conservation can fill that role.

    The case is limited.

    It doesn't justify sport hunting in general.

    It's still killing for fun.

    The hunter pays for the pleasure of killing, not for conservation.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Even if sport hunting funded conservation, it would still be killing an animal for pleasure. The moral question is whether it's fair to take a life for fun, and no spreadsheet answers that.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Conservation needs money, and communities living alongside wildlife need real benefits to support it. In some places, such as the United States, regulated hunting has generated resources and incentives to maintain habitats.

But

But most of the money doesn't reach where it should, there are alternatives that don't involve killing, and the argument forgets the dead animal. Conservation can be funded without killing for sport.
