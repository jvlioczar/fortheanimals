A23

"Rights are a human invention, only for humans"

The answer in one sentence

that rights are a human creation says nothing about whom they should protect. On the contrary: if we create them, extending them is our choice, and that choice needs a justification. The argument also ignores that the law already protects animals, from Article 13 of the EU Treaty to the German and Brazilian constitutions.

Short answer

Saying something was invented by us doesn't say whom it applies to. Mathematics was also created by humans, and numbers don't hold only among mathematicians. Besides, the argument turns against whoever uses it: if we decide whom rights apply to, then excluding animals is a choice, and choices need a justification. And real law already includes them: the EU Treaty recognizes animals as sentient beings, and several constitutions, including Germany's and Brazil's, protect them from cruelty.

For children

It's true that we invented the rules. But think of it this way: the rules of football were invented too, and they don't apply only to the people who invented them. If the rules are ours, we can decide whom they protect. And then the right question becomes: who can get hurt by what we do? The answer includes animals.

Ask a question back

"If a country abolished by law the rights of a group of people, would that make it right to treat them as things?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Rights were invented by us, for us. Animals don't come into it."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Rights don't exist in nature: they're conventions created by human communities.
  2. P2A convention holds only among the members of the community that created it.
  3. P3Animals aren't part of that community and took no part in creating it.
  4. CSo rights don't apply to animals.

The argument has two variants. The skeptical one denies the existence of natural rights, in the line of Jeremy Bentham, who called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts". The relativist one says each society invents its own, and ours chose these.

It's close to A22, but not the same. A22 demands reciprocity; A23 appeals to origin: rights would be ours because we made them.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2, which is false as a description of how law works, and in the general leap: a norm's origin doesn't determine its scope.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument commits the genetic fallacy: it judges a norm's scope by its origin. Origin and scope are independent questions, as trivial cases show.

    Human creationWhom it applies to
    MathematicsThe whole universe, not just mathematicians
    Veterinary medicineAnimals, though created by humans
    Traffic rulesTourists who never voted for them
    Children's rightsThose who took part in no deliberation
    Protection of future generationsThose who don't even exist yet

    There's also a problem of self-defeat. If rights are our invention, so is their scope. The argument then says: "we decided they don't apply to animals". That isn't a reason; it's a description of the decision under discussion.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. P2 is false.

    Legal conventions constantly protect those who took no part in creating them: babies, people in a coma, foreigners, future generations, historical heritage. Participation has never been a condition of protection.

    2. The history of law is a history of extension.

    The abolition of slavery, women's suffrage, the Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989) and the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) were each, in their time, called excessive. In all of them, the answer "it's always been this way" lost.

    3. Current law already protects animals.

    Article 13 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union requires member states to pay full regard to the welfare of animals "since animals are sentient beings". Germany added animals to its Basic Law in 2002. Brazil's Constitution, in Article 225, prohibits practices that "subject animals to cruelty". If rights applied only to humans, these norms would be unintelligible.

    4. Skepticism about rights doesn't imply indifference to suffering.

    The man who coined "nonsense upon stilts" against natural rights was Bentham, the same author of "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" One can doubt natural rights and still recognize duties.

    5. Ethics doesn't depend on the word "right".

    A consequentialist ethics does without the language of rights: if there's avoidable suffering, there's a moral reason against causing it. Dismissing rights as fiction doesn't eliminate that reason.

    6. The argument proves too much.

    If validity comes only from local convention, then where the law allowed slavery there was nothing wrong, and abolition was not a moral correction, just a change of taste. Practically no one accepts that consequence.

    7. Being invented doesn't make ethics arbitrary.

    Mathematics and medicine were also created by humans, and in them there are better and worse answers. In ethics, there are positions that weigh the harms and benefits of those affected in a biased way and others that don't. From the fact that rights are a human creation it doesn't follow that we may do as we please with those who took no part in creating them. See Questão de Ética, vol. I, ch. 11.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: rights as an institution, not a label

    The sophisticated version doesn't say animal suffering doesn't matter. It says "right" is a complex institution, presupposing title, exercise, representation and practical reciprocity. Extending that institution to beings who can't exercise it would create conceptual confusion and enormous practical problems, and it would be better to protect animals through human duties and welfare standards.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's a thesis about the tool, not about value.

    Even if "right" isn't the ideal instrument, the question remains of what we owe animals.

    The problem of exercise is already solved in law.

    Guardianship and legal representation exist precisely for holders who can't act for themselves.

    The alternative route already exists and is violated.

    If protection is to come through welfare duties, industrial livestock farming breaches them at scale, as the family B chapters show.

    In practice, the reply is usually used to change nothing.

    Those who defend it rarely propose the alternative model of protection it promises.

    Convention is changeable by definition.

    Recognizing that rights are a human creation is admitting that we can and should discuss whom they apply to. That's exactly what this book does.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Genetic fallacy
Judges a norm's scope by its origin
Self-defeat
If the scope is our choice, the exclusion needs a justification, which the argument doesn't give
Non sequitur
From "took no part in creating it" it concludes "isn't protected"
Proving too much
It would legitimize any legal exclusion of the past, slavery included

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Misdescribing law
Ignores that law routinely protects those who take no part in it
Not knowing current norms
Ignores constitutional protections for animals that already exist

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Legalistic fallacy
Confuses what the law says with what morality requires
Convention as justification
Uses the very decision in dispute as a reason to keep it

Biases

Status quo bias

The current legal arrangement seems the only one possible

Literature on preference for the current state

System justification

Leads people to treat current institutions as just because they're current

Correlated with speciesism (Caviola et al., 2019)

Presentism

Makes the present seem the end point of moral history, when every earlier extension seemed absurd before it happened

A historical pattern documented in each extension of rights

In-group bias

Defines the community of rights by one's own group

A common basis of speciesism and prejudice among humans

Legalistic bias

Confuses "isn't recognized" with "shouldn't be"

A pattern in debates about extending rights

Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "rights hold only for those who invented them or those local convention recognizes".

If it applied to…Enslaved people in the pastTap to see

Slavery wouldn't have been unjust while it was legal. If validity comes from the convention in force, those excluded simply had no rights, and abolition was a change of taste, not a correction

Practically no one accepts this reading of history

If it applied to…Regimes that violate rights todayTap to see

There'd be nothing to criticize. Where the law authorizes torture or persecution, local convention would be the final word

Whoever condemns these regimes already uses a criterion above convention

If it applied to…Foreigners and stateless peopleTap to see

They could be treated however we liked. They took no part in creating our norms and, in the case of stateless people, no community recognizes them

The idea of universal human rights was built against exactly this

If it applied to…Future generationsTap to see

We'd have no duties to them. They invented nothing, take no part and can't demand anything

All long-term environmental policy presupposes the opposite

If it applied to…The same animal in two countriesTap to see

An octopus would have moral value in the UK and none once it crossed the border. Its capacity to suffer wouldn't change; only the convention would

Shows that convention records recognition; it doesn't create the moral fact

Contradictions

Condemns rights violations in other countries.

Whoever thinks torture wrong when practiced by a state that authorizes it is already admitting a moral criterion above local convention.

Supports animal protection laws.

The same person usually thinks it right to punish someone who beats a dog, which only makes sense if protecting animals is legitimate.

Invokes rights when they're the one harmed.

No one suffering an injustice says "but after all, rights are just a convention".

Accepts protection for those who weren't party to the agreement.

Children, foreigners and future generations are protected without having invented anything.

Celebrates past extensions.

Almost everyone considers abolition and women's suffrage moral progress, not mere changes of convention.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Rights really are social constructions. They aren't written in nature, they depend on institutions to exist in practice and they need careful design: who represents the holder, which specific rights make sense, how to resolve conflicts. Those who treat rights as obvious natural facts oversimplify, and Bentham's skepticism has merit on this point.

But

But recognizing that we build these norms increases responsibility rather than diluting it. If the boundary was drawn by us, it can't be invoked as if it were a natural limit. It's exactly what's under discussion.
