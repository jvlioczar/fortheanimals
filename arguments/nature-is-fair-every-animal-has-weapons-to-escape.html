N25

"Nature is fair, every animal has weapons to escape"

The answer in one sentence

the idea that predator and prey compete on equal terms comes from a romantic picture of nature. Most animals die while still young, with no weapons at all, and predators pick precisely the weakest: the young, the old and the sick. Having similar abilities also doesn't guarantee the same luck, because disease, accidents and scarcity don't respect talent. And even if the contest were fair, that wouldn't show it's wrong to improve the conditions in which it takes place.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

Most animals that die in nature are young, with no weapons at all. Predators prefer the weakest: the young, the old and the sick. A species not having gone extinct only shows that some survived to reproduce, not that each one could escape. And even a fair contest can take place in terrible conditions that are worth improving.

For children

In a game, it isn't fair if one team is all babies. In nature, most of the animals that get hunted are babies who can't yet run or hide. And hunters prefer whoever is sick or old. That isn't a fair game.

Ask a question back

"If a healthy adult were attacked by a jaguar in the forest, would you not help because, in theory, they know how to defend themselves?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"The lion is strong, but the gazelle is fast. Each has its own weapons. Nature is balanced; no one needs to interfere."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1If a situation is fair, there's no need to improve it.
  2. P2If abilities are distributed in a balanced way, the situation is fair.
  3. P3Nature distributes abilities in a balanced way: predators have strength, prey have speed, camouflage or numbers.
  4. CSo there's no need to help animals in nature.

A philosophical version of this reasoning is found in Tom Regan, one of the leading animal rights theorists. In the 2004 preface to The Case for Animal Rights, he distinguishes the duty to protect a human child from a predator, which he accepts, from the case of a wild prey animal, which would be able to escape, as supposedly shown by the fact that its species hasn't gone extinct (Cunha, 2022, p. 161).

The argument is valid, but all three premises fail, and P3 is false as a description of nature.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    Each premise fails for a different reason:

    PremiseWhat it claimsWhy it fails
    P1A fair situation needs no improvementA fair share in scarcity still leaves everyone hungry
    P2Equal abilities make the situation fairEqual abilities don't guarantee equal luck
    P3Nature balances abilitiesMost victims are young, and predators pick the weakest
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Most victims have no weapons at all.

    In most species, each female produces many young, and almost all die early, of hunger, disease or being eaten. These young, without experience or strength, are the most common prey (Cunha, 2022, p. 162; N1).

    2. Predators pick the weakest.

    A study of the northern Yellowstone elk herd showed that wolves killed mostly calves and old females: the average age of adult females killed by wolves was 13.9 years, against 6.5 years for those taken by hunters (Wright et al., 2006). In Colorado, deer infected with chronic wasting disease, caused by prions, were about 3.7 times more likely to be killed by mountain lions (Miller et al., 2008). The "contest" is often between a healthy predator and an animal that is already weakened.

    3. Equal abilities don't guarantee equal luck.

    Even if everyone had the same weapons, disease, accidents and where one is born would make some suffer far more than others, for reasons beyond their control (Cunha, 2022, p. 137).

    4. You can't move from the species to the individual.

    That a species hasn't been driven extinct by predation only shows that some of its members reproduced before dying. It doesn't show that each individual was able to escape, nor that it escaped until the end of its life (Cunha, 2022, p. 162).

    5. Fairness in the contest isn't wellbeing.

    Imagine a situation of scarcity in which food was shared out as fairly as possible. There would still be reason to end the scarcity. Likewise, even if the contest between predator and prey were fair, that wouldn't show it's wrong to improve the conditions in which it happens (Cunha, 2022, pp. 136–137).

    6. The human test.

    A healthy human adult is the most common example of someone able to look after themselves. Even so, if they were attacked by an animal, almost everyone would think it right to help them. Being capable in general doesn't mean being able to escape this particular threat (Cunha, 2022, p. 162).

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the arms race is real

    The most serious version appeals to evolutionary biology. Predators and prey evolve together, in a kind of arms race. Richard Dawkins and John Krebs (1979) observed that prey tend to have the edge because they run for their lives, while the predator runs for a meal: losing costs the prey much more. That's why many hunts fail. And, from a rights standpoint, intervening would treat competent animals as incapable, a paternalism they never asked for.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The race applies to those who reach adulthood.

    The fleeing prey's edge exists among healthy adults. Most victims, however, are the young, the old and the sick, who are outside that race.

    Escaping often isn't escaping always.

    Even if most hunts fail, each one that succeeds is a catastrophe for the one who dies. And successful escape has costs too: injuries, stress and less time to feed.

    Competence isn't the criterion.

    We help competent humans when they're in danger, and no one calls that paternalism. What justifies helping is the harm someone would suffer, not their general incapacity. Concerns about autonomy bear on how to help, not on whether it's permitted.

    Even if it weren't necessary, it wouldn't be wrong.

    If prey could manage on their own, that would at most show that helping isn't required, not that it's forbidden (Cunha, 2022, p. 162).

Contradictions

Would rescue an adult attacked by an animal.

Even knowing that, in theory, they can defend themselves.

Thinks it's right to protect children.

But not the young of other species, who are most of the prey.

Accepts that bad luck justifies helping humans.

Insurance, public health and emergency services exist because equal abilities don't guarantee equal luck.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that many animals have impressive adaptations for fleeing and defending themselves, and that among healthy adults many hunts fail. It's also right that respecting animals' ability to look after themselves matters, and that helping shouldn't mean controlling their lives unnecessarily.

But

But those weapons protect a minority, and poorly. For the young, the old and the sick, who are most of the victims, nature offers no fair contest, and the question of how to help them still stands.
