I24

"I fish what I eat; it's more honest than buying at the store"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

taking responsibility for what you eat is more consistent than outsourcing the killing, and that deserves recognition. But being honest about a harm doesn't make it necessary. A fish taken out of water usually dies by asphyxia, which doesn't cause immediate loss of consciousness: in a study of trout, researchers estimated about 10 minutes of moderate to intense pain per fish. Knowing where your food comes from doesn't change what the fish went through.

Short answer

Taking responsibility is more consistent than pretending store-bought fish was born packaged, and that has value. But being honest about a harm doesn't make it necessary. A fish out of water usually dies by asphyxia, which doesn't make it lose consciousness right away: in a study of trout, researchers estimated about 10 minutes of moderate to intense pain per fish. For anyone with other food options, the question remains: why cause that?

For children

When a fish comes out of the water, it can't breathe. It flaps around because it's suffocating, and that can go on for many minutes. Catching your own fish is being honest about where food comes from, but the fish feels the same fear and the same lack of air.

Ask a question back

"If you know where it comes from and how the fish dies, does that lessen what it feels?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I fish, take it home and eat it. At least I know where it comes from, unlike people who buy packaged fish at the store."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Whoever catches their own fish takes direct responsibility for the animal's death.
  2. P2Whoever buys at the store hands that responsibility to others and avoids thinking about it.
  3. P3Taking responsibility is morally better than avoiding it.
  4. CSo fishing for food is an honest, and acceptable, way to eat fish.

P1, P2 and P3 can be granted. The problem is the jump to "acceptable": being more honest than another practice doesn't show that the practice is fair.

The argument is invalid: the conclusion goes beyond the premises.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument compares two ways of eating fish and concludes that one of them is acceptable:

    QuestionWhat the argument answersWhat's left out
    Is it more honest than buying at the store?YesHonesty isn't a justification
    Does the fish suffer?Usually not askedHook, asphyxia, slow death
    Is it necessary?Usually not askedFor most people, there are alternatives
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Asphyxia is slow.

    Out of water, a fish doesn't lose consciousness right away. In a study of rainbow trout published in Scientific Reports in 2025, researchers estimated about 10 minutes of moderate to intense pain per fish killed by air asphyxia, ranging from about 2 to 22 minutes.

    2. In other species, it can last longer.

    Fishcount's report Worse things happen at sea cites a Dutch study in which herring, cod and plaice took 55 to 250 minutes to die by asphyxia.

    3. Fish feel pain.

    The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024) recognizes a realistic possibility of consciousness in all vertebrates, including fish (A2).

    4. Honesty isn't a justification.

    Someone who does something wrong openly is more consistent than someone who hides it, but the act is still wrong. Consistency is a step, not the end of the question.

    5. There's a way to reduce the harm, but not to remove it.

    Someone who fishes can kill the fish immediately with a blow to the head instead of letting it suffocate. That reduces suffering; the question about necessity remains.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: fishing for food is more natural and has less impact than industry

    The most serious version says that catching your own food on a small scale avoids industrial fishing, bycatch and waste, and that it's the oldest and most natural way of getting food.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Less environmental impact isn't less suffering for the fish.

    A fish caught on a small scale goes through the same hook and the same asphyxia (H16).

    Being old and natural doesn't make something right (C1).

    Many natural, old practices were abandoned once they stopped being necessary.

    Where there's no alternative, the situation changes.

    Communities that depend on fishing to survive are in a different position (J14). For anyone with other options, the question remains.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question isn't whether fishing is more honest than buying, but whether it's fair to put a fish through minutes of suffocation when there are other ways to eat. Being consistent about the harm is a good start for asking whether it needs to happen.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Selective comparison
Comparing only with the worst (buying without thinking) makes the practice look acceptable

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Not knowing about asphyxia
Assumes fish die quickly out of the water

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Honesty as justification
Being frank about the harm is treated as if it made it legitimate

Contradictions

Values knowing where it comes from.

And avoids looking at how long the fish takes to die.

Criticizes those who outsource the killing.

And wouldn't apply the same reasoning to other animals they could kill with their own hands.

Wouldn't accept suffocating a mammal.

Letting a dog or a rabbit die without air for minutes would be cruelty.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that taking responsibility for what you eat is more consistent than outsourcing the killing and avoiding thinking about it. People who fish know the fish was a living animal, and many people who eat packaged fish lack that clarity. It's also true that killing the fish immediately reduces suffering.

But

But honesty leads to the next question rather than closing it: if the fish feels and there are other ways to eat, why put it through this?
