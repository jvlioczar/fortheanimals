L14

"Vegans shouldn't watch violent films or play violent games"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

depicting violence isn't committing it. In a film or game where an animal is hunted, nobody was hunted: there are actors, effects, animation, lines of code. Veganism is about real victims, and that is where the accusation gets one thing right: there are productions in which animals were genuinely injured or killed during filming, and avoiding funding those works is consistent with veganism. Beyond that, the rule would also forbid the documentaries that show what happens in slaughterhouses, which are among the most violent images there are and are exactly what animal advocates ask people to watch. Works made to promote exploitation and reinforce speciesist ideas are another matter: those can certainly be questioned — not for showing violence, but for what they advocate.

Note: this chapter is about depicting violence. Refusing to look at what happens on farms is E12

Short answer

In a game or a fiction film, no animal was hunted: there are actors, effects and animation. Veganism is about real victims. When an animal was genuinely injured or killed to make a scene, the concern is legitimate, and avoiding those productions is consistent. Beyond that, the rule would forbid even the documentaries about slaughterhouses, which are among the most violent images there are and are exactly what vegans ask people to watch. And the leading US psychological association concluded there isn't enough evidence that violent games cause violent behaviour.

Question back: "Does someone who watches crime films support murder? If not, why would a vegan who watches a film with a hunting scene support hunting?"

For children

In a cartoon or a game, when an animal gets hit, nobody really gets hurt: it's all pretend, made of drawings or computer code. Being vegan is about not hurting real animals. So a film where an animal was really hurt to shoot a scene is different, and many people prefer not to watch that kind of film.

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"A vegan who plays hunting games or watches a film with an animal dying has no right to talk about cruelty."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Veganism opposes violence against animals.
  2. P2Violent films and games present violence against animals as entertainment.
  3. P3Consuming violent entertainment normalises violence and funds those who produce it.
  4. CSo vegans who watch violent films or play violent games are inconsistent.

P1 is right. P2 is true of some works and false of many others, which show violence precisely to criticise it. P3 mixes two things: funding real harm, which is a legitimate concern, and "normalising" depicted violence, whose link to real violence the evidence doesn't support.

The argument is valid only if depicting violence is the same as committing it. Its soundness fails there: veganism opposes real victims, and fiction has none.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument lumps together three situations that call for different answers.

    SituationIs there a real victim?What veganism says
    Violence against animals that is staged, animated or digitalNoNothing to refuse: nobody was harmed
    Productions in which animals were genuinely injured or killedYesA legitimate concern: avoiding funding them makes sense
    Works that show real violence in order to expose it, such as documentaries on animal farmingYes, but the work didn't cause itWatching can be part of confronting the problem

    The accusation treats all three as if they were the first and the second at once.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Depicting isn't doing.

    A war film isn't a war, and a crime novel isn't a murder. In a game where the character hunts an animal, no animal feels anything: there are pixels and code. Veganism opposes harming beings who feel, and in fiction there are none.

    2. Where there is a real victim, the criticism is right, and veganism agrees.

    There are films in which animals were killed for real in front of the camera, such as the water buffalo sacrificed in a ritual filmed for Apocalypse Now (1979). And a Hollywood Reporter investigation in 2013 pointed to injuries and deaths of animals on productions that carried the "no animals were harmed" label. Avoiding funding works like these is exactly what veganism asks, as far as is possible and practicable.

    3. The rule would forbid exposés too.

    Documentaries showing what happens in slaughterhouses and on farms are among the most violent images there are. If watching violence against animals were inconsistent, vegans couldn't watch or share those works, which are exactly what the defence of animals asks people to see. Ironically, it's the people who won't watch those videos who tend to avoid the real violence (E12).

    4. The evidence doesn't support violent games making people violent.

    The question has been debated for decades, and the current picture is this. The American Psychological Association concluded in 2020 that there is insufficient scientific evidence of a causal link between violent video games and violent behaviour, while noting a small association with mild aggression, such as yelling and pushing. A 2019 reanalysis of the meta-analyses that seemed to contradict each other concluded that they agree on one point: where there is an effect on aggression, it is almost always small. A 2020 meta-analysis of 28 samples and around 21,000 young people followed over time found a very small relationship, which in the best-designed studies is indistinguishable from zero. And a preregistered study of more than a thousand British adolescents found no relationship between playing violent games and aggressive behaviour as reported by their carers. It is worth remembering that "aggression" in this research is usually measured in mild laboratory situations, not as real violence. Concern about subtle effects is legitimate, but it doesn't turn playing into committing violence.

    5. It's the same structure as other charges of inconsistency.

    As with the phone and artificial intelligence, the argument looks for a failing in the advocate so as not to discuss what happens to animals (L11, L13). Even if a vegan were inconsistent, that wouldn't make the exploitation acceptable.

    6. Many works show violence in order to criticise it.

    A scene of cruelty to an animal may exist precisely to provoke revulsion and empathy. Judging a work by the presence of violence, rather than by what it does with it, treats denunciation and glorification alike.

    7. Works that promote exploitation can certainly be questioned.

    There are films, games, programmes and adverts made to encourage animal exploitation and strengthen speciesist ideas: they treat hunting, rodeos or slaughter as admirable fun, present animals as things to be used, or work as advertising for those who profit from them. Criticising them is legitimate, and contradicts nothing said above. The difference is what is criticised: not the violence depicted, which may even serve to expose it, but the message the work advocates. It is the same standard used for racist or misogynist works: the problem isn't showing prejudice, it's promoting it.

  4. d

    The best reply: desensitisation

    The serious version doesn't say playing is killing. It says repeated exposure to violence, even fictional, may reduce sensitivity to suffering, and that those who defend animals should cultivate exactly the opposite.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The evidence is weak for what is claimed.

    Small associations with mild aggression don't amount to moral inconsistency.

    It's advice, not proof of hypocrisy.

    It may apply to anyone concerned about their own wellbeing, and says nothing against veganism.

    It would forbid the exposé documentaries too.

    And those, on the contrary, tend to make viewers more sensitive to real suffering.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets two things right that deserve acknowledgement. The first is that there are productions in which animals were genuinely injured or killed, and that the "no animals were harmed" label hasn't always been reliable. Avoiding funding those works is consistent with veganism, and many animal advocates already do so. The second is that it's worth paying attention to what entertainment teaches about animals, for example when it treats hunting as fun.

And it gets right, above all, that some works are made to promote exploitation and reinforce speciesism, and that those deserve criticism.

But

But depicting violence isn't committing it, and the evidence doesn't show that violent games make people violent. Veganism is about real victims, and fiction has none. What can be questioned in a work is what it advocates, not the fact that it shows violence. If the rule held, it would also forbid the images that have most helped people see what happens to animals.
