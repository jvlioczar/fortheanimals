D10

"God gave us dominion over animals"

The answer in one sentence

the meaning of dominion in Genesis is disputed: part of the tradition read it as permission to use animals, and many traditions read it as responsibility and care. Pope Francis's encyclical Laudato Si' itself states that deriving from it an absolute dominion over other creatures is not a correct interpretation of the Bible. Since the text doesn't settle the question on its own, it's worth examining the moral reasons themselves, and many believers reach compassion for animals from their own faith's values.

Reply

You can read that dominion the way many religious traditions do: as responsibility, like that of someone looking after something that isn't theirs. Pope Francis, in the encyclical Laudato Si', wrote that deriving an absolute dominion over other creatures from it isn't a correct interpretation of the Bible. And look at the very next verse (Genesis 1:29): the food given to humans is plants and fruit. It's true that other readers through history have understood dominion as permission to use animals; the text alone doesn't decide between the readings. That's why it's worth looking at the moral reasons themselves too: causing suffering and death without need is something believers and non-believers can assess together, and many believers reach compassion for animals through their own faith's values.

For children

Imagine someone leaves you in charge of a garden while they travel. Does being responsible for the garden mean you can pull up all the flowers? No! It means you should take care of it. Many religious people understand that this is what God asked of humans: to take care of animals, not to mistreat them. And anyone, religious or not, can see that making an animal suffer for no reason is wrong.

Ask a question back

"If God entrusted animals to human beings, how do you think He'd want them treated?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"It's in Genesis. God made animals for man. Who are you to contradict God?"

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1In Genesis 1:26-28, God gives human beings dominion over the animals.
  2. P2Having dominion over something includes the right to use it.
  3. P3What God authorizes is morally permitted.
  4. CSo using and eating animals is morally permitted.

The strong version rests on a text that is sacred to millions of people and deserves to be taken seriously. The problem lies in P2: reading "dominion" as unlimited use is disputed within the religious traditions themselves.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2, and premise P3, being theological, can't be required of those who don't share the same faith.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument depends on a specific interpretation of "dominion":

    ReadingWhat it meansWho holds it
    Dominion as permission to useAnimals exist for human use, including as foodAn important part of the Christian tradition, such as Thomas Aquinas
    Dominion as responsibilityHuman beings care for creation on God's behalfMany Christian, Jewish and other traditions; Laudato Si'

    Both readings have support in the text and in history. The argument only works if the first is the only possible one, and it isn't.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The text allows more than one reading.

    The verbs in Genesis 1:28 translated as "have dominion" and "subdue" are strong, and major interpreters, such as Thomas Aquinas, concluded that animals exist for human use. But right after the dominion, verse 29 gives humans "every herb bearing seed" and "every tree, in the which is the fruit" as food, and in the Genesis narrative eating meat only appears after the flood. The text alone doesn't force us to read dominion as a license to exploit.

    2. The Catholic Church rejects absolute dominion.

    In Laudato Si' (2015), Pope Francis wrote that deriving an absolute dominion over other creatures from the mandate in Genesis isn't a correct interpretation of the Bible, and linked the idea to "tilling and keeping" in Genesis 2:15.

    3. There are religious traditions that defend animals.

    Many Christians, Jews, Buddhists, Hindus, Jains and Seventh-day Adventists practice or recommend vegetarianism for religious reasons. Religion doesn't speak with a single voice.

    4. Permission isn't obligation.

    Even if the text permitted eating animals, it wouldn't require it. A permission doesn't answer whether it's good to do, especially under the conditions of industrial animal farming.

    5. In a public debate, the premise has to be shared.

    For those who don't share the faith, "God permits it" isn't a reason. In a debate among people of different beliefs, one has to offer reasons everyone can assess.

    6. Moral reasons can be examined on their own.

    Plato's Euthyphro already asks: is something good because the gods approve of it, or do they approve of it because it's good? Many theologians answer that God commands what is good for reasons the human conscience can understand. If so, the question "is it right to make an animal suffer and die without need?" can be considered directly, without depending on which reading of a verse prevails. Separating moral analysis from religious authority isn't an attack on faith: it's a way of thinking more carefully about our own actions. And many believers take this path from within their religion, reaching compassion for animals through values their faith already teaches, such as mercy and respect for others. The book of Proverbs (12:10) says that "a righteous man regardeth the life of his beast".

    7. Dominion isn't the same as license to do anything.

    Ruling over other beings allows very different forms, from benign paternalism to tyranny, and it's possible to rule over someone and still have duties of justice toward them. The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane notes that Augustine and Thomas Aquinas didn't take the exclusion of animals from the Bible alone: they inherited much of it from Greek philosophy, above all from the Stoic idea that we have no obligations to those who lack reason (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 17–19). Reading dominion as a license for use owes as much to philosophy as to the text.

    8. Reading dominion as a licence carried real weight in history, and that should be said.

    Peter Singer gathers examples from early Christian tradition. Paul, commenting on the law of Moses that forbids muzzling the ox while it treads out the grain (Deuteronomy 25:4), asks whether it is for oxen that God cares, and reads the precept as a lesson for humans (1 Corinthians 9:9-10). Augustine, commenting on the Gospel episodes of the withered fig tree and the drowned swine, concludes that refraining from killing animals and destroying plants is superstition. And Thomas Aquinas, following Aristotle, recognized only sins against God, against oneself and against one's neighbour, none against animals (Practical Ethics, ch. 10). Singer also records more generous minority voices, such as Basil, John Chrysostom and Francis of Assisi, and thinks the biblical text gives little support to reading dominion as stewardship. That assessment is disputed: theologians such as Andrew Linzey, and Pope Francis himself in Laudato Si', defend the stewardship reading (point 2). The debate confirms point 1: the text doesn't settle the matter alone, and the moral question has to be examined on its own terms (point 6).

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the Bible permits eating meat after the flood

    The most serious version accepts the reading of care, but points out that in Genesis 9, after the flood, God explicitly permits eating animals, and that the Christian tradition has never forbidden meat.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Permission isn't a recommendation.

    Many theologians read Genesis 9 as a concession to a fallen world, not as the ideal.

    The permission doesn't include cruelty.

    Even those who read the permission literally recognize the duty not to cause unnecessary suffering.

    The context has changed.

    A permission given under conditions of scarcity isn't a mandate for industrial animal farming.

Contradictions

Doesn't follow other parts of the text literally.

Many biblical rules aren't followed by those who cite dominion.

Condemns cruelty to animals.

Recognizes that creation should be cared for.

Which contradicts the idea of unlimited use.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Faith is an important part of many people's lives, and it's legitimate to seek moral guidance in it. It's also true that biblical texts, at various points, permit eating animals, and that most Christian traditions don't require vegetarianism.

But

But the tradition itself offers another reading: dominion as care, plant food as the ideal of Eden, mercy toward all creatures. Faced with a text that allows more than one reading, the question that remains is which one fits better with the mercy the tradition itself asks for. And that question can also be answered through the moral reasons themselves, which believers and non-believers can examine together.
