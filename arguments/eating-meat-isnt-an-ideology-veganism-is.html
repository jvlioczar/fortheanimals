E9

"Eating meat isn't an ideology; veganism is"

The answer in one sentence

every practice that involves choices about who may be used rests on beliefs, eating meat included. The difference is that the dominant belief is invisible to those who follow it. The psychologist Melanie Joy called this belief system carnism: it teaches which animals are food and which are companions, and makes this seem natural. Veganism doesn't politicize food; it only makes visible a choice that already existed.

Short answer

Eating meat also depends on beliefs: that certain animals may be used and others may not, that the pig is food and the dog is family. These beliefs seem neutral because they're the majority's, and the dominant norm is invisible to those who follow it. The psychologist Melanie Joy called this system carnism. Veganism doesn't invent an ideology; it only makes visible a choice that was already being made.

For children

Who decided the dog is a friend and the pig is food? Nobody is born knowing that: we learn it from our family and our culture. In other countries, people learn it differently. So eating certain animals also comes from ideas someone taught us, even if they seem normal.

Ask a question back

"Who taught you that the pig is food and the dog is family?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Eating meat is normal, it's not a philosophy. You're the ones politicizing food. Carnism is a made-up word."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Eating meat is a traditional and almost universal practice that doesn't depend on any theory.
  2. P2Veganism depends on a specific moral theory about animals.
  3. P3Whoever proposes changing a practice on the basis of a theory carries the ideological burden.
  4. CSo eating meat is neutral, and veganism is an ideology.

The strong version has some truth in it: whoever proposes changing a custom has to offer reasons. The problem lies in P1: eating meat does depend on beliefs, such as the belief that certain animals may be used and others may not.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1, which confuses "dominant" with "neutral".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument treats the dominant norm as an absence of belief:

    Belief behind eating meatIs it neutral?
    Certain animals may be raised and killed for foodNo: it's a moral position
    Which animals count as food varies by cultureShows the choice is learned
    Eating meat is normal, natural and necessaryThese are justifications, studied by psychology
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Dominant isn't neutral.

    Every practice that decides who may be used involves moral judgments. Most people simply don't notice the judgments they share with almost everyone.

    2. The choice of which animals to eat is cultural.

    In Europe and the Americas people eat pigs but not dogs; in some places it has been different, and in others cows are not eaten. This shows the division is taught, not natural.

    3. The concept of carnism describes this system.

    Melanie Joy proposed the term in 2009 to name the belief system that conditions people to eat certain animals. One can disagree with the term, but the idea that eating animals is sustained by beliefs is supported by research on the 4Ns: natural, normal, necessary and nice.

    4. "Ideology" isn't an insult.

    Every moral position is a set of beliefs about how to act. The relevant question isn't whether something is an ideology, but whether its reasons are good.

    5. The burden applies to both sides.

    Whoever proposes change has to give reasons, and so does whoever causes harm to others. A practice being old doesn't exempt it from justification (D1).

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Joy, *Why We Love Dogs, Eat Pigs, and Wear Cows* (2009)Proposes the concept of carnismThe author's page on the concept
    Piazza et al., 2015The most common justifications for eating meat are that it's natural, normal, necessary and niceShows the belief system at work
    Bratanova, Loughnan and Bastian, 2011Classifying an animal as food lowers the moral status attributed to itShows the weight of learned categories
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: not every habit is an ideology

    The most serious version says not every habit expresses an ideology: brushing your teeth, for example, is just a custom. Calling meat eating an ideology would inflate the word.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Brushing your teeth has no victim.

    Habits that involve other beings require moral justification.

    The argument doesn't depend on the word.

    One can swap "ideology" for "set of beliefs", and the question remains.

    The justifications exist and are studied.

    The fact that people reach for them shows there are beliefs sustaining the habit.

Contradictions

Wouldn't eat dogs.

Which shows they follow a rule about which animals to eat.

Defends eating meat with arguments.

Natural, normal, necessary: these are beliefs.

Would call practices of other cultures ideological.

Such as eating dogs in other countries.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that whoever proposes changing a custom has to give good reasons, and that accusing everyone of "ideology" can impoverish the debate. It's also true that veganism is a moral position, with assumptions that can be discussed.

But

But the same holds for eating meat. The difference isn't that one side has beliefs and the other doesn't; it's that the majority's beliefs are invisible. Making those beliefs visible is the first step to examining them.
