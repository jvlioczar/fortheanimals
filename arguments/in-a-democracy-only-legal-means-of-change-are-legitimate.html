K8

"In a democracy, only legal means of change are legitimate"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about effectiveness and individual responsibility, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended only by each person's impact, anyone who proved their purchase changes nothing would be let off. But no one accepts this for other harms: taking part in an injustice is still wrong even when each person's share is small.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

in a democracy, legal channels are the main path and carry moral weight of their own: deciding by vote is far better than deciding by force. But the existence of legal channels doesn't make every illegal act unjustifiable. Peaceful civil disobedience, done openly and accepting punishment, played a recognized role in abolition, women's suffrage and civil rights, and it often serves to inform the majority rather than coerce it. Violence against people is something else, and this guide rejects it. Each case has to be assessed, and the law alone doesn't decide what is right.

Reply

Legal channels are indeed the main path in a democracy, and deciding by vote is far better than deciding by force. But "only legal means" goes too far. Remember that abolition, women's suffrage and civil rights all included peaceful civil disobedience, like the 1960 protests at segregated lunch counters in the United States, now remembered as part of democratic history. This kind of action, done openly and accepting punishment, usually serves to inform the majority, not to coerce it. Violence against people is something else, and it isn't justified; beyond that, each case has to be discussed. And the law alone doesn't answer the underlying question: is what is legally done to animals today fair?

For children

In a democracy, people vote and talk to change the rules, and that's really good: it's much better than fighting. Most of the time it's the right way. But in history, some people disobeyed unfair rules without hurting anyone, to show everyone that something was wrong. In the United States, Black people sat at lunch counters where the law only let white people sit, and today we admire them. Hurting people, never. And each case needs careful thought.

Ask a question back

"Were the people who sat at whites-only lunch counters in 1960 wrong because they broke the law?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"If you want change, change the law. Breaking into a farm is a crime."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1In a democracy, there are legal channels for changing the law: voting, petitions, courts, the press and demonstrations.
  2. P2Majority rule is the fairest and most peaceful way of settling disagreements in a society of equals.
  3. P3Breaking the law for a cause is an attempt to override the majority, and it weakens the respect for the law that everyone depends on.
  4. CSo, in a democracy, only legal means are legitimate.

P1 and P2 are largely true, and P3 has a correct core. The problem lies in the "only": P3 treats every illegal act as coercion, and the conclusion ignores cases where legal channels fail or where disobedience seeks to inform rather than impose.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, which overgeneralizes, so the absolute conclusion doesn't hold.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument lumps very different actions together under the label "illegal":

    Peaceful civil disobediencePeaceful blockade, sit-in, rescue done openly and with identificationIllegal, but public, nonviolent and accepting punishment; assessed case by case
    Damage to propertyDestroying equipmentMuch harder to justify; high risks for the cause
    Violence against peopleThreats, assaults, explosivesRejected
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. "Only" is too strong.

    Almost no one accepts the rule in its absolute form. Those who helped enslaved people escape in the United States, defying the fugitive slave laws such as the one of 1850, or who sat at the segregated lunch counters of Greensboro in 1960, broke laws then in force. The question isn't whether the law may ever be broken, but when, how and why.

    2. The law has moral weight, and it's real.

    Peter Singer, in Practical Ethics (ch. 11), gives two reasons for obeying even laws we disagree with: disobeying may encourage others to disobey, eventually eroding the order that protects everyone, and it imposes on the community the costs of enforcing the law. He adds a third, specific to democracy: in an egalitarian age, the rule of "one person, one vote" is the only compromise acceptable to everyone for settling disputes without force, and the likely result of abandoning it is rule by whoever has the most force. These reasons carry weight, but they aren't absolute: they go on the scale against the gravity of the wrong one is trying to prevent.

    3. Legal channels exist, but they can be slow or blocked.

    Singer notes that the mere existence of legal channels doesn't prove that every illegal means is wrong: the prospect of change may be remote while the harm continues. The most it shows is that legal means should be tried first. And in representative democracies, voters choose packages of policies; a policy may be opposed by the majority without any major party opposing it.

    4. Civil disobedience isn't coercion: it's a call for attention.

    In John Rawls's classic definition, in A Theory of Justice (1971), civil disobedience is a public, nonviolent, conscientious act contrary to law, done to change a law or policy and addressed to the majority's sense of justice. Singer goes the same way: when the public doesn't know what is happening, or when organized interests block reform, peaceful disobedience that accepts punishment seeks to restore the democratic process, not to frustrate it (ch. 11). One of his examples is making known how animals are treated in laboratories and factory farms.

    5. Democratic history includes disobedience.

    Henry David Thoreau wrote the essay that popularized the term "civil disobedience" (1849) after refusing to pay a tax, in protest against slavery and the war with Mexico. Mohandas Gandhi led the Salt March in 1930. In the United States, the Montgomery bus boycott (1955–1956), the 1960 lunch-counter sit-ins and the marches of Martin Luther King Jr., who defended disobeying unjust laws in his "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (1963), preceded the Civil Rights Act of 1964. In women's suffrage, some British suffragettes also turned to property damage, which divided the movement itself: methods have always been debated within just causes.

    6. The University of Pennsylvania laboratory case.

    In May 1984, members of the Animal Liberation Front (ALF) broke into the university's head-injury laboratory, damaged equipment and took about 60 hours of recordings of experiments on baboons. According to Singer, they had first tried to obtain information through official channels, without success. An edited version of the videos, released as Unnecessary Fuss, provoked public outrage; after a year of protests and a four-day peaceful sit-in at the National Institutes of Health (NIH), which funded the research, US Health Secretary Margaret Heckler suspended the funding in July 1985, and the lab was closed. The federal office that investigated the case said the video grossly overstated the deficiencies, but found serious violations. Singer thinks it likely that, without the videos, the lab would have kept operating, and he considers the action justifiable if it was the only way to end the experiments. That is a consequentialist philosopher's assessment of a specific case, not a rule.

    7. Violence against people is something else.

    Singer separates civil disobedience, damage to property and violence against people. He considers violence very hard to justify and condemns terrorist violence in virtually absolute terms. Even property damage, for him, requires answering hard questions first: won't the equipment and animals simply be replaced? What will happen to the freed animals? Won't the government harden instead of reforming? And violent attacks give researchers the chance to call all critics terrorists. This guide rejects violence against people, without exception.

    8. The law can also punish those who inform.

    In the United States, several states passed so-called ag-gag laws, which criminalized filming on farms or taking jobs there to document abuse, and several were struck down in court for violating freedom of speech (L5). The Animal Enterprise Terrorism Act (AETA) of 2006 increased penalties for actions against such businesses, and critics argue it can reach peaceful protest. When the law forbids precisely the information democratic debate needs, "use legal means" becomes harder to follow.

    9. Courts are divided too.

    In 2022, a Utah jury acquitted the activists Wayne Hsiung and Paul Picklesimer, charged with theft for taking two sick piglets from a Smithfield Foods-linked farm in an "open rescue", done with identification and on camera. In California, Hsiung was convicted of trespass and conspiracy in another rescue case. Similar conduct got different answers from the courts, which shows that the moral question isn't settled just by invoking the law.

    10. The criterion applies to causes one disagrees with.

    Singer applies the same reasoning to a group that blockaded abortion clinics in the United States and concludes that the disagreement with that group lies in the merit of its cause, not in its use of civil disobedience. This guide takes no position on abortion (L20), but the example makes the point: whoever accepts civil disobedience in one case has to accept that others may use it for causes they disagree with, and answer them on the merits.

    11. Citing slavery or Nazism here isn't comparing victims.

    Examples such as helping enslaved people escape or hiding Jews from the Nazis, illegal acts that almost everyone now considers right, serve only to show that the premise "if it's illegal, it's wrong" is false. The philosopher Nathan Nobis insists that this use makes no comparison at all between the animal question and slavery or the Holocaust (Animals & Ethics 101, 2016, p. 79). Answering "you're making a comparison" doesn't answer the argument, which only tests a principle (see L22).

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: who decides what is serious enough?

    The most serious version says that if each person decides for themselves when a cause justifies breaking the law, any fanatic will have a justification. Groups with opposing causes will do the same, and the rule only protects if it is absolute.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    There is no other body to decide.

    Singer grants that each person has to judge for themselves which side of the line a case falls on, because the majority can't be judge in its own cause. But that doesn't make the decision arbitrary: it requires arguments, which can be examined and challenged.

    The absolute rule doesn't hold either.

    No one today condemns those who disobeyed segregation laws or fugitive slave laws. A rule that would condemn those cases is too strong.

    The worry justifies limits, not a total ban.

    Acting openly, without violence, accepting punishment, trying legal means first and rejecting any violence against people: these limits are what separate civil disobedience from imposition by force.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument is about means, not ends. Even someone who considers every illegal act wrong still has to answer whether what happens to animals on farms and in laboratories is just. And legal means, such as changing what one buys, voting, lobbying and informing, remain open to everyone.

Contradictions

Admires those who disobeyed unjust laws in the past.

But condemns on principle those who act the same way today.

Invokes the law in one direction only.

Animal cruelty is also a crime, as under article 32 of Brazil's Environmental Crimes Law, and breaches of those laws rarely provoke the same outrage.

Uses the law to close the moral discussion.

But would hardly accept a future law, more favorable to animals, closing the discussion against their own habits.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Legal channels are the main path, and much of what has been won for animals came through them (K6, K7). Majority rule and the peaceful settlement of disputes have real moral value, and those who break the law take on risks for themselves, for the cause and for trust in the law. Violence against people must be rejected.

But

But recognizing the weight of the law isn't the same as saying it alone decides what is right. Peaceful civil disobedience has a long, legitimate history, and each case has to be judged on the merit of the cause and the means used.
