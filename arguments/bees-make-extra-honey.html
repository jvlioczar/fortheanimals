I5

"Bees make extra honey"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

honey is the bees' food reserve for periods without flowers, not a surplus. In beekeeping it's taken and often replaced with sugar syrup. Honeybees are kept like domestic animals and can compete with native bees, which are the main pollinators in many ecosystems; there are some 20,000 bee species worldwide. And there is growing evidence that bees have experiences, which the New York Declaration recognizes as a realistic possibility.

Short answer

Honey is the bees' food reserve, not a surplus; in beekeeping it's often replaced with sugar syrup. The bees in decline are wild native species, not honeybees, which are kept like domestic animals and can compete with them for flowers and spread disease. Buying honey doesn't help native bees. And there's growing evidence that bees have experiences.

For children

Bees make honey so they have food in winter, when there are no flowers. When people take the honey, they often give the bees sugar water instead. Besides, there are lots of wild bees that live freely and help plants. They're the ones that need protecting.

Ask a question back

"If the problem is the decline of native bees, does keeping more honeybees help?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Honey is natural. Without beekeepers, bees disappear. Bees don't even feel anything."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Bees produce more honey than they consume in good seasons.
  2. P2Beekeeping helps maintain bee populations, which are important for pollination.
  3. P3Bees are insects, with little or no capacity to suffer.
  4. CSo eating honey causes no harm and even helps bees.

P1 is partly true. P2 confuses honeybees, which are kept animals, with native bees, which are in decline. P3 is increasingly disputed by science.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2 and P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses two groups of bees:

    GroupSituationRelationship to honey
    Honeybees (Apis mellifera)Kept by beekeepers, like domestic animalsProduce commercial honey
    Native bees (around 20,000 species worldwide)Many in decline from habitat loss and pesticidesNot helped by buying honey
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Honey is a reserve, not a surplus.

    Bees store honey for periods without flowers. Beekeeping removes that store and often replaces it with sugar syrup, which is nutritionally inferior.

    2. Honeybees are kept animals.

    They aren't threatened with extinction for lack of beekeeping. The decline that worries scientists is that of native bees.

    3. Honeybees can harm native bees.

    A 2017 review found that most competition studies indicated negative effects of managed bees on wild bees, and that a large share of the disease studies pointed to transmission risks. The evidence is still debated, but the claim that honey "saves the bees" doesn't hold up.

    4. Bees may have experiences.

    The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024) recognizes a realistic possibility of consciousness in insects. Research shows bees learn, play and respond flexibly to stimuli.

    5. Beekeeping also kills bees.

    When honey is harvested, when hives are transported and when queens are replaced.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Mallinger, Gaines-Day and Gratton, 2017, *PLoS ONE*53% of competition studies found negative effects of managed bees on wild bees; 70% of pathogen studies pointed to potential negative effectsA systematic review; later studies find mixed results
    The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024)A realistic possibility of consciousness in insectsSigned by hundreds of researchers
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: beekeeping helps crop pollination

    The most serious version points out that many crops depend on pollination by honeybees, and that beekeeping is important for food production.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Pollination doesn't depend on eating honey.

    It's a service that can be paid for separately, as already happens.

    Native bees pollinate too.

    And often more efficiently for certain crops.

    It doesn't answer the question about honey.

    Buying honey isn't the same as supporting pollination.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Honey is a borderline case, and there are honest debates about insect experience. But the argument used to defend it, that it saves the bees, is false. If there's uncertainty about what bees feel, and easy alternatives to honey, caution recommends leaving it to them.

Contradictions

Says they want to save the bees.

But doesn't support native bees, which are in decline.

Accepts that honey is the bees' food.

And replaces it with sugar.

Doesn't eat other insect products.

The "they don't feel" criterion is used only here.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The decline of bees is a real and serious problem, and pollination is essential for food production. Well-run beekeeping can play a role in agriculture, and insect experience is indeed an open question.

But

But buying honey doesn't protect the bees that are in decline, and may even harm them. Protecting bees means protecting their habitats, reducing pesticides and planting flowers, not eating their food reserve.
