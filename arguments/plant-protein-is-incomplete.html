G2

"Plant protein is incomplete"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about health and nutrition, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on health grounds, anyone who manages to be healthy eating animals, and many people do, would have no reason to change. And if an animal product turned out to be demonstrably healthier, the same argument would start defending exploitation. The eater's health says nothing about whether what is done to the animal is just.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's true that many plant proteins, on their own, are low in some essential amino acid, such as lysine in cereals. But no one eats just one food. Plant foods contain all nine essential amino acids, in different proportions, and eating a varied diet over the day provides enough of each. The idea that foods must be combined in the same meal dates from the 1970s and has been abandoned. And traditional pairings such as rice and beans already combine a cereal and a legume well.

Short answer

Plant foods have all the essential amino acids, just in different proportions. Cereals have less lysine, legumes have more; together, over the day, they complete each other. The idea that everything must be combined in the same meal is an old myth, abandoned even by the person who popularized it. Rice and beans, an everyday dish in much of the world, is already an excellent combination. And soy has an amino acid profile close to that of animal proteins.

For children

Protein is like a necklace made of beads of different colors. Every food has all the colors, but some have more of one color and less of another. When you eat a variety of foods during the day, like rice, beans, lentils and peanuts, your body puts all the beads together and makes the complete necklace.

Ask a question back

"If rice and beans already combine the amino acids, what's missing?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"It lacks amino acids. Plant protein has low biological value. You have to combine everything in the same meal."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Proteins are made of amino acids, and nine of them are essential: they must come from the diet.
  2. P2Many plant proteins have less of some essential amino acid, such as lysine in cereals.
  3. P3Animal proteins have all the essential amino acids in proportions close to what we need.
  4. CSo only animal proteins are complete, and plant diets will have deficiencies.

P1, P2 and P3 are true. The problem lies in the conclusion: a diet isn't made of a single food, and variety makes up for the differences between sources.

The argument isn't valid: nothing about a varied diet follows from premises about isolated foods.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument assesses isolated foods, but people eat diets:

    SourceAmino acid in smaller amountsWhat makes up for it
    Cereals (rice, wheat, corn)LysineLegumes
    Legumes (beans, lentils, chickpeas)MethionineCereals, nuts and seeds
    SoyBalanced profileClose to animal proteins
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Plant foods have all the essential amino acids.

    What changes is the proportions. There's no common plant food completely lacking an essential amino acid.

    2. Combining in the same meal is a myth.

    The idea of mandatory "protein combining" was popularized in the 1970s. Today we know the body continuously recycles amino acids and keeps a circulating pool of them, and that variety over the day is enough. The author who popularized the rule, Frances Moore Lappé, revised it herself in the 1981 edition of Diet for a Small Planet.

    3. Science confirms adequacy.

    A 2019 review concluded that concern about amino acids in vegetarian diets had been overstated, and that legumes, nuts and seeds are enough for protein adequacy in adults.

    4. Rice and beans combine well.

    The traditional dish joins a cereal, low in lysine, and a legume, low in methionine, and each largely makes up for what the other lacks.

    5. "Biological value" measures an isolated food.

    The measure is useful in the lab, but it doesn't describe a real diet, which includes many foods.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Mariotti and Gardner, 2019, *Nutrients*Concern about amino acid deficiency in vegetarian diets has been overstated; minimally varied diets provide enoughScientific review
    Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, 2016Proteins from a variety of plant sources, eaten over the day, provide enough amino acidsOfficial position
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: you have to eat more

    The most serious version grants that plant diets can provide all the amino acids, but notes that because of lower digestibility and the proportions, it may be necessary to eat a little more total protein.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Eating a little more is easy.

    Just include legumes, soy and nuts regularly.

    That's planning, not impossibility.

    Every diet requires some planning.

    The difference is small for adults with a varied diet.

    Nutrition bodies don't consider it an obstacle.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Even if one had to eat a few more beans, that wouldn't justify raising and killing animals. The question remains: if plants provide everything we need, what justifies making animals suffer to get the same protein?

Contradictions

Eats rice and beans.

And doesn't think that dish lacks protein.

Doesn't ask about the amino acids of people who eat badly.

The concern only comes up with vegans.

The animals they eat get amino acids from plants.

Cattle and chickens eat soy and corn.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that plant proteins have amino acid profiles different from animal ones, and that a diet based on a single plant food would be inadequate. Nutrition deserves attention, and the idea of variety is important.

But

But variety is exactly what solves the problem. With legumes, cereals, soy and nuts over the day, plant protein is complete, and the need for animals disappears.
