L15

"Vegans are crazy; they have mental problems"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

calling someone crazy is a way of not answering what they say. If the vegan question is whether it's just to make animals suffer for a taste, the mental health of the person asking doesn't change the answer. The idea that vegans have "mental problems" mixes an insult with a misread statistical association, and it treats mental health as something to mock, which is unfair to anyone who lives with a disorder.

Short answer

Calling someone crazy doesn't answer the question: is it just to make animals suffer for a taste? On mental health, some studies find an association between vegetarian diets and symptoms of depression, but the authors themselves say it isn't possible to know what causes what, and there are signs that many people stop eating meat after a diagnosis. And using mental disorders as an insult is unfair to those who live with them, whether they eat meat or not.

For children

When someone doesn't know how to answer a question, sometimes they call the person who asked a name. But the name-calling doesn't answer anything. If a person asks "is it right to hurt the little pig?", calling them crazy doesn't change what the little pig feels.

Ask a question back

"If a person with depression says two plus two is four, does the sum become wrong?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Vegans are all crazy. You have to be nuts to cry over a chicken."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1People with mental disorders tend to have less reliable beliefs and behavior.
  2. P2Some studies associate vegetarian diets with more symptoms of depression and eating disorders.
  3. P3Caring intensely about farm animals is a sign of emotional imbalance.
  4. CSo vegans' ideas don't deserve to be taken seriously.

P2 has a basis in some studies, with important caveats. The problem lies in P1 and P3: judging an idea by the health of whoever holds it is an attack on the person, and caring about others' suffering isn't a symptom of illness.

The argument is invalid: it's ad hominem. Even if the premise about health were true, it would say nothing about the animals.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument mixes three things:

    ClaimTypeStatus
    "Vegans are crazy"InsultNot an argument
    "There's an association between vegetarianism and some symptoms"EmpiricalFound in some studies, with uncertain cause
    "So what they say about animals is wrong"LogicalDoesn't follow: it's an attack on the person
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's ad hominem.

    The truth of a claim doesn't depend on the health of whoever makes it. An animal's pain doesn't get smaller because the person pointing it out has depression, anxiety or any other condition.

    2. The association doesn't show cause.

    A 2021 meta-analysis found higher depression scores in vegetarians, with great variation between studies, and the authors stress that the direction of the relationship can't be established. There are signs that many people start avoiding meat after a diagnosis, not the other way round (G16, G19).

    3. Sensitivity to suffering isn't illness.

    Caring about others' suffering is one of the foundations of any morality. If it were a symptom, anyone who cries at an injured child would be ill too.

    4. The insult is unfair to people with mental disorders.

    Using "crazy" or "mental problem" as an insult reinforces the stigma against people who already suffer, vegan or not.

    5. Prejudice against vegans is documented.

    Research shows vegans are the target of negative attitudes equal to or greater than those directed at several groups that are commonly targets of prejudice. Pathologizing an opponent is a well-known way to dismiss them without discussing what they say.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Ocklenburg and Borawski, 2021Meta-analysis: vegetarians had higher depression scores, with great heterogeneity; the direction of the relationship can't be establishedJournal of Affective Disorders, 294
    MacInnis and Hodson, 2017Omnivores evaluated vegetarians and vegans equally or more negatively than several common prejudice target groupsGroup Processes & Intergroup Relations, 20(6)
    Minson and Monin, 2012Meat eaters tend to disparage vegetarians because they expect to be judged by themSocial Psychological and Personality Science, 3(2)
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: obsession with the subject shows imbalance

    The most serious version says: it isn't the idea, it's the intensity. People who talk about animal suffering all the time, are outraged at every meal and cut ties over it would be showing an unhealthy fixation.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Intensity doesn't decide truth.

    A person can talk too much about a subject and still be right about it.

    Comparing with other causes helps.

    People devoted to fighting child labor or hunger aren't called ill for it.

    Some people's behavior doesn't define the group.

    If someone handles their indignation badly, that's a personal matter, not an answer to the question about animals (L1).

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Even if some vegan had a mental disorder, the question would remain the same: is it just to make animals suffer and die for the pleasure of taste? The answer doesn't depend on who asks it.

Contradictions

Doesn't reject the ideas of people with depression on other subjects.

A sum or a fact still holds.

Admires people moved by the suffering of children or dogs.

But calls people moved by pigs ill.

Condemns the stigma against mental disorders.

But uses the label as an insult.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that mental health matters, that there are studies on the relationship between diet and depressive symptoms worth attention, and that vegans, like anyone, can suffer from indignation and need support. Looking after one's own mental health is part of sustainable activism.

But

But the health of whoever holds an idea doesn't decide whether it's true, and using mental disorders as an insult is unfair to everyone who lives with them.
