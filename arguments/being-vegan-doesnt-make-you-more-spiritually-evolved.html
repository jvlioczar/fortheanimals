D15

"Being vegan doesn't make you more spiritually evolved"

The answer in one sentence

the phrase is right about one thing: giving up eating animals doesn't make anyone a better person in every way, and spiritual pride is a real problem. But it swaps the question. The issue isn't who is more evolved, but whether it's right to cause exploitation, suffering and death to animals who have done nothing. On its own spiritual terms, the phrase doesn't hold up: if there is a benevolent being or a law of consequences, such as karma or divine justice, it would fall to whoever defends the harm to show why it would be more right than avoiding it. Many traditions teach the opposite, from the ahimsa of Jainism, Hinduism and Buddhism to the Jewish prohibition on making an animal suffer needlessly and the hadiths on mercy toward animals. And even without any religion, the same demand still holds: whoever causes harm to another for their own benefit needs to justify it.

Reply

I agree: being vegan doesn't make anyone more evolved, and thinking you're superior is a problem. But the conversation isn't about me or you, it's about the animals. If there is a kind being or a law of consequences, someone would have to show how causing suffering and death to the innocent could be the most right thing. Many traditions, from Indian ahimsa to mercy toward animals in Judaism and Islam, teach people to avoid harm. And even without religion, whoever causes harm to another for their own benefit needs a justification.

For children

Nobody becomes better than others just because of what they eat. That's true. But the question isn't who is better. The question is whether it's right to make an animal suffer when we can avoid it. Almost everyone who believes in kindness thinks that hurting someone who has done nothing wrong isn't the way.

Ask a question back

"Setting aside who is more evolved, do you think making an animal suffer needlessly is right?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"Being vegan doesn't make you more spiritually evolved. Spirituality is in the heart, not on the plate."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1A person's spiritual worth depends on inner virtues, such as humility, love and compassion, not on rules about what one eats.
  2. P2Diet doesn't measure those virtues: there are proud and cruel vegans, and there are people who eat meat and are generous and humble.
  3. CSo veganism has no spiritual importance, and there is no spiritual reason to stop eating animals.

P1 is an idea shared by many traditions, and P2 is true. The problem lies in the leap to C. Showing that diet doesn't measure a person's worth doesn't show that what happens to the animal is indifferent. The compassion P1 values is precisely a reason to take the suffering of others seriously, animals' included.

The argument is invalid: what follows from the premises is that veganism doesn't prove superiority, not that the harm done to animals doesn't matter.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The phrase answers a question nobody asked:

    What the phrase discussesWhat it leaves outWhat goes unanswered
    The spiritual worth of whoever is veganThe animal exploited and killedWhether causing that harm is right
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The phrase swaps the question.

    Veganism isn't, first of all, a practice of personal improvement, but a refusal to take part in harm done to others. Saying it doesn't make anyone more evolved may be true, but it doesn't answer whether the harm is justified. It's like saying that returning a lost wallet doesn't make anyone a saint: true, and still the wallet has an owner.

    2. On its own spiritual terms, the burden is on whoever defends the harm.

    If there is a benevolent being, or a system of consequences such as karma or divine justice, the question is: would it be more right, before it, to cause exploitation, suffering and death to innocent individuals, or to avoid them when possible? Whoever says the harm is spiritually indifferent needs to show how perfect goodness or a moral law of the universe would approve of the avoidable suffering of those who have done nothing. Without that argument, the phrase merely asserts what it would need to demonstrate.

    3. Many traditions teach not causing harm.

    The examples are varied:

    • Jainism, Hinduism and Buddhism. Ahimsa, non-violence toward all living beings, is a central virtue in these traditions. In Buddhism, the first of the five precepts is not to take the life of any sentient being, animals included. In Jainism, non-violence leads to strict vegetarianism.
    • Judaism. The principle of tza'ar ba'alei chayim forbids causing unnecessary suffering to animals. Most authorities consider it a commandment of the Torah, though some treat it as a rabbinic rule.
    • Christianity. The Book of Proverbs (12:10) says the righteous person cares for the life of their animal. The Catechism of the Catholic Church states that it is contrary to human dignity to cause animals to suffer or die needlessly. The Seventh-day Adventist Church has recommended a vegetarian diet since the 19th century.
    • Islam. Hadiths collected by al-Bukhari tell of a man who was forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog, and of a woman who was punished for locking up a cat until it died, giving it neither food nor water. In the first account, the Prophet says there is a reward for doing good to every living being.
    • Sikhism and Taoism. The community kitchen of Sikh temples (langar) serves vegetarian food, and some Sikh groups advocate a meat-free diet. Taoist religious orders often recommend vegetarianism to reduce harm to other beings.
    4. Honesty about the diversity of the traditions.

    Almost all of these traditions also permit eating animals, or have strands that do. Judaism and Islam regulate slaughter but don't forbid it. The Catechism, in the paragraph before the one cited, considers using animals for food legitimate. Adventist vegetarianism is recommended, not required: in a large study of Adventists in North America, about half weren't vegetarian. In India, according to a 2021 survey, 44% of Hindus and 25% of Buddhists say they are vegetarian, against 92% of Jains. Many Sikhs eat meat. So the point isn't that "religion commands being vegan". The point is more modest: care for animals and the refusal of unnecessary harm have deep roots in many traditions, and the phrase can't treat them as foreign to spiritual life.

    5. Without religion, the demand remains.

    The moral debate doesn't depend on religious belief. In almost every ethics, causing harm to another to obtain a benefit for oneself is something that calls for justification. Nobody needs faith to understand that hurting someone for pleasure or convenience is a problem. The question, then, is the same: what justifies imposing suffering and death on an animal when there are alternatives? That question doesn't depend on who is more evolved, nor on whether karma or God exists.

    6. The argument is right about pride.

    Feeling superior because of one's own diet is a real problem, and not only among vegans. Research in psychology has found feelings of spiritual superiority linked to certain spiritual practices, and associated with narcissism. But that is a criticism of an attitude, not an answer to the moral question. An arrogant vegan still causes less harm to animals, and a humble person who eats meat still takes part in that harm.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: veganism has become a badge of purity, and that's what I criticize

    The most serious version says: I don't deny that animals matter. What I criticize is turning a diet into a sign of spiritual elevation. In many settings, eating a certain way has become a way of showing oneself purer than others, and that is vanity disguised as virtue.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The criticism is fair, but it's about something else.

    One can reject spiritual vanity and, at the same time, recognize that harm to animals calls for justification. The two don't compete.

    The purity of the speaker doesn't change the harm.

    If an argument is right, it stays right even when said by someone vain. The animal doesn't suffer less because the vegan speaking about them is arrogant.

    The best answer to vanity is humility, not harm.

    If the problem is pride, the solution is to stop comparing, not to keep causing avoidable suffering. Many traditions that condemn pride also condemn cruelty.

Contradictions

Values compassion as a spiritual virtue.

But treats the suffering of those who can feel as indifferent.

Condemns the pride of those who think they're superior.

But uses the criticism to avoid examining their own conduct.

Says what matters is the heart.

But a compassionate heart is precisely what leads one to avoid harm.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that diet doesn't measure a person's worth, and that feeling superior because of it is a real problem, as discussed in L1 and L8. There are arrogant vegans, and there are people who eat meat and are generous and humble. It's also right that spirituality goes far beyond what one eats, and that each person's faith deserves respect.

But

But the question was never who is more evolved. The question is whether it's right to cause avoidable suffering and death to animals, and it still stands with or without religion.
