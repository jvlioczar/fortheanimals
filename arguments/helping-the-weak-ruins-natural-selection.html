N5

"Helping the weak ruins natural selection"

The answer in one sentence

natural selection doesn't work for animals' wellbeing; it favours whatever passes genes on, and that is often terrible for those who carry those genes. A species feels nothing, so "the good of the species" can only mean the good of the animals still to come. Weighed honestly, the price of adaptation is paid by generations of individuals who suffer and die young. And when the victims are human, we call the idea of letting the weak die to improve the species by its proper name: eugenics.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Reply

Natural selection doesn't pick what's best for animals; it picks what leaves more offspring, and sometimes that means having hundreds of young that starve. A species doesn't suffer; individuals do. And if what worries you is passing on bad genes, reproduction can be prevented without letting anyone die. With humans, you know the name for the idea of not treating the weak to improve the species: eugenics.

For children

When a classmate hurts their leg, we don't leave them behind so that only the fastest are left in the class. We help. It's similar with animals: the one who is suffering is the little animal, not the "species".

Ask a question back

"Do you think we shouldn't vaccinate children, so that only those born resistant to disease survive and have children?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"If you save the sick or weak animal, it'll pass those genes on and the species gets worse. Nature knows what it's doing."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Helping weak or sick individuals increases the chance that they reproduce.
  2. P2This spreads less adapted genes, and later generations become more vulnerable.
  3. P3The harm to later generations is greater than the benefit to those helped.
  4. CSo we shouldn't help the weaker wild animals.

This objection has defenders in environmental ethics and veterinary medicine. The philosopher Holmes Rolston (1992) held that predation improves both predators and prey over the generations. The veterinarians James Kirkwood and Anthony Sainsbury (1996) argued that giving the less fit a second chance amounts to harming the fitter ones of future generations. P1 and P2 may be true in some cases. P3 is the unsupported premise.

The argument is valid, but P3 doesn't hold up, and the conclusion ignores alternatives that solve the problem in P2.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument assumes that "better adapted" means "better for the animals". They're different things:

    TermWhat it means in biologyWhat the argument assumes
    FitnessSuccess in leaving offspring in an environmentA better life for the animal
    Natural selectionA process without a goal that filters who reproduces moreA process that improves the species
    Good of the speciesA phrase with no subject: the species doesn't feelSomething that weighs against individuals' suffering

    Once these ideas are separated, premise P3 has no support.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Species don't feel.

    A species has no point of view: it gains and loses nothing. The supposed benefit can only be for individuals of future generations, never for the animal that suffers and dies now (Cunha, 2022, p. 254; A10).

    2. Fitness isn't wellbeing.

    A trait that passes genes on efficiently may be bad for whoever carries it. Having an enormous number of offspring is very efficient for a species to persist and terrible for the young, most of whom die soon after birth (Cunha, 2022, p. 254; N1). Biology describes what reproduces, not what lives well.

    3. Who pays the bill.

    An advantageous trait only spreads after many generations in which large numbers of individuals without it die young. With harms and benefits on the scale, the harms of the process are enormous, and it isn't obvious that the benefit to descendants makes up for them (Cunha, 2022, p. 255).

    4. There's a third way.

    If the problem is reproduction by those with genes that harm their carriers, one can help the individual and reduce the chance that it reproduces, for example with contraception. Measures that reduce the number of births don't prevent the better adapted traits from prevailing (Cunha, 2022, p. 255; N19).

    5. The human test.

    Vaccines, glasses, insulin and caesarean sections let people who wouldn't survive without help live and have children. Those who defend letting the weak die in nature don't propose giving up these resources. When someone proposed this for humans, it was called eugenics, and the result was one of the most condemned episodes of the 20th century (Cunha, 2022, p. 256).

    6. The predator isn't a health screener for the species.

    Predators prefer to attack the most vulnerable: the young, the old and the sick. This may remove sick animals, but it does so through the suffering and death of those same animals, and it doesn't stop the disease from spreading (N4).

    7. You can't get an ought from an is.

    From natural selection, the argument draws a norm about who should be helped. But a fact, by itself, doesn't say what is right: reaching a moral conclusion requires a moral premise, and that premise is what needs defending. This leap is Hume's law; see also Describing is not evaluating, in Start here.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: conservation genetics confirms the risk

    The most serious version points out that the concern isn't merely philosophical. Conservation genetics shows that shielding animals from environmental pressures can reduce their fitness. In the study by Araki and colleagues (2007), trout reared in captivity for a single generation already reproduced much less in the wild, and the loss accumulated with each generation. If helping at scale removes selective pressure from populations, the animals of the future may become sicker and more dependent, and suffer more.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The risk depends on scale and method.

    The study concerns programmes that replace part of a population with animals born and raised in captivity. That shows large interventions need to be studied first, not that helping an injured animal or vaccinating a population is wrong.

    It's a design problem, not a prohibition.

    If the risk is reproductive, the answer is to combine help with control of reproduction, as is already proposed (N19). The objection points to a condition for helping well, not a reason not to help.

    The counter-reply doesn't say what to do about those suffering now.

    Even if there were a genetic cost, it would have to be weighed against the suffering prevented. The objection only works if that suffering doesn't count, and that is what would need to be shown.

    With humans, we accept the cost.

    Modern medicine probably allows more unfavourable genetic variants to be passed on. No one concludes that we should stop treating the sick; the conclusion is that medicine should carry on and deal with the consequences.

Status quo bias

In-group bias

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that large-scale interventions can have genetic effects on populations, and that conservation genetics has serious data on this. It's also right that helping without thinking about reproduction can create problems for later generations.

But

But that calls for well-designed help, combined with reproductive care and research. It doesn't justify letting individuals suffer so that a species, which feels nothing, can be "better".
