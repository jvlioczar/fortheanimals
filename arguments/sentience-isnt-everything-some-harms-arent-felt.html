A28

"Sentience isn't everything: some harms aren't felt"

The answer in one sentence

the objection gets one thing right: some harms don't involve pain, such as dying in one's sleep, being betrayed without knowing it or being assaulted while unconscious. But it confuses two questions. The sentience criterion answers who can be harmed: beings who have experiences. It doesn't say which theory of well-being is correct. Hedonism, preference-satisfaction theories and objective-list theories disagree about what makes a life go well, but they all deal with someone's good, and the harms "that aren't felt" still fall on someone who feels. The objection refutes narrow hedonism, not the sentience criterion.

Reply

I agree with you: not every harm hurts. Dying in your sleep, being betrayed without knowing, those are all harms. But look at who suffers them: always someone who feels, who has wants, who had a life ahead of them. No one betrays a rock or steals a chair's future. The sentience criterion doesn't say only pain matters; it says who can be harmed. And by that same reasoning, killing an animal painlessly is still a harm, because it takes away everything they would still live.

For children

Imagine someone secretly reads your diary, and you never find out. It didn't hurt, but they did something wrong to you. Now imagine someone reads a rock. That makes no sense, right? You can only wrong someone who is someone, who feels and wants things. Animals are someone too.

Ask a question back

"If some harms aren't felt, who do you know that could suffer them without being someone who feels?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"It's not all pain and pleasure. Killing someone in their sleep doesn't hurt and it's still wrong. So sentience isn't everything."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1The sentience criterion says that only what affects a being's experiences matters.
  2. P2Some harms don't affect experiences: a painless death, a betrayal the victim never discovers, an assault on someone unconscious who remembers nothing.
  3. P3A theory that doesn't recognize these harms is wrong.
  4. CSo the sentience criterion is wrong, or at least incomplete: something beyond sentience must count.

P2 is plausible, and so is P3: most people, and many philosophers, recognize these harms. The problem lies in P1. The sentience criterion doesn't say that only what is felt matters; it says that only those who feel can be harmed.

The argument is valid in form, but P1 attributes to the criterion a thesis it doesn't hold. The objection hits hedonism, a theory of well-being, and not the criterion of who counts.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    QuestionWho answers itExample answer
    Who can be harmed or benefited?The criterion of moral considerationSentient beings, who have experiences
    What makes someone's life better or worse?The theory of well-beingPleasure and pain; preferences; objective goods; or a combination
    What should be done?The theory of right actionUtilitarianism, rights, contractualism, virtues

    The objection treats the first question as if it were the second. Sentience, here, is the capacity to have experiences: for there to be someone there, with a point of view of their own.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Two different questions.

    The philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha shows that the sentience criterion says which beings should be considered, not in which ways they can be harmed or benefited. That second question belongs to theories of well-being. Accepting the criterion is compatible with hedonism, but doesn't require it (*A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. VI, ch. 5).

    2. The three main theories deal with someone's good.

    The literature usually divides theories of well-being into three families: hedonist, preference (or desire) satisfaction, and objective list, as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarizes (Crisp, entry "Well-Being"). The entry on the moral status of animals (Gruen and Monsó) uses the same division in animal ethics. Hedonism counts experiences; preference theories count what the individual wants; objective lists count goods such as knowledge, friendship or achievement. In all of them, the good is the good of a subject: experiences, preferences and goods such as friendship presuppose someone with a mind.

    3. The objection's examples fall on sentient beings.

    An assault on someone unconscious who never finds out what happened is a harm to a person. The same act done to an inanimate object would harm nothing. Cunha explains these cases as the violation of preferences, actual ones or ones the person would have if they knew, and preferences only exist in those who have a mental life. The case refutes hedonism, not the sentience criterion.

    4. Death is the most important example.

    Dying painlessly causes no suffering, but it takes from the one who dies all the good experiences they would still have. This is the deprivation account of death, defended by Thomas Nagel in the essay "Death" (1970) and discussed in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry "Death" (Luper). Cunha notes that, to assess this harm, we compare what the individual would live through in each scenario: whoever raises the objection is already using the sentience criterion without noticing (*A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. VI, ch. 4). See B2.

    5. The objection turns against those who use it to defend exploitation.

    If there are harms beyond pain, then "painless" slaughter doesn't remove the harm: the animal loses their future, has their preferences frustrated and is deprived of goods such as social bonds, play and exploring their surroundings. The list of capabilities Martha Nussbaum (2006) proposes for animals includes life, bodily integrity, emotions, affiliation and play. And animals have measurable preferences: animal welfare science studies what they choose and how hard they work to obtain something (Dawkins, 1990).

    6. The theories converge on the essentials.

    Cunha argues that almost all serious theories accept that suffering is bad and happiness good in themselves, and that, whichever theory one adopts, the typical lives of exploited animals go badly: they suffer, are prevented from acting and die young (*A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. XI, ch. 2).

    7. The experience machine doesn't help the objection either.

    Robert Nozick (1974) imagined a machine that would give someone whatever experiences they wanted, and concluded that most people would refuse to plug in. The case is an objection to hedonism. Whoever refuses the machine is still a sentient being, with preferences about their own life.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: if there are objective goods, plants and ecosystems can be harmed too

    The most serious version says: if well-being includes objective goods that don't depend on feeling, then perhaps living beings without sentience also have a good. This is Paul Taylor's biocentrism (1986): every plant, animal or micro-organism would be a teleological center of life, with a good of its own. The sentience criterion would then be too narrow.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    A biological "good of its own" isn't well-being.

    Saying a plant grows better with water describes how an organism functions; it doesn't show there is someone for whom this is good. Critics cited in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry "Environmental Ethics" note that this notion of good is descriptive, not prescriptive: even a virus would have a "good" in that sense.

    Objective lists also presuppose a subject.

    Knowledge, friendship, achievement and affiliation are goods of someone with a mind. Gruen and Monsó also note that grounding the list in what is "normal for the species" risks a naturalistic fallacy (see C1).

    Even if accepted, it doesn't help exploitation.

    If plants had a good, animal farming, which consumes far more plants than a plant-based diet, would be the first to be condemned (see A4).

Biases

Motivated reasoning

The objection appears in order to set the criterion aside, not to apply it better

*A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. X, ch. 5

Framing effect

Describing sentientism as "only pain and pleasure" makes the objection look decisive

Illusion of explanatory depth

Thinking one knows what the criterion claims, without having examined the distinction between the questions

Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "if some harms aren't felt, the capacity to feel doesn't define who can be harmed".

Contradictions

Says there are harms beyond pain.

But usually accepts "painless" slaughter as a solution.

Uses the death of someone asleep as an example of harm.

And doesn't apply the same reasoning to animals killed young.

Says sentience isn't everything.

But all its examples of harm involve someone who feels.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that not every harm is pain, and that simple hedonism struggles with painless death, undiscovered betrayal and assault on someone unconscious. It's also right that preferences, bonds and opportunities can be part of a good life. These questions are legitimately debated in philosophy.

But

The mistake is concluding from this that sentience doesn't define who counts. All these harms fall on someone who feels. Recognizing them widens what we owe animals rather than narrowing it.
