K6

"Vegans should fight for laws, not diets"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about effectiveness and individual responsibility, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended only by each person's impact, anyone who proved their purchase changes nothing would be let off. But no one accepts this for other harms: taking part in an injustice is still wrong even when each person's share is small.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's a false dichotomy. Personal consistency and political action don't compete; they complement each other. Many animal protection laws were won by people who had also changed their own lives. And those who say change must only be political can be asked the same question: are they supporting the laws they say they prefer? Often, the argument serves to do neither one nor the other (E2).

Short answer

You don't have to choose. Not eating animals takes no time away from political action, and the two reinforce each other: many animal protection laws were won by people who had also changed their own lives. Besides, those who prefer the political route can support the laws already under discussion. If you think laws are the way, you can support both.

For children

You can do both: choose food that doesn't hurt animals and also ask leaders to make laws that protect animals. One helps the other. People who look after animals every day are more eager to fight for them.

Ask a question back

"Would you support laws to protect farmed animals, if they were proposed?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Diet is individualism. Do politics instead of pestering people."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Change at scale depends on laws, public policy and structural change.
  2. P2Individual consumer choices have limited impact.
  3. P3Activists' time and energy are limited.
  4. CSo vegans should focus their efforts on politics, not on individual choices.

P1 is true. So is P2, in part. So is P3. The problem lies in the conclusion: not eating animals doesn't take time away from political action, and the two reinforce each other.

The argument is valid as a strategic argument for activists. It fails as a personal excuse for eating animals.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument presents two actions as mutually exclusive:

    ActionDoes it take time from the other?
    No longer eating animalsNo: it's an everyday choice
    Fighting for laws and policiesNot prevented by individual choice
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's a false dichotomy.

    Changing one's own consumption and fighting for political change aren't alternatives.

    2. The two reinforce each other.

    Personal consistency gives political action credibility, and changes in consumption create demand that makes new policies easier.

    3. Laws depend on social support.

    Legal change happens when enough people are convinced. Individual consumption is one way to express and spread that conviction.

    4. The argument often serves to do nothing.

    The useful question is whether the person using it supports the legal changes they say they prefer. If they do, great, and nothing stops them from also changing their own consumption (E2).

    5. Individual choice has a real effect.

    Each purchase has an expected effect on production (K1).

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Kagan, *Do I Make a Difference?* (2011)Each purchase has a real expected effect on productionShows the value of individual choice
    EU Cosmetics Regulation (EC) No 1223/2009Bans animal testing of cosmetics and the sale of cosmetics tested on animals in the EUAn example of regulatory change driven by public pressure; also used in I2
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: focusing on consumption is a trap

    The most serious version says that "conscious consumption" talk shifts onto individuals a responsibility that belongs to companies and governments, and that the vegan movement sometimes falls into that trap.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The criticism is of an exclusive focus, not of individual choice.

    No one has to choose.

    No longer paying for deaths isn't luxury "conscious consumption".

    It's refusing to take part in a harm.

    The question to the individual remains.

    Even if laws are the main route.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Campaigning for laws for animals is great, and it's consistent with not eating them. The moral question doesn't wait for the laws: until they change, what justifies paying for animals to be killed?

What the argument gets right

What is true

Political and structural changes are essential, and the animal movement should invest in them. Protection laws, an end to harmful subsidies and transition policies can reach far more animals than isolated individual choices.

But

But fighting for laws doesn't excuse personal consistency, and personal consistency doesn't prevent fighting for laws. Each strengthens the other, and neither justifies continuing to pay for animals to be killed.
