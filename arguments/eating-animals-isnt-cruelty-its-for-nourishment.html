B19

"Eating animals isn't cruelty; it's for nourishment"

The answer in one sentence

the argument defines cruelty as gratuitous harm or harm caused for pleasure and concludes that eating animals, because it has a purpose, isn't cruel. But the eater's purpose doesn't change what the animal goes through. Taken as a whole, common farming and slaughter practices include prolonged confinement, back-to-back induced pregnancies (in dairy cows and sows), separation of mothers and young, mutilations often without anesthesia, long transport, and slaughter in which some animals aren't rendered unconscious on the first attempt. In law and in ordinary usage, cruelty usually means unnecessary suffering, and nutrition bodies hold that well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets can be nutritionally adequate. If nourishment can be had without that suffering, what remains as the reason, for most meals, is habit, taste and price. Many anti-cruelty laws leave these practices out only because they expressly exempt them. Judged as it would be if it were done to a dog or a person "for a purpose", this set of practices fits what the argument itself calls cruelty.

Reply

I agree that almost nobody eats meat out of malice. But your intention doesn't change what the animal goes through. On many farms, the sow spends weeks in a crate where she can barely turn around, the dairy cow is made pregnant again every cycle and loses her calf soon after birth, the piglet is castrated, in many places without anesthesia, and at slaughter some animals aren't unconscious after the first attempt. If someone did that to a dog "for a purpose", what would you call it? And the purpose, nourishing yourself, can be reached without any of it: dietetics associations consider well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets adequate. In truth, day to day, what decides the plate is mostly taste and habit, not need.

For children

Imagine someone keeps a dog in a box where it can't turn around, takes its puppies away right after they're born, and then says: "it's not meanness, it's because I need to." The dog suffers just the same, whatever the reason. Something similar happens to pigs, cows and chickens. And we can grow up strong and healthy eating beans, rice, fruit, vegetables and other plants, without any animal having to go through that.

Ask a question back

"If you could nourish yourself just as well without any animal going through this, would that suffering still be necessary?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"Nobody kills a steer out of malice. Cruelty is hurting animals for fun. Eating is something else: it's to feed yourself."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Cruelty is causing suffering for pleasure, out of indifference, or without a legitimate purpose.
  2. P2Eating animals has a legitimate purpose: nourishment.
  3. P3The suffering involved in production is a means to that purpose, not an end sought for its own sake.
  4. CTherefore, eating animals isn't cruelty.

The most serious version rests on three distinctions that deserve attention. The first is necessity: eating is a requirement of life, not a whim. The second is intention: people who buy meat don't want the animal to suffer, and ordinary morality distinguishes those who do harm on purpose from those who cause it as a side effect. The third separates cruelty from killing: it's possible to kill without cruelty, as in euthanizing a sick animal, so death alone wouldn't make eating cruel.

The argument is valid if "cruelty" has the same meaning in the premises and the conclusion. It isn't sound: P2 holds only if the suffering is necessary for the purpose, and it isn't, because nourishment can be had without animals in most contexts; P3 treats as an unavoidable means a set of practices chosen for cost and convenience; and the argument slides from "cruelty" as a character trait of the eater to "cruelty" as a description of what the animal suffers.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument depends on how cruelty is defined and on what purpose is assigned to the act:

    LevelWhat the argument saysWhat actually follows
    DefinitionCruelty is gratuitous harm or harm for pleasureLaw and ordinary usage also include unnecessary suffering, even when there is some purpose
    PurposeEating is for nourishmentNourishment is legitimate, but can be achieved without animals in most contexts (G1)
    IntentionThe eater doesn't want the animal to sufferThat says something about the eater's character, not about what the animal goes through
    Real practiceSuffering would be a small, unavoidable side effectStandard practices involve large, foreseeable and avoidable suffering
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The eater's purpose doesn't change what the animal goes through.

    The pain of castration without anesthesia, the stress of weeks in a crate, or the fear in the slaughterhouse race are the same whatever the motive of whoever pays for the product. The argument describes the act from the consumer's point of view, which sees only the meal. From the animal's point of view, what matters is what happens to them.

    2. Taken as a whole, standard practices are cruel.

    The argument tends to think only of the moment of slaughter. But the animal's whole life is part of the cost:

    • Confinement. In Brazil, Ministry of Agriculture Normative Instruction No. 113/2020 still allows individual crates for sows in the first days of gestation, with adaptation deadlines running to 2045 (B10). In 2022, the scientific opinion of the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) on pig welfare recommended, according to specialist coverage, grouping sows from weaning and ending the use of farrowing crates.
    • Back-to-back pregnancies. Cows only produce milk after giving birth, so they are made pregnant repeatedly, usually by artificial insemination; so are sows (B5). A dairy cow can live about 20 years, but according to Penn State Extension, in commercial systems she is usually slaughtered at somewhere between 4.5 and 6 years.
    • Separation of mothers and young. In dairy production, it's common to separate the calf from the mother within the first hours or days (B5, B6). In 2023, EFSA recommended keeping the calf with the mother for at least one day, which suggests that current practice is often faster.
    • Mutilations. Castration, tail docking, beak trimming and dehorning are routine in several systems. The same 2022 EFSA opinion concluded, according to specialist coverage, that tail docking shouldn't be done and that surgical castration without anesthesia and analgesia shouldn't be performed. In Brazil, anesthesia for surgical castration of piglets becomes mandatory for everyone only in 2030 (B10).
    • Transport. In 2022, EFSA recommended shorter journeys, more space and temperature limits for cattle transport, which shows that current conditions cause welfare problems (B11).
    • Slaughter. In 2020, EFSA concluded that high-concentration carbon dioxide, used to stun pigs, is aversive and causes pain, fear and respiratory distress (B1). In a study at commercial Swedish slaughterhouses covering nearly a thousand cattle, 12.5% were inadequately stunned (16.7% of bulls and 6.5% of other cattle), according to Atkinson, Velarde and Algers (2013). At a scale of billions of animals a year, even small failure rates reach multitudes (B1).

    The simplest test is to swap the animal. If a person kept a dog in a box where it couldn't turn around, castrated it without anesthesia and sent it to its death, few would accept the defense "it wasn't malice, it was for a purpose". In Brazil, cruelty to dogs and cats has carried a prison sentence of two to five years since Law 14,064/2020, which added paragraph 1-A to article 32 of Law 9,605/1998. Done to human beings, the same set of practices would be described as cruelty without hesitation. The difference in judgment comes from the species, not from the nature of the act.

    3. In law and ordinary usage, cruelty includes unnecessary suffering.

    The argument picks the narrowest definition of cruelty, the one of sadism. But animal protection laws usually use another criterion. The UK Animal Welfare Act 2006 (section 4) makes it an offense to cause a protected animal "unnecessary" suffering. To decide whether it is unnecessary, the law itself directs consideration of whether the suffering could reasonably have been avoided or reduced, whether the conduct had a legitimate purpose, and whether the suffering was proportionate to that purpose. In other words: the law recognizes that purpose counts, as the argument says, but only when the suffering couldn't have been avoided and is proportionate. In Brazil, article 32 of Law 9,605/1998 punishes abuse and mistreatment, and paragraph 1 extends the penalty to anyone who performs a painful experiment on a live animal, "even for teaching or scientific purposes", when alternative methods exist. The logic is the same: having a purpose isn't enough when there's another way.

    And there is another way to nourishment. The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, the largest association of dietitians in the United States, stated in 2016 that appropriately planned vegetarian diets, including vegan diets, are healthful and nutritionally adequate at all stages of life; its current position, from 2025, covers adults and maintains that well-planned vegetarian and vegan dietary patterns can be nutritionally adequate. The joint position with Dietitians of Canada, from 2003, reached a similar conclusion, and the 2013 Australian Dietary Guidelines (NHMRC) also consider well-planned vegan diets adequate, with attention to vitamin B12. In Brazil, the Federal Council of Nutritionists published in 2022 a technical opinion guiding nutritionists in caring for vegetarians. These bodies stress planning and B12 supplementation, and there are contexts of extreme poverty or isolation in which the alternative isn't within reach (G1). But for someone choosing what to eat in a supermarket, the purpose "nourishment" doesn't require the animal product.

    4. What decides most meals is habit and taste.

    If nourishment can be had in several ways, choosing one of them has another reason. In practice, nobody picks the steak because the dish next to it lacks protein; they pick it because they like it, because it's customary, convenient or cheap. Research on justifications for eating meat finds mainly four: that it's natural, normal, necessary and nice (the "4Ns" of Piazza et al., 2015). "Necessary" shows up alongside "nice", and the argument tends to use the first to cover the second. Taste and habit are real reasons, but too weak to justify the set described in point 2 (J6, D1).

    5. Many laws fail to reach these practices only because they exempt them.

    If raising and slaughtering animals involved no relevant suffering, there would be no need to exclude it from the law. In the United States, most state anti-cruelty laws exempt so-called "customary", "common" or "accepted" farming practices. Attorney David Wolfson counted 28 states with exemptions of this kind in 1996, and later surveys report higher numbers, though the counts vary by date and criterion. Wolfson and Mariann Sullivan observed that the industry itself thereby defines what is legal: whatever becomes common becomes permitted. The Animal Legal Defense Fund lists among the covered practices confinement in crates and cages, castration and tail docking without pain relief, beak cutting and early separation of calves. The exemption is a political choice, not a finding that there is no suffering (D9, B10).

    6. Intention judges the person, not the harm.

    It's true that people who buy meat aren't sadists, and that difference matters for judging someone's character. But the moral question about the practice is a different one. Tom Regan, criticizing what he called the "cruelty-kindness" view, observed that cruelty and kindness describe motives and character, and that the absence of cruelty doesn't guarantee that an act is permissible (Regan, 1985; see B18 and A25). A kind person can fund a practice that causes great suffering without noticing. The argument is right to say the consumer isn't cruel in the sense of sadism; it's wrong to conclude that, therefore, what happens to the animal isn't cruel.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: nourishment is a legitimate purpose, and killing isn't the same as cruelty

    The most serious version concedes part of the criticism and falls back: even if it isn't strictly necessary, eating animals serves real human interests, such as practical nutrition, culture, pleasure and the livelihood of those who farm. The UK law itself accepts that a legitimate purpose can justify some suffering. And killing isn't cruel in itself: a quick, well-performed slaughter of an animal who lived well wouldn't be cruelty, even if one argues it's wrong for other reasons.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    A legitimate purpose requires proportionality.

    The UK law asks that suffering be unavoidable and proportionate to the purpose. Pleasure, habit and price hardly justify the set described in point 2, especially when there is an alternative.

    The counter-reply describes ideal slaughter, not real slaughter.

    The quick death of an animal who lived well is the exception. What people buy, in most cases, comes from systems with confinement, mutilations and stunning failures (B1, B3, B4).

    Separating cruelty from death doesn't save the argument.

    Even granting that killing isn't always cruel, much of what makes the practices cruel happens before death. And death is a harm for another reason (B2).

    The interests of those who make a living from farming count, but they call for transition, not absolution.

    It's reasonable to think about gradual change and support for producers. That doesn't make what is done today not cruel.

Contradictions

Says cruelty is suffering without necessity.

And accepts suffering that could be avoided with another meal.

Condemns those who mistreat dogs and cats, even "for a purpose".

And accepts similar practices on pigs and cows.

Says eating is for nourishment.

And chooses dishes by taste, habit and price.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The argument is right to distinguish the character of the person acting from the practice they fund. People who eat meat aren't sadists, and treating them as such harms the conversation. It's also right to recall that necessity matters: in situations of hunger, extreme poverty or isolation, causing some harm to survive may be justifiable, and the law also accepts that a legitimate purpose can weigh in favor of the person acting.

But

But purpose justifies only the suffering it makes necessary and proportionate. For someone choosing what to eat with alternatives within reach, nourishment doesn't require the animal product, and the whole of what the animals go through, from confinement to slaughter, fits the sense of cruelty that law and ordinary usage adopt.
