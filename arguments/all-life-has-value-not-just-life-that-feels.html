A29

"All life has value, not just life that feels"

The answer in one sentence

the phrase skips a question: value for whom? A plant, a fungus or a bacterium is alive, but there is no one there for whom things can go well or badly. Biological life matters a great deal, but as a condition for the experiences of those who feel, not the other way around. Including more beings doesn't make a criterion better by itself, and biocentrism doesn't say how to choose between lives, since living always costs other lives. In practice, putting an animal's life on the same level as a plant's or a bacterium's dilutes the weight of those who suffer. The sentience criterion doesn't dismiss non-sentient life: it gives it enormous value as a means to the good of the beings who feel.

Reply

I get the idea of respecting every form of life, and I think it's a beautiful one. But ask yourself: value for whom? A plant has no one inside for whom things go well or badly. And think about yourself: if someone offered to keep you alive forever, but you'd never feel anything again, would you take it? I doubt it. What we want to keep is the experience, and life matters because it makes that possible. Including more things doesn't make a criterion better; if it did, including rocks would be even fairer. And if all life is worth the same, the cow is worth the same as the grass she eats. That doesn't protect plants; it just makes the cow's suffering weigh less.

For children

A stone, a flower and a dog are in the garden. The flower is alive, like the dog. But if you step on the flower, it doesn't cry or feel scared. If you step on the dog, he feels pain. We take care of the flower because it's pretty and because animals and people need plants. And we take care of the dog also because he feels.

Ask a question back

"If what counts is just being alive, would you trade your conscious life for a coma you'd never wake from, as long as your body lived longer?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"Why only animals? All life is sacred, whether it feels or not. Pain only exists to keep the animal alive; what matters is life."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Pain and pleasure evolved because they help an organism avoid what would kill it and seek what keeps it alive.
  2. P2So sentience is a means, and the end it serves is life.
  3. P3Everything alive has a good of its own: it flourishes under some conditions and perishes under others.
  4. P4A moral criterion that includes more beings with a good of their own is fairer than one that includes fewer.
  5. CSo the right criterion is life, not sentience: all life has value in itself, whether it feels or not.

P1 is a reasonable hypothesis about evolution. The argument is valid in form, but P2 confuses the origin of a capacity with its value, P3 uses "good" and "want" in a metaphorical sense, and P4 treats the number included as a measure of fairness.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    QuestionWhat biocentrism answersWhat is left unanswered
    Who counts?Every living organismSentient beings that aren't organic, if they ever exist
    Why do they count?Because they have a good of their ownFor whom that good is good
    How much do they count?In Taylor's version, all equallyHow to decide when living requires taking lives
    What is lost?A lifeWhether anyone loses anything

    Biocentrism is the view that every living being, sentient or not, deserves direct moral consideration. Its best-known academic version is Paul Taylor's (1981, 1986): each organism, whether animal, plant or microorganism, would be a "teleological center of life", with a good of its own, and all would have the same inherent worth. The sentience criterion says that the beings who count are those able to have good or bad experiences.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Value for whom?

    Something being good or bad for someone requires that there be someone. A plant grows when watered and wilts when not, but it experiences none of these states, not even as neutral. The philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha describes these organisms as living bodies that no one inhabits (Questão de Ética, vol. VI, ch. 17). Current evidence indicates that plants aren't conscious (see A4).

    2. "The plant wants water" is a metaphor.

    We say a plant "likes" sun, just as we say a car "wants" gasoline or the sea is "angry". Concluding from this that the plant has a good of its own, in the moral sense, is sliding from the figurative to the literal meaning. Cunha records this criticism and also attributes it to Peter Singer. Critics within environmental ethics itself make the same point: saying an organism has a "biological good" is a description, not a reason to give it moral weight; HIV also flourishes under certain conditions (Brennan and Lo, SEP).

    3. The origin of a capacity doesn't set its value.

    Pain may well have evolved because it helped survival. But evolution explains why something exists, not what has value (on the leap from "is" to "ought", see Hume's law). And when we look at ourselves, the relation runs the other way: we seek food and company for what they let us live through, not to keep cells working. If this also keeps us alive, that's an added effect (Questão de Ética, vol. VI, ch. 16).

    4. Three tests.

    Cunha proposes three situations (vol. VI, ch. 19):

    • The irreversible coma. Between dying now and entering a coma with no experience at all, from which one never wakes, almost no one sees any advantage in the coma for themselves. The reasons to prefer it, such as relatives' wishes, are preferences of sentient beings.
    • The insurer. Someone pays for decades for an "afterlife" and discovers it will be just their body kept alive, with no experience at all. Anyone would feel cheated, because "life", in that question, always meant going on feeling.
    • Sentience without life. If the body were gradually replaced by a non-biological one, keeping consciousness, the person would no longer be a living being in the biological sense. Even so, their existence would still be worth something.

    In all three cases, what we value is experience, and life shows up as a means to it.

    5. Being necessary isn't having value in itself.

    The reverse is also said: since one must be alive to feel, life would have value in itself. But that only shows that life is an indispensable means to sentience, and a means doesn't gain value of its own by being indispensable (Questão de Ética, vol. VI, ch. 16).

    6. Including more isn't being fairer.

    What makes the sentience criterion better than the anthropocentric one isn't the number included: it's that it includes those who can be harmed and were left out. A criterion that also included rocks, tables and clouds would be "more inclusive", and no better for it. It would have to be shown that the beings added can be harmed. And biocentrism isn't even broader in every sense: if sentient non-organic beings ever exist, digital ones for example, it would leave them out, although they could suffer (Questão de Ética, vol. VI, ch. 15).

    7. Biocentrism doesn't say how to choose between lives.

    Living always costs lives: eating, walking on grass or fighting an infection kills organisms. If all are worth the same, the theory doesn't help decide. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy points to this as the central challenge for such views: Taylor proposed principles for resolving conflicts, but they need a justification that doesn't come from the beings' moral status, since that status was declared equal. Robin Attfield, also a biocentrist, prefers a hierarchical version, in which some beings, such as persons, are worth more. But to order the hierarchy one has to appeal to capacities such as feeling and thinking, and that gives back to sentience the role biocentrism wanted to take from it.

    8. In practice, the equality of all life lowers those who feel.

    If the cow is worth the same as the grass, causing her suffering weighs little more than mowing a lawn. And even by biocentrism's own count, eating animals takes more lives: about 36% of the calories from the world's crops go to feed, and only about 12% of those calories reach people as meat, milk and eggs (Cassidy et al., 2013; see A4 and F8).

    9. Having value isn't having priority.

    Even if non-sentient life had some value in itself, sentient beings would have priority in conflicts: only they can suffer, and only for them does death take away good experiences. Environmentalists already accept this when those affected are human (Questão de Ética, vol. VI, ch. 14; see N15).

    10. Non-sentient life still matters a great deal.

    The sentience criterion doesn't say forests, plants or rivers don't matter. It says they matter for what they mean to those who feel: food, shelter, air, beauty. That instrumental value is enormous and justifies protecting them, without putting a blade of grass above an animal who suffers.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: a good of its own doesn't require experience

    The most serious version doesn't say the plant feels. Taylor, Goodpaster (1978) and other biocentrists don't claim it feels; they claim that having a good of its own is enough: an organism can be helped or harmed in its functioning, even without perceiving anything. So the question "for whom?" would have an answer: for the organism itself.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Functioning well isn't being well.

    A car also runs better with oil, and a knife cuts better when sharp. "Good for functioning" doesn't imply that anyone gains or loses anything.

    The counter-reply proves too much.

    If having conditions for functioning were enough, machines, viruses and perhaps ecosystems would have interests in the same sense, and the idea of harm would lose its content.

    Even if accepted, it settles nothing.

    One would still have to say how much that good weighs against an animal's suffering, and the reasons to prioritize those who feel would still stand (point 9).

Teleological bias and agency detection

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right to ask for humility before nature, to recall that life is the condition for everything that matters, and to reject a gaze that sees only usefulness in the living world. Forests, plants and ecosystems deserve protection, and respect for them is legitimate.

But

The mistake is giving the same weight to those who feel and those who don't. Taking life seriously means asking how it is lived, and only those who feel live their own lives.
