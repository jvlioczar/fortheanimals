N2

"Suffering and death are part of nature"

The answer in one sentence

being part of nature doesn't make something good or indifferent for the one who suffers. Diseases, parasites and death in childbirth are also part of human nature, and that was never a reason not to treat them. The absence of a culprit matters for judging agents, not for measuring the victims' harm.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

Disease is also part of nature, and we still treat it. No one leaves a child with an infection untreated because bacteria are natural. That no one is to blame for suffering doesn't make it any less bad for the one who suffers. The question is whether we can help without causing more harm.

For children

Catching a cold is part of life, right? Even so, when you're sick, someone looks after you, gives you medicine and a warm drink. No one says "leave it, it's natural". What's natural can hurt just the same. And when it hurts, we help if we can.

Ask a question back

"If a disease is natural, would you stop treating it?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"That's just how nature is. Some eat others, some get sick, others die. It's part of it."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1It only makes sense to speak of moral evil when there's a responsible agent.
  2. P2In nature, suffering is caused by processes without intention: predation, disease, climate.
  3. C1So natural suffering isn't a moral evil.
  4. C2And what isn't a moral evil gives us no reason to act.

P1 and P2 are acceptable if we're talking about blame: a hurricane is guilty of nothing. The problem lies in C2, which jumps from the absence of a culprit to the absence of a victim.

The argument is invalid: from "no one is to blame" it doesn't follow that "no one was harmed".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses two questions:

    QuestionDepends onExample
    Is anyone to blame?There being an agent who choseAn arsonist is to blame; lightning isn't
    Was anyone harmed?There being a victim who sufferedA forest burned by lightning has as many victims as one burned by an arsonist

    For the one who suffers, the origin of the suffering doesn't change what they feel.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Neutral for whom?

    Nature has no intentions, but animals have experiences. The suffering of an animal eaten alive by parasites isn't neutral for it.

    2. Natural isn't a criterion of good.

    In the essay Nature, John Stuart Mill observed that nature does, every day, things for which a human being would be imprisoned or hanged. Copying nature was never a good moral guide (C1).

    3. We already intervene in the natural all the time.

    Vaccines, antibiotics, glasses, anaesthesia, shelter from the cold: much of civilization consists of reducing natural suffering.

    4. The human test.

    Faced with an epidemic or an earthquake, no one says "it's part of nature, let's not help". The absence of a culprit was never a reason to deny help to humans. Denying it to animals for the same reason is using a criterion we apply only to them.

    5. There's no agent, but there's someone who can help.

    Ethics asks not only who caused something, but also what we, who can act, ought to do.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Mill, Nature (1874)Nature kills, tortures and destroys all the time; imitating it isn't a moral criterionPublished in Three Essays on Religion
    Animal Ethics, "Why wild animal suffering matters"Suffering matters to the one who suffers, whatever its cause
    *A Journey Through Animal Ethics*, vol. VIIDiscusses whether natural suffering is neutral and whether nature's indifference releases us from helpingChapters 13, 15 and 16
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: suffering has a function

    The most serious version says pain is useful: it teaches animals to avoid danger, and natural selection depends on it. Ending natural suffering would destroy something that gives meaning to animals' lives.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Function isn't wellbeing.

    Pain serves the survival of genes, not the good of the individual. For the young animal that dies on its first day, it teaches nothing.

    We accept relieving pain that has a function.

    The pain of childbirth and of a fracture have functions, and we still use anaesthesia.

    No one proposes ending all pain at once.

    The serious proposal is to reduce avoidable suffering with research and caution (N6, N8).

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Appeal to nature
From the natural it concludes the acceptable
Non sequitur
From "no one is to blame" it concludes "no one was harmed"

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Confusing blame and harm
Measures the victim's harm by the cause's intention
Double standard
Accepts treating the natural in humans, but not in animals

Biases

Appeal to nature

The natural seems good or inevitable

Linked to C1 and C6

Omission bias

Not acting seems less serious than acting

Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991

Intention bias

Harm only seems serious when someone meant to cause it

Tomasik, "Intention-based moral reactions distort intuitions about wild animals"

Just-world hypothesis

If suffering is part of the order of things, it seems no one is wronged by it

Lerner and Miller, 1978

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that nature isn't a moral agent and that it makes no sense to blame lions or viruses. It's also right that pain has important biological functions and that tampering with it requires great care.

But

But the question isn't whether nature is to blame. It's whether we, who can act, have reason to help those who suffer. And the natural origin of suffering has never, in any other context, been a reason not to help.
