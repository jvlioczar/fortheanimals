N2

"Suffering and death are part of nature"

The answer in one sentence

being part of nature doesn't make something good or indifferent for the one who suffers. Diseases, parasites, famines, natural disasters and death in childbirth also strike human beings through natural causes, and that was never a reason to leave the victims without help. The absence of a culprit matters for judging agents, not for measuring the victims' harm.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Reply

Disease is also part of nature, and we still treat it. You wouldn't leave a child with an infection untreated because bacteria are natural, or refuse to help victims of famine or earthquakes because droughts and earthquakes are natural. That no one is to blame for suffering doesn't make it any less bad for the one who suffers. So the question is whether we can help without causing more harm.

For children

Catching a cold is part of life, right? Even so, when you're sick, someone looks after you, gives you medicine and a warm drink. No one says "leave it, it's natural". What's natural can hurt just the same. And when it hurts, we help if we can.

Ask a question back

"If a disease is natural, would you stop treating it?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"That's just how nature is. Some eat others, some get sick, others die. It's part of it."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1It only makes sense to speak of moral evil when there's a responsible agent.
  2. P2In nature, suffering is caused by processes without intention: predation, disease, climate.
  3. C1So natural suffering isn't a moral evil.
  4. C2And what isn't a moral evil gives us no reason to act.

P1 and P2 are acceptable if we're talking about blame: a hurricane is guilty of nothing. The problem lies in C2, which jumps from the absence of a culprit to the absence of a victim.

The argument is invalid: from "no one is to blame" it doesn't follow that "no one was harmed".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses two questions:

    QuestionDepends onExample
    Is anyone to blame?There being an agent who choseAn arsonist is to blame; lightning isn't
    Was anyone harmed?There being a victim who sufferedA forest burned by lightning has as many victims as one burned by an arsonist

    For the one who suffers, the origin of the suffering doesn't change what they feel.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Neutral for whom?

    Nature has no intentions, but animals have experiences. The suffering of an animal eaten alive by parasites isn't neutral for it.

    2. Natural isn't a criterion of good.

    In the essay Nature, John Stuart Mill observed that nature does, every day, things for which a human being would be imprisoned or hanged. Copying nature was never a good moral guide (C1).

    3. We already intervene in the natural all the time.

    Vaccines, antibiotics, glasses, anaesthesia, shelter from the cold: much of civilization consists of reducing natural suffering.

    4. The human test.

    We don't use this argument for human beings. Diseases, famines caused by droughts and pests, earthquakes, floods and hurricanes are also "part of nature", and we still vaccinate, treat, send food and help the victims. Faced with an epidemic or an earthquake, no one says "it's part of nature, let's not help". The absence of a culprit was never a reason to deny help to humans. Denying it to animals for the same reason is using a criterion we apply only to them.

    5. There's no agent, but there's someone who can help.

    Ethics asks not only who caused something, but also what we, who can act, ought to do.

    6. Value and morality are different questions.

    Asking whether something is good or bad isn't the same as asking who is responsible for it. We don't conclude that suffering is bad because someone caused it; it's the other way round: we shouldn't cause it because it's bad. A process with no moral agent produces results that are still good or bad for those who live them. The "human touch" changes nothing about the pain (Cunha, 2022, p. 100).

    7. Three thought experiments.

    Imagine a factory farm that appeared on its own, with no human behind it: would the reasons to end it be any weaker? Imagine that the plastic that kills turtles formed naturally in the ocean: would it stop being a problem? Finally, imagine a human deliberately designing a system in which each mother produces thousands of young so that almost all of them starve, in which many are eaten alive or consumed by parasites, and in which all of this is hidden behind a beautiful landscape. We'd call that designer a monster. That is the description of nature for most animals (Cunha, 2022, pp. 106–107).

    8. "It's part of life" isn't "it should be part of life".

    The environmental philosopher J. Baird Callicott (1980) wrote that refusing suffering and death is refusing the conditions of life. No one denies they are part of life; the question is whether they should remain so when they can be avoided. We also know pain is part of our lives, and we still take painkillers. And "it has always been this way" would prove too much: sexual violence has always existed too (Cunha, 2022, p. 109).

    9. "Nature never makes mistakes" assumes a designer.

    It only makes sense to say a process gets things right or wrong if it has goals. Evolutionary biology doesn't attribute goals to natural selection. And even if there were someone behind it, we'd have to show that this someone knows everything and wants what's good for animals, which the daily carnage makes hard to maintain. Nor does it help to say that "everything leads to the best, we just can't see it": by the same reasoning, we shouldn't treat human diseases either (Cunha, 2022, pp. 109–110).

    10. Those who find suffering useful prefer, when they can, the benefit without it.

    The environmental philosopher Holmes Rolston III (1992) argues that suffering has value for the animal itself, for other animals and for the species. But most animals in nature suffer from birth to death, with no benefit at all. And when suffering is useful, we prefer to reach the same good without it, as we do when we operate under anesthesia (Cunha, 2022, pp. 103–104).

    11. You can't get an ought from an is.

    From the fact that suffering is natural, the argument concludes that we should not reduce it. But a fact, by itself, doesn't say what is right: reaching a moral conclusion requires a moral premise, and that premise is what needs defending. This leap is Hume's law; see also Describing is not evaluating, in Start here.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: suffering has a function

    The most serious version says pain is useful: it teaches animals to avoid danger, and natural selection depends on it. Ending natural suffering would destroy something that gives meaning to animals' lives.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Function isn't wellbeing.

    Pain serves the survival of genes, not the good of the individual. For the young animal that dies on its first day, it teaches nothing.

    We accept relieving pain that has a function.

    The pain of childbirth and of a fracture have functions, and we still use anaesthesia.

    No one proposes ending all pain at once.

    The serious proposal is to reduce avoidable suffering with research and caution (N9, N10).

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that nature isn't a moral agent and that it makes no sense to blame lions or viruses. It's also right that pain has important biological functions and that tampering with it requires great care.

But

But the question isn't whether nature is to blame. It's whether we, who can act, have reason to help those who suffer. And the natural origin of suffering has never, in any other context, been a reason not to help.
