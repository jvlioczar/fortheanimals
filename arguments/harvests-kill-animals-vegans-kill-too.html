H2

"Harvests kill animals; vegans kill too"

The answer in one sentence

it's true that agriculture kills animals, such as rodents, insects and birds, during planting and harvest. But livestock farming uses far more cropland, because the animals eat feed, as well as using enormous pastures. About 80% of the world's farmland serves the production of meat, dairy and farmed fish. Eating plants directly tends to cause fewer harvest deaths than eating animals fed on plants.

Reply

It's true: agriculture kills animals. But livestock farming needs much more agriculture, because the animals eat feed, and it occupies immense pastures: about 80% of the world's farmland serves the production of meat, dairy and farmed fish. By eating plants directly, you use less land and tend to cause fewer harvest deaths. The question isn't which diet is perfect, but which causes fewer deaths. And that count doesn't even reach the heart of the matter: the animals raised and slaughtered also suffer and die, and what would justify doing that to them?

For children

When machines harvest the fields, some little animals can die. That's sad. But farm animals eat a lot of crops. So to make meat, even more has to be planted, and more little animals die in the harvest. Eating the plants directly means fewer little animals die.

Ask a question back

"If harvests kill animals, doesn't eating animals fed on harvests kill even more?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"How many mice died for your salad? Vegans have blood on their hands too."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Agriculture kills animals during soil preparation, planting, pesticide application and harvest.
  2. P2Vegans eat agricultural products.
  3. P3So vegans also cause animals' deaths.
  4. CSo the vegan diet isn't morally superior.

P1, P2 and P3 are true. The problem lies in the conclusion: it ignores that eating animals requires even more agriculture, and therefore even more harvest deaths, on top of the deaths of the farmed animals themselves.

The argument is valid to show that no diet is entirely innocent. Its soundness fails when it concludes the diets are equivalent.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument compares the vegan diet with a cost-free diet, not with the real diet with meat:

    DietHarvest deathsDeaths of farmed animals
    VeganFrom crops for human foodNone
    With meat from feed-fed animalsFrom crops for human food and from crops for feed, which is largerBillions of animals a year
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Livestock farming uses most of the farmland.

    According to Our World in Data, about 80% of the world's farmland, between pasture and feed crops, serves the production of meat, dairy and farmed fish, which provide only 17% of calories.

    2. The animals eat feed.

    To get one calorie of meat, the animal must be given several calories of feed. This multiplies the area planted and, with it, tends to multiply harvest deaths. Estimates of how many animals die per hectare are very uncertain, but the difference in area is large enough to matter.

    3. A vegan diet would greatly reduce farmland.

    According to Poore and Nemecek, a global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%.

    4. There are also the deaths of the farmed animals themselves.

    On top of harvest deaths, livestock farming directly kills tens of billions of land animals a year.

    5. Some deaths in crop farming are intentional, and that distinction doesn't settle things on its own.

    Many harvest deaths are side effects, while in slaughter death is the goal (F5). But pesticides and rodenticides kill animals on purpose. The distinction between accidental and intentional matters, but it isn't enough: the decisive question is whether the harm is necessary.

    6. The most detailed philosophical analysis points the same way.

    The philosopher Joshua Jarvis-Campbell tested, in a 2026 paper, whether the principle of unnecessary harm also condemns industrial crop farming. He grants that the harm is real and possibly extensive, and that the easy defences ("they'd die soon anyway", "they wouldn't exist without the crops") don't hold. But he argues that this harm is plausibly necessary in the short term, because cheap food for many people currently depends on agriculture at scale. The additional harm of animal farming, by contrast, isn't necessary: cheap food can be had without it, and cheap animal farming depends on the same crops. Whether better-off people have a duty to buy from less harmful sources, the paper leaves open (H3).

    7. Even in this dispute, everyone condemns intensive farming.

    Steven Davis and George Schedler defend a system with some pasture, Gaverick Matheny replies that crops for direct human consumption kill fewer animals, and the calculation is still debated. But, as the political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane notes, whatever model is proposed, everyone agrees that the current system, based on intensive farming, should end (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 41–42). The harvest argument, even in its best version, doesn't defend the meat people buy today.

    8. A debate within animal ethics: what about the land left over?

    The philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha, in Razões para ajudar (Reasons to Help, 2022, pp. 272–277), examines a more sophisticated version of the objection. It grants that livestock farming uses more cropland, but says that this is exactly why veganism would free up a lot of land, and that a common environmentalist slogan is to use that land to "give it back to nature". If the vegetation that returns hosts populations of animals who have many young, almost all of whom die soon after birth, the result could be more suffering than exploitation itself causes. Cunha doesn't conclude that exploitation should be supported. He replies that the link isn't necessary: in a world where animals were taken into account, freed land wouldn't be turned into natural areas, and the smaller cropped area would then be an advantage of veganism. And he adds that veganism's greatest value would lie in the ideal it spreads, provided it is defended out of consideration for animals; that is why, he claims, a veganism spread only for environmental reasons would risk outcomes worse even than exploitation (p. 277).

    It's a contested thesis, and this guide records it without endorsing it. It clashes with other chapters (H21, H1) and with climate science, which see the return of native vegetation on freed land as a gain, for example as a carbon sink (Hayek et al., 2021). It depends on open premises, such as that most wild animals' lives are net negative, which Groff and Ng (2019) and Browning and Veit (2023) dispute. And it starts from an ethics that judges exploitation by its results; animal rights advocates such as Tom Regan and Gary Francione hold that exploiting is wrong in itself, a view Cunha considers untenable (p. 273). For this chapter, the point that remains is more modest: no one in this debate concludes that eating animals is the answer.

    9. The calculation is uncertain, but it points to plants.

    Brian Tomasik, who studies the effect of crops on wild animals, notes that a gram of animal protein usually requires many times more cultivated grain than a gram of plant protein, although not even vegans avoid every death in the field. The effect of crops on insects is very uncertain; in an attempt to compare foods, which he himself calls crude, beans and nuts seem to affect wild animals less than grains. The practical conclusion doesn't change: eating plants directly requires less cropland and therefore fewer deaths in the field.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: pasture-raised meat may kill less

    The most serious version, defended by the philosopher Steven Davis in 2003 and taken up by George Schedler in 2005, says cattle raised only on pasture, without feed, would cause fewer wild animal deaths per calorie than crops, because pastures aren't harvested.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The numbers were disputed.

    Matheny showed that Davis compared deaths per hectare, not per calorie produced. Per calorie, crops for direct human consumption come out better.

    Pasture requires far more land.

    Pasture-raised meat uses an enormous area, often won through deforestation (H1).

    Almost no one eats only pasture-raised meat.

    Most meat comes from animals fed on feed.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument recognizes that the death of animals matters, and that's the central point of veganism. If it matters, the consistent choice is the one that causes fewer deaths. The moral question returns: if eating plants kills fewer, why choose what kills more?

Contradictions

Says they care about the mice in the harvest.

But eats animals raised and slaughtered.

Ignores the cropland for feed.

Which is larger than that for human food.

Wouldn't reduce their own contribution.

Even knowing that eating plants would cause fewer deaths.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The argument gets one important thing right: agriculture kills animals, and no diet is entirely innocent. Vegans should recognize this and support farming practices that reduce those deaths. The philosophical debate on the subject is serious. The easy defences, such as "those animals would die soon anyway" or "they wouldn't exist without the crops", don't hold. And cheap measures to reduce the deaths, such as nets and barriers around fields, may be a duty where they're feasible.

But

But comparing real diets, eating plants directly uses less land and causes fewer deaths. If harvest deaths matter, they're one more reason to stop eating animals.
