N27

"Talking about wild animals pushes people away from veganism"

The answer in one sentence

the concern is legitimate, but it's a factual claim, and the facts haven't been measured yet. No study shows that talking about wild animal suffering drives people away from veganism, and the little research that exists shows that most people already care about these animals. A communication strategy can decide where to start and whom to talk to about what. It can't decide that immense suffering be postponed indefinitely.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

That's a prediction, and no one has measured whether it's true. The research that exists shows that most people already say they care about wild animals that suffer, whatever the cause. Choosing where to start the conversation makes sense; never talking about where most of the suffering is does not. And supporting this help asks less of each person than being vegan does.

For children

Sometimes we're afraid to say something new because others might think it's strange. But many things that seemed strange became normal once people understood them. We can explain calmly, starting with the easiest part.

Ask a question back

"What evidence shows that talking about this pushes people away? And if we never talk about it, when will this suffering get onto the agenda?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"People already think veganism is weird. If you start talking about helping wild animals, no one will take it seriously."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1The public sees nature as good and untouchable, and already resists veganism.
  2. P2If it finds out that taking animals seriously leads to intervening in nature, it will reject veganism too.
  3. P3Ending exploitation is the most urgent and most achievable goal today.
  4. CSo the movement should avoid talking about wild animals, at least for now.

P1 has support: many people idealize nature. P3 is debatable but defensible. The weak point is P2, an empirical prediction presented as if it were certain. Cunha himself acknowledges that there are no statistical data on this effect (Cunha, 2022, p. 256).

The argument is valid, but P2 has no empirical basis, and the conclusion confuses "how and when to talk" with "not talking".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument depends on a prediction about the public. What is known today:

    QuestionWhat the data showSource
    Does the public care about wild animals?Yes, mostly, including about suffering from natural causesSleegers et al., 2025
    Does it support helping?Divided in general; high support for giving water, food, vaccinating and treating; low for genetic modificationSleegers et al., 2025
    Does framing change support?Yes, in different directions depending on the questionSchulzetenberg, 2017
    Does talking about it push people away from veganism?Not measuredNo study found

    The argument's conclusion depends on the last row, which is empty.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The prediction hasn't been tested.

    We found no study measuring whether talking about wild animal suffering reduces uptake of veganism or sympathy for animals. Cunha admits the lack of data, so the strength of the argument depends on a probability no one knows (Cunha, 2022, p. 256).

    2. The public already cares more than one might think.

    While developing a scale of attitudes toward wild animal welfare, Rethink Priorities researchers surveyed 2,866 people in four studies. In a study by the same team with 830 people in the United Kingdom, summarized by Faunalytics in 2024, 90% agreed that they care about wild animals in pain, whether from human or natural causes. Support for intervening was more divided, but high for measures such as providing water and food, vaccinating and treating sick animals (Sleegers et al., 2025).

    3. What changes support is the framing.

    In a study with 1,371 people in the United States, short texts in favour of acting lowered support for a general question about "reducing suffering in nature", but raised support for specific proposals (Schulzetenberg, 2017). This suggests that framing matters, not that the topic should be avoided.

    4. Supporting this help asks little of each person.

    Professionals would carry out the research and interventions. The public only needs to support the idea, which is less than changing one's diet, clothes and habits (Cunha, 2022, p. 257; N22).

    5. Prejudices challenged early lose force sooner.

    Waiting decades to start the discussion only delays the moment it stops seeming absurd. And people tend to reconsider their reverence for the natural when they meet arguments, not when they never hear of the subject (Cunha, 2022, p. 256).

    6. Silence has a cost too.

    Even if exploitation declined, the suffering that comes from natural processes would remain untouched as long as no one argued for reducing it. That cost isn't a risk: it's certain (Cunha, 2022, p. 256).

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: credibility is a limited resource

    The most serious version is strategic. Social movements have limited credibility, and opponents use a group's strangest ideas to discredit all the others. Someone laughing at "saving mosquitoes" never gets to hear the argument against animal farming (L4). Research on activism shows that some tactics provoke backlash. Under uncertainty, the prudent course would be to focus the message on what has the most support.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's an argument about how to talk, not about what's true.

    It can justify care: not starting with the most controversial points, choosing the audience, and beginning with examples already accepted, such as rescuing animals from fires or vaccinating against rabies. Cunha himself advises against introducing the topic to newcomers by starting with the proposal not to expand natural areas (Cunha, 2022, p. 241).

    The effects of "radical" ideas are mixed.

    Research on the radical flank effect finds both harms and gains for moderate groups (K8). There's no basis for assuming the effect here would be purely negative.

    The burden of proof lies with whoever predicts harm.

    Whoever says a sound argument will push people away needs to show it, especially when the alternative is to set aside most animal suffering.

    The debate on environmental arguments remains open.

    Cunha goes further and says there are studies showing that environmental arguments for veganism make the public more resistant to helping wild animals, but he doesn't say which (Cunha, 2022, p. 257). The only study we found on the topic, by Animal Charity Evaluators, pointed in the opposite direction on one measure, and its authors urged caution. Cunha's thesis that veganism promoted for environmental reasons might, by expanding natural areas, be worse for animals than exploitation is also contested: environmentalists and conservationists reject it, and part of the field itself, such as Wild Animal Initiative, prefers to focus on research rather than argue against expanding nature.

Reasoning errors

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Prediction without evidence
Presents as certain an effect that has never been measured

Logical

Is the form valid?

False dilemma
Either you talk about everything at once, or never
Confusing levels
Treats a question of strategy as if it settled a moral question

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Invisible cost
Counts the risk of speaking and ignores the certain cost of silence

Biases

False consensus effect

Assumes the public will react the way the person does

Ross, Greene and House, 1977

Ambiguity effect

An unknown risk of speaking weighs more than the known cost of silence

Ellsberg, 1961

Omission bias

Not speaking seems safer than speaking, even with a worse outcome

Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991

Contradictions

Advocates veganism, which was also once considered absurd.

And keeps advocating it even though it puts off part of the public.

Approves of rescuing wild animals from fires and floods.

Which is already helping wild animals, and is usually well received.

Criticizes those who say "it's not the right time" for other causes.

But uses the same argument here.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that framing matters, that new topics can cause puzzlement and that a movement needs to think about the effect of what it says. It's also right that data are lacking, and that it would be good to measure how the public reacts.

But

But a lack of data cuts both ways: it doesn't license the claim that speaking pushes people away. The prudent path is to speak carefully, start with what's already accepted and research the effect, not to hide the topic.
