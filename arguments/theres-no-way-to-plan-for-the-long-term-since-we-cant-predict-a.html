N19

"There's no way to plan for the long term, since we can't predict a distant future"

The answer in one sentence

predicting isn't the only way to act. Much of what is done with the long term in mind doesn't depend on getting a scenario right: it depends on changing what will hold in any scenario. Widening who is taken into account morally, building institutions that are hard to reverse and producing knowledge all pay off across a wide range of possible futures. The uncertainty is real and should change which actions you choose, not switch off the concern. And it cuts both ways: not knowing what's coming is no licence to assume it will be fine.

Note: denying that future beings matter is N17; assuming the future improves on its own is N20

Short answer

The objection works against plans that depend on getting one scenario right. It doesn't work against actions that pay off in almost all of them. Widening who counts morally, building institutions that resist backsliding and producing knowledge about the problem require guessing nothing. And the uncertainty is symmetrical: if it rules out saying the future will be bad, it also rules out assuming it will be good — and whoever assumes that is already making a long-term prediction.

Question back: "Do you also think there's no point in tackling climate change, since nobody knows what the climate will be like in 2200?"

For children

Nobody knows what day it will rain next year. We still build houses with roofs. You don't have to guess the right day: you just have to know that rain comes. The future is like that. We can't know everything that will happen, but we can leave the world ready to treat better whoever arrives.

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Nobody has the faintest idea what the world will be like in two hundred years. Planning for that is a waste of time."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Acting rationally toward an end requires predicting, at least roughly, the effect of your actions.
  2. P2We have no way of predicting the distant future.
  3. CSo there are no rational long-term strategies.

P2 is largely true for specific, distant scenarios. The problem is P1, which conflates predicting scenarios with predicting effects, and assumes every action needs a dated target.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1: there are actions whose expected value is positive in almost every scenario, precisely because they don't depend on which one occurs.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The objection treats "long-term strategy" as if it were one thing. The Senciência summary on the subject separates two families, and that separation settles much of the discussion.

    Type of strategyHow it worksVulnerability to uncertainty
    BroadSeeks good outcomes across a wide diversity of scenarios: widening moral consideration, including to aquatic animals, invertebrates and future forms of sentience; institutional changes that make regressions harder; spreading concern for future suffering; increasing knowledgeLow: it doesn't depend on which scenario occurs. The effect tends to be smaller than that of a well-aimed action
    TargetedSeeks impact in a specific scenario: preventing new forms of exploitation, developing non-animal substitutes, building welfare biology for wild animals, regulating space exploration, securing rights for non-biological sentient entitiesHigh: a large effect if the scenario occurs, possibly none if it doesn't

    The objection lands squarely against the second column and misses the first.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It confuses predicting the scenario with predicting the effect.

    We don't know what technologies will exist in 2200. We do know that, if sentient beings exist, it will matter whether the people of that time take them into account. Spreading moral consideration is a bet on an effect, not on a scenario.

    2. We already do this elsewhere, without complaint.

    Basic education, constitutions, vaccination, seed banks, environmental treaties and pension saving are all long-term investments under deep uncertainty. Nobody calls them irrational for not knowing what the world will be in 2150.

    3. Uncertainty cuts both ways.

    If unpredictability rules out claiming that there will be suffering on a very large scale, it also rules out claiming there won't be. Faced with a bad and irreversible possibility, the usual response isn't to ignore it: it's to take proportionate precautions (N20).

    4. Uncertainty calls for robust actions, not inaction.

    The right conclusion from "I don't know which scenario is coming" is to prefer what works across many, to increase available knowledge and to avoid what can't be undone. None of that is stopping.

    5. The objection usually arrives with a prediction built in.

    Whoever says the future can't be planned for normally acts as though they knew the future will look after itself. That is a long-term prediction, only an unexamined one.

    6. Ignoring the long term is also a choice with long-term effects.

    Today's decisions about agricultural expansion, aquaculture and technology are already shaping how many beings will exist and under what conditions. There is no option of not influencing.

    7. Epistemic humility is part of the strategy, not an argument against it.

    The Senciência summary itself recommends acknowledging complexity, being suspicious of one's own biases and preferring goals acceptable from varied perspectives, since highly controversial approaches drive away people with other priorities. Taking uncertainty seriously leads to those recommendations, not to abandonment.

  4. d

    The best reply: the evidence-base problem

    The serious version doesn't say the future doesn't matter. It says that the further out the target, the weaker the evidence about any action's effect, and that expected-value calculations with very large numbers and very small probabilities lead to absurd conclusions. Better to concentrate effort where returns can be measured.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It argues for caution in method, not for abandoning the goal.

    The natural conclusion is to prefer broad, measurable strategies, which is exactly what is being proposed.

    The tiny-probability problem doesn't apply to everything.

    Widening the moral circle isn't a bet at vanishing odds: it is a change whose effects are already observable on a human scale, across centuries.

    Many long-term actions pay off immediately.

    Reducing animal suffering today and spreading consideration for animals are the same thing done twice.

    The reply doesn't spare anyone the choice.

    While the quality of the evidence is debated, decisions with lasting effects keep being made by people who aren't in that debate.

Contradictions

It takes out pensions, insurance and wills.

Long-term decisions under uncertainty, with no complaint.

It supports climate targets for 2100.

Which depend on far less certain scenarios than the ones discussed here.

It thinks educating children is worthwhile.

Though the effect shows up decades later, and never predictably.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets right, and quite strongly, that the uncertainty is deep and that detailed predictions about the distant future almost always fail. It gets right that the long term can be used as an excuse to ignore those suffering now, and that calculations with gigantic numbers and minuscule probabilities produce conclusions nobody should trust. Humility here is obligatory.

But

What doesn't follow is stopping. Uncertainty selects the type of action — broad rather than targeted, reversible rather than final, informed rather than wagered — and doesn't excuse anyone from acting. Whoever concludes "then it makes no difference" has made, without noticing, the strongest prediction of all: that nothing we do now will matter later.
