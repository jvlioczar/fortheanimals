L7

"Veganism is a cult, a religion"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

labeling a position a "cult" is a way of not assessing it. Veganism rests on public arguments that anyone can examine: animals feel, we can live without killing them, and causing unnecessary suffering is wrong. A religion asks for faith; veganism asks us to look at the facts and at the consistency of our own values.

Short answer

Calling it a cult is a way of not discussing it. A religion asks for faith; veganism rests on claims that can be checked: animals feel, well-planned vegan diets are adequate, and causing unnecessary suffering is wrong, which almost everyone already accepts. Defending a position with conviction doesn't make it religious. If it did, any cause defended with passion would be a cult.

For children

A religion asks people to believe in things you can't see. Veganism is different: it talks about things you can check, like "animals feel pain" and "you can live well without eating meat". Anyone can think about this and reach their own conclusion.

Ask a question back

"Which part of the argument depends on faith rather than facts and reasons?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"You preach like fanatics. That's indoctrination."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Some vegans speak of veganism with fervor, rituals and group identity similar to religious ones.
  2. P2Religious beliefs can't be imposed on those who don't share them.
  3. P3Veganism is a belief of that kind.
  4. CSo vegans shouldn't try to persuade others.

P1 may be true in some cases. The problem lies in P3: veganism doesn't depend on faith, but on facts and arguments everyone can assess.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument labels instead of assessing:

    Feature of a religionDoes veganism have it?
    Belief in entities or revealed truthsNo
    Sacred authorityNo
    Verifiable claimsYes: animal sentience, nutritional adequacy
    A widely accepted moral principleYes: don't cause unnecessary suffering
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Labeling isn't refuting.

    Calling a position a cult doesn't show it's wrong.

    2. Veganism rests on public arguments.

    Animal sentience (A1), nutritional adequacy (G2) and the principle of not causing unnecessary suffering can be assessed by anyone.

    3. Conviction isn't fanaticism.

    Firmly defending a moral position is common in many causes.

    4. Eating meat also involves beliefs.

    They're simply invisible because they're dominant (E4).

    5. Vegans are diverse.

    There are religious vegans and atheist vegans, from many cultures and political positions.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness (2024)Strong scientific support for consciousness in mammals and birdsShows the basis of veganism is verifiable
    Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, 2016Well-planned vegan diets are adequateAn empirical basis, not a basis in faith
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the group behavior is religious

    The most serious version grants that veganism's reasons are public, but says the social behavior of many vegans resembles that of religious groups: strong identity, their own language, exclusion of those who don't follow.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Every group has social dynamics.

    Sports fans, parties and professions do too.

    Group behavior doesn't change the reasons.

    The question is still about the animals.

    It's a collective ad hominem.

    It judges the idea by its followers.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The cult label avoids the question. But the question doesn't require faith: animals feel, and we can live without killing them. What justifies carrying on?

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that some vegans speak with fervor and that groups with a strong identity can become closed. It's also true that no one likes to feel indoctrinated, and that respectful communication works better.

But

But veganism doesn't ask for faith. It asks us to look at facts and at the consistency of our own values, something anyone can do.
