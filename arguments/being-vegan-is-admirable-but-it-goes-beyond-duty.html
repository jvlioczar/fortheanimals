E17

"Being vegan is admirable, but it goes beyond duty"

The answer in one sentence

the sentence uses a real category in ethics, the supererogatory: what is good to do but not required, such as donating a kidney to a stranger or risking one's life to save someone. That category, however, usually applies to acts of help that cost a great deal to those who perform them. Not eating animals is, above all, no longer taking part in a serious harm paid for out of taste and convenience, and not causing serious harm without need is the most basic kind of duty. The cost of changing varies, and where it is truly high the very definition of veganism, "as far as is possible and practicable", already takes it into account. Admiring something doesn't make it optional.

Reply

I'm glad you find it admirable. And there really is a category of acts that go beyond duty, like donating a kidney to a stranger. But it's for people who help someone at great cost to themselves. Not eating animals is no longer paying for them to be confined and killed, when one can live well without it. Not causing serious harm for the sake of taste isn't heroism; it's the minimum we ask in any other case. And where changing really costs a lot, the definition of veganism already says "as far as is possible and practicable".

For children

Imagine two classmates. One shares their lunch with someone who forgot theirs: that's really nice, but nobody has to do it. The other takes a younger kid's lunch every day, and one day decides to stop. Stopping isn't being a hero: it's just no longer doing harm. It's similar with animals: no longer harming them isn't an extra, it's the basics.

Ask a question back

"If no longer causing serious harm were optional, what other harms would be optional too?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"I think what you do is lovely, really. But that's for saints; no one is obliged to do it."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1There are morally good acts that go beyond duty: doing them is admirable, but not doing them isn't wrong.
  2. P2An act is of this kind when it brings about some good but requires of the person a sacrifice that morality can't demand of everyone.
  3. P3Giving up animal products requires changing habits, giving up pleasures and facing social costs, and the effect of a single person is small.
  4. CSo veganism is admirable, but not obligatory.

P1 has been accepted by most philosophers since J. O. Urmson's article "Saints and Heroes" (1958), although some, especially in the utilitarian and Kantian traditions, deny that the category exists. P2 describes the typical cases well. The problem lies in P3 and in the step to C: P3 treats as a heroic sacrifice what, in most cases, is no longer taking part in a harm, and the criterion in P2 was designed for acts of help, not for ceasing to harm.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, which exaggerates the cost and swaps "no longer causing harm" for "doing extra good", and because P2 doesn't apply to duties not to cause harm.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The philosopher Nathan Nobis proposes replacing the ambiguous phrase "morally right" with more precise categories (Animals & Ethics 101, 2016, pp. 7–9):

    CategoryWhat it meansExample
    ImpermissibleIt's wrong to do itPushing someone into the pool to enjoy their fright
    ObligatoryIt's wrong not to do itPulling a drowning baby out of the bathtub, when it's easy and safe
    PermissibleIt's neither wrong to do nor wrong not to doChoosing between beans and lentils
    SupererogatoryIt's permissible, good and admirable, but not requiredGiving a large part of one's income to fight hunger; donating a kidney to a stranger

    The sentence puts veganism in the last row. Nobis notes that some people do this and observes that, if this classification is correct, the arguments concluding that veganism is obligatory are unsound (p. 9). The question, then, is whether it belongs in that row or in the first: whether it is help beyond duty or ceasing to do what is impermissible.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The supererogatory is, above all, costly help.

    Urmson's examples are the soldier who throws himself on a grenade to save his comrades and the doctor who goes to work in a plague-stricken city. The cases philosophy usually discusses are similar: charity, heroism, volunteering and, among omissions, giving up something that is one's own by right, as when someone forgives an offence (see the "Supererogation" entry in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy). In all of them, the person gives up something of their own, such as time, money, safety or a right, for the sake of another. None of them consists in ceasing to harm someone.

    2. Not causing serious harm without need is a duty, not an extra.

    There are negative duties, not to harm, and positive duties, to help. Even those who defend the category of the supererogatory usually treat duties not to harm as stricter. Nobis observes that respecting animals is, above all, leaving them alone: not hunting them as trophies or killing them for their fur or their flesh (p. 100). No one calls someone heroic for not kicking a dog for fun.

    3. The cost is lower than the sentence assumes, and where it is high it's already provided for.

    The 2016 position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, the largest association of dietitians in the United States, states that well-planned vegetarian and vegan diets are appropriate for all stages of life. For people with access to shops and basic foods, the change is an effort of habit, not a heroic sacrifice. Where the cost is truly high, because of extreme poverty, isolation or illness, the classic definition already limits the request to what is "possible and practicable" (D7 and E10).

    4. The small effect of one person doesn't make the harm optional.

    P3 adds to the cost the idea that a single person changes nothing. That is a different objection, discussed in K1 and K3. Even if it were true, it would show that each person's action is ineffective, not that it is a sacrifice beyond duty.

    5. Admiration comes from rarity, not from going beyond duty.

    In 1791, British abolitionists, many of them Quakers, launched a campaign for families to stop buying sugar produced by enslaved people. According to the historian Julie Holcomb, nearly half a million Britons joined; other sources give lower figures. At the time, those who gave up sugar were seen as exemplary. Today we would say that not funding slavery was the minimum. When a harm is normal, ceasing to take part in it looks heroic; that says something about custom, not about the nature of the duty.

    6. The boundary of the supererogatory is itself contested.

    Peter Singer, in "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972), argues that even donations that common sense considers generous beyond duty are obligatory, when one can prevent something very bad without sacrificing anything of comparable importance. Nobis cites this debate (p. 8). There's no need to accept Singer's position for this chapter's point, which asks for much less: not to help, but to stop harming.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: morality can't demand too much

    The most serious version is the demandingness objection. Almost everything we consume causes some harm to someone (L12); a morality that required avoiding all harm would be impossible to follow, and an impossible morality guides no one. Food is tied to family, culture and identity, and for some people the cost of changing is real: little money, little time, little choice, a history of eating disorders. So the line between the obligatory and the admirable should be drawn by cost, and for many people veganism falls on the admirable side.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The objection is legitimate, and it's already built in.

    "As far as is possible and practicable" is exactly an answer to demandingness. It shows there are exceptions and degrees, not that the whole rule is optional for those who have access to alternatives (E10).

    Social cost isn't heroic sacrifice.

    Explaining one's request at a dinner or learning new recipes is uncomfortable, and not comparable to risking one's life or donating an organ.

    The demandingness bar is stricter for not harming than for helping.

    We accept that someone doesn't give away half their income; we don't accept that someone keeps causing serious harm because stopping would be a hassle. No one would say that no longer buying products of slave labour is heroic just because they are cheaper.

    "Some harm" isn't "any harm".

    That it's impossible to avoid all harm doesn't make easily avoidable harm acceptable, as E10 already shows.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Praising someone who has stopped eating animals is kind, and the person giving the praise often already recognizes that there is a good reason to change (E14). But the question isn't whether veganism is lovely; it's whether it's right to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to. If the answer is no, ceasing to do it isn't heroism. It's what is asked of anyone faced with a harm they can avoid.

Biases

Omission bias

Continuing to buy feels like a neutral omission rather than taking part in the harm

Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991

Status quo bias

The current habit feels like the default, and changing feels like an extraordinary effort

Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988

Meat paradox

Classifying the change as heroic eases the tension between liking animals and eating them

Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010

Contradictions

Wouldn't accept that not mistreating a dog is "for saints".

And accepts it when the animals are pigs or chickens.

Recognizes that there is something admirable.

That is, that there is a moral reason; and treats that reason as if it bound no one.

Fulfils costlier duties without calling them heroic.

Paying taxes, caring for elderly parents and obeying traffic rules take more effort than changing lunch.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that the category of the supererogatory exists and matters: morality can't demand that everyone be a saint or a hero, and there are admirable acts no one has a duty to perform. It's also right that the cost of changing isn't the same for everyone, and that praise usually works better than condemnation. And, by admiring, the person already concedes that there is a moral reason at stake.

But

But the category is for those who do extra good, not for those who stop doing harm. No longer paying for the suffering and death of animals, when one can live well without it, is on the side of duty.
