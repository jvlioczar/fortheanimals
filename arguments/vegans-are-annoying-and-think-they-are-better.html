Home / L. Attacks on veganism and vegans / Part IND

L1

"Vegans are annoying and think they are better"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's an attack on the person, not the argument. Even if some vegans are annoying, that doesn't change the validity of what they say about animals. Psychology even has a name for the reaction: do-gooder derogation. In 2012, researchers showed that meat eaters tend to rate vegetarians worse precisely when they imagine being judged by them. The irritation often comes from discomfort, not from the vegan's behavior.

Short answer

Some vegans may be annoying, like people in any group. But that changes nothing about the animals. Psychology shows that part of the irritation comes from feeling judged: in a 2012 study, meat eaters rated vegetarians worse precisely after imagining that vegetarians judged them. Often, a vegan only has to say they don't eat meat to be seen as "superior". The question remains: do the animals deserve to be spared?

For children

Sometimes someone speaks in an annoying way, even when they're right. Imagine a classmate tells you, in an irritating way, that you're standing on someone's foot. The way they said it might be annoying, but it's still good to move your foot. What matters is whether the person is right, not whether they're nice.

Ask a question back

"If the nicest vegan in the world made the same argument, would they be wrong?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"How do you know someone's vegan? They'll tell you. You think you're better than everyone."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Some vegans are pushy, moralistic or arrogant.
  2. P2That behavior is unpleasant and harms dialogue.
  3. P3A movement that behaves like that doesn't deserve to be taken seriously.
  4. CSo veganism can be ignored.

P1 and P2 may be true in some cases. The problem lies in P3: the behavior of some people says nothing about whether the idea they defend is true.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, which is an ad hominem.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument swaps assessing the idea for assessing the people:

    What the argument assessesWhat it should assess
    The tone of some vegansWhether animals feel and whether it's right to kill them
    The supposed superiorityThe reasons given
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's ad hominem.

    The behavior of those defending an idea doesn't make it true or false.

    2. The irritation often comes from discomfort.

    Minson and Monin showed in 2012 that meat eaters tend to disparage vegetarians, especially when they imagine being morally judged by them. The authors called this do-gooder derogation.

    3. Merely mentioning it already bothers people.

    Often, a vegan is seen as annoying just for turning down a dish or answering a question.

    4. Stereotypes generalize.

    Vegans are diverse, and many avoid talking about the subject precisely so as not to be seen this way.

    5. We wouldn't accept the argument for other causes.

    That some supporters of a cause are inconvenient is no reason to dismiss it; the question about the cause remains.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Minson and Monin, 2012, *Social Psychological and Personality Science*Meat eaters disparage vegetarians, especially when they anticipate being morally judged by themA preprint of the article
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: moralism hurts the cause

    The most serious version says some vegans' moralistic behavior harms their own cause, and that they should change how they communicate.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's a legitimate criticism of form.

    And animal advocates should take it seriously.

    It doesn't change the content.

    The message is still the same.

    It's no excuse for the listener.

    The question about the animals is for each person.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument is about vegans, not animals. But the animals didn't choose their advocates. The moral question doesn't depend on anyone's charm: is it fair to make them suffer and die when we don't need to?

What the argument gets right

What is true

There are vegans who communicate badly, judge others and push people away, and the movement should listen to that criticism. No one likes to feel judged, and dialogue works better with respect.

But

But the quality of an idea doesn't depend on the charm of whoever defends it. The question about the animals is still waiting for an answer, whoever asks it.
