N27

"Bringing back extinct species makes up for what we destroyed"

The answer in one sentence

de-extinction treats the species as what matters and the animals as a means to recover it. The individuals created, the females used to carry them and the animals they would hunt if released are left out of the account. A species feels nothing; animals do, and without securing a good life for them, recreating a species repairs no harm at all.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if not, that shows speciesism.

Reply

Recreating a species looks like repairing a mistake, but think about who will live through it. A species feels nothing; the animals born from the experiment do. Nobody guaranteed that the dire wolf pups will have a good life, or said where they will live. And if they are released, they will hunt animals who feel fear and pain. If the idea is to repair harm, the harm was done to individuals, not to a name on a list of species.

For children

Imagine someone makes a baby animal of a kind that no longer exists be born, just to show they could. Who will take care of it? Will it have companions, a good place to live? What matters most isn't the animal's name, but whether it will have a good life.

Ask a question back

"If the species comes back but the animals born have a bad life, what has been repaired?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"They brought the dire wolf back! Science is undoing the damage humanity did."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Species and biodiversity have value.
  2. P2Many species were driven extinct by human action.
  3. P3Whoever caused harm should repair it when they can.
  4. CSo bringing back extinct species is good, and even a duty when we caused the extinction.

P2 is true in many cases, and P3 carries weight. The problem lies in P1, which places value in the species rather than in the individuals who feel, and in the conclusion, which forgets who will live and die for the species to return.

The argument is valid in form, but P1 counts the species, which doesn't feel, and leaves out the animals, who do.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    QuestionWhat de-extinction usually answersWhat is left out
    Who benefits?The species, biodiversity, the prestige of scienceThe animals created
    How will the animals live?Usually no answerEach individual's welfare
    Who is used in the process?Talks about cells, embryos and techniqueThe females who carry them and lab animals
    What if they are released?Talks about restoring ecosystemsThe prey and the lives of the released animals themselves

    The term de-extinction, also called biological resurrection or species revivalism, refers to bringing into existence members of species that had gone extinct, or animals modified to resemble them.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. A species doesn't feel.

    Those who have good or bad experiences are individuals. The philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha (2025) notes that, in de-extinction projects, the animals' good appears only as a means to preserve the species, so nothing guarantees that anything would be done to prevent their suffering once the species was no longer at risk.

    2. The dire wolf case.

    In April 2025, the American company Colossal Biosciences announced the birth of three pups it called dire wolves, a species extinct for about 10,000 years, obtained by editing the genome of gray wolf cells and cloning. Many scientists consider them modified gray wolves; Cunha notes that the name matters little for the ethical discussion. What matters is that the company didn't say whether the wolves would live in sanctuaries, reserves, laboratories, zoos or the wild, and that nothing guarantees them a minimally good life. The same company plans to do the same with the mammoth.

    3. Some are used along the way.

    Cloning requires eggs, embryos and females who carry the pups, and it usually involves many losses before it works. Counting only the result leaves these animals out.

    4. Releasing predators is choosing for the prey.

    If the recreated animals are predators and are released, each of them will kill many others, and predation involves slow, painful deaths; in many cases the prey is eaten alive. When it's said that predators control herbivore populations, there are less harmful alternatives, such as sterilization and vegetation management (see N17).

    5. Repair whom?

    The harm of an extinction fell on the individuals who died, and bringing others of the same kind into existence repairs none of them: it creates new beings, whose welfare becomes the responsibility of whoever created them. Cunha also rejects the idea that the origin of an extinction, natural or human, changes the value of those living now.

    6. More species isn't more welfare.

    Species diversity, number of individuals and quality of life are different things. One more species can mean more lives that go badly, and the question that counts is how each one will live (N3).

    7. The problem isn't "playing God".

    The criticism isn't that the technique is artificial: gene therapy shows that altering genes isn't wrong in itself (N10). The problem is deciding without considering the beings affected. A de-extinction guided by the good of all sentient beings, and compared with other ways of reducing suffering, could be assessed differently; that isn't what happens in current projects.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the technique helps endangered species

    The most serious version says that the techniques developed for de-extinction, such as gene editing and cloning, also help save endangered species. In the United States, for example, a black-footed ferret was cloned in 2020 from cells stored for decades, to increase the species' genetic diversity.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It changes the subject.

    Helping endangered species doesn't justify bringing animals of extinct species into existence without securing their lives.

    The criterion is still the species.

    Even in conservation, each animal created, cloned or used as a surrogate mother has interests of its own, which need to count.

    Resources are limited.

    Cunha proposes comparing de-extinction with other interventions and asking which reduces suffering most. In that comparison, recreating an extinct predator is unlikely to come out ahead.

Contradictions

Celebrates the recreated pups.

But doesn't ask how they will live.

Talks about repairing human harm to nature.

But accepts creating new harms, to the animals used in the process and to the prey.

Values the dire wolf for being rare.

And ignores the gray wolves and other animals used to obtain it.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that many extinctions were caused by humans, that genetics can bring benefits and that losing forms of life impoverishes knowledge and ecosystems. The technique isn't wrong in itself, and caring about what we destroyed is legitimate.

But

The mistake is deciding without asking how the animals created, those used in the process and those affected by them will live. Repair, here, would mean caring for the individuals who feel.
