J6

"There are more important human problems"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's the fallacy of priority. Eating plants takes no time, money or energy away from any other cause. No one has to choose between fighting hunger and no longer paying for animals to be killed: it's possible to do both, and many people do. Besides, livestock farming is linked to serious human problems, such as deforestation, climate change, pandemics and dangerous working conditions.

Short answer

Eating plants takes no time or money away from any other cause. No one has to choose between fighting hunger and no longer paying for animals to be killed: it's possible to do both, and many people do. And livestock farming is linked to serious human problems: deforestation, climate change, pandemic risk and dangerous work. Caring for animals is often caring for people too.

For children

You can help people and animals at the same time. Eating beans instead of meat doesn't stop anyone from donating food, helping a friend or studying to change the world. You don't have to pick just one good thing to do.

Ask a question back

"What would you stop doing for people if you ate beans instead of meat?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"With so many children going hungry... Fight for people first."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1There are serious human problems, such as hunger, war and poverty.
  2. P2Moral resources and attention are limited.
  3. P3Human problems should have priority.
  4. CSo concern for animals distracts from what matters more.

P1 is true. So is P2, in part. The problem lies in the conclusion: no longer eating animals doesn't use up resources that would go to other causes.

The argument is valid if veganism competed with other causes. Its soundness fails because it doesn't.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument assumes causes compete:

    ActionDoes it use up resources from other causes?
    No longer eating animalsNo: it's a swap on the plate, often cheaper
    Campaigning for animalsSome time, like any cause
    Donating to fight hungerNot prevented by veganism
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's the fallacy of priority.

    That there are more serious problems doesn't make smaller ones irrelevant. If it held, almost no cause could be defended.

    2. Not causing harm isn't a competing cause.

    No longer eating animals is an abstention, not a project that takes up time.

    3. Livestock farming affects humans.

    Deforestation (H1), emissions (H12), antibiotic resistance and the risk of diseases that pass from animals to humans are human problems.

    4. The argument often serves to end the conversation.

    Even when it comes from someone devoted to another cause, it's still a false choice: not eating animals doesn't compete with any other struggle.

    5. Many people champion several causes.

    Animal advocates also work for human rights, against hunger and for social justice.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Our World in Data, land for agricultureLivestock uses about 80% of farmland and provides 17% of caloriesShows the link between livestock and food security
    Our World in Data, food choice versus eating localFood production is a large source of emissions, with beef among the highest-impact foodsShows the link between livestock and climate
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: animal activism takes energy

    The most serious version grants that eating plants doesn't compete with other causes, but says animal activism takes time and money that could go to human causes.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It changes the subject.

    The question was about eating animals, not about activism.

    All causes compete for time.

    And no one demands that only the most serious be championed.

    Animal suffering is enormous.

    Billions of sentient beings a year, which makes the cause relevant.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument doesn't say animals don't matter; it says other things matter more. But no longer paying for animals to be killed takes nothing away from those other things. The moral question remains: if it harms no one, why not stop?

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Fallacy of priority

Either humans or animals

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Ignoring connections
Doesn't see the link between livestock and human problems

Biases

Motivated reasoning

The priority of other causes excuses change

General literature on motivated cognition

Speciesism

Human interests always seem more important

Caviola, Everett and Faber, 2019

Zero-sum bias

Caring for animals is seen as taking something from humans, though the two do not compete for the same resources

Meegan, 2010

Contradictions

Devotes time to things less serious than hunger.

Such as hobbies, sport or local politics.

Isn't fighting hunger.

Uses the argument without acting.

Cares about cruelty to dogs.

Without thinking it distracts from human causes.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Human problems are serious and urgent, and no one should be indifferent to hunger, poverty and violence. It's also true that some people present the animal cause in ways that seem to disdain humans, which is a mistake.

But

But no longer eating animals doesn't compete with any of those causes. It's possible to care for people and animals at the same time, and often one helps the other.
