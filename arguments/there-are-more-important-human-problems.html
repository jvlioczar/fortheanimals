J20

"There are more important human problems"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's the fallacy of priority. Eating plants takes no time, money or energy away from any other cause. No one has to choose between fighting hunger and no longer paying for animals to be killed: it's possible to do both, and many people do. Besides, livestock farming is linked to serious human problems, such as deforestation, climate change, pandemics and dangerous working conditions. And if the idea of giving priority to the worse off is taken seriously and applied to all sentient beings, it may point to the animals, who are the most numerous victims and among those who suffer most intensely.

Reply

Does eating plants take time or money away from any other cause? You don't have to choose between fighting hunger and no longer paying for animals to be killed: it's possible to do both, and many people do. And livestock farming is linked to serious human problems: deforestation, climate change, pandemic risk and dangerous work. Caring for animals is often caring for people too. And if the rule is to give priority to the worse off, animals raised for food, killed by the billions and often in intense suffering, are near the front of the line.

For children

You can help people and animals at the same time. Eating beans instead of meat doesn't stop anyone from donating food, helping a friend or studying to change the world. You don't have to pick just one good thing to do. And if we should help those who suffer most first, many farm animals are among those who suffer most.

Ask a question back

"What would you stop doing for people if you ate beans instead of meat?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"With so many children going hungry... Fight for people first."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1There are serious human problems, such as hunger, war and poverty.
  2. P2Moral resources and attention are limited.
  3. P3Human problems should have priority.
  4. CSo concern for animals distracts from what matters more.

P1 is true. So is P2, in part. The problem lies in the conclusion: no longer eating animals doesn't use up resources that would go to other causes.

The argument is valid if veganism competed with other causes. Its soundness fails because it doesn't.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument assumes causes compete:

    ActionDoes it use up resources from other causes?
    No longer eating animalsNo: it's a swap on the plate, often cheaper
    Campaigning for animalsSome time, like any cause
    Donating to fight hungerNot prevented by veganism
    Giving priority to the worse offIf applied to all sentient beings, it may point to the animals
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's the fallacy of priority.

    That there are more serious problems doesn't make smaller ones irrelevant. If it held, almost no cause could be defended.

    2. Not causing harm isn't a competing cause.

    No longer eating animals is an abstention, not a project that takes up time.

    3. Livestock farming affects humans.

    Deforestation (H1), emissions (H7), antibiotic resistance and the risk of diseases that pass from animals to humans are human problems.

    4. The argument often serves to end the conversation.

    Even when it comes from someone devoted to another cause, it's still a false choice: not eating animals doesn't compete with any other struggle.

    5. Many people champion several causes.

    Animal advocates also work for human rights, against hunger and for social justice.

    6. Taken seriously, priority may point to the animals.

    There is a moral theory that gives the idea of priority a precise form: prioritarianism, associated with the philosopher Derek Parfit, who presented it in 1991 in the lecture "Equality or Priority?". On this theory, a benefit matters more the worse off its recipient is. It doesn't say "humans first", but "the worse off first". What counts is the level of well-being, not species. If the principle is applied to all sentient beings, priority may fall on the animals: more than 80 billion land animals are killed for food every year, plus a very uncertain number of fish, estimated in the hundreds of billions or more, and many of them spend their lives in confinement, in intense pain and deprivation. There is disagreement here: prioritarianism is usually formulated for human persons only; the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy acknowledges that this restriction is problematic because it excludes animals and treats the extension as an open question; comparing well-being across species is hard; and some authors defend different weights for humans and animals. But whoever appeals to priority can't, without further argument, stop at the species line. The philosopher Catia Faria, for example, argues that egalitarians and prioritarians are committed to rejecting speciesism and to giving priority to the interests of the animals who are worst off.

    7. Historically, the causes have gone together.

    In the 19th century, William Wilberforce, who fought slavery, was among the founders of the British animal protection society that would become the RSPCA, and Lord Shaftesbury, who championed the laws protecting factory workers, also campaigned against cruelty to animals. The Humanitarian League, founded by Henry Salt, defended social reform and compassion for all sentient beings at the same time. John Stuart Mill, Gandhi and Salt himself show that compassion can extend to several causes without contradiction (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 102, 104–105). The opposite criticism also has a history: part of the left, such as the Marxist philosopher Howard Parsons (1977), saw animal protection as a diversion of energy that should go to human struggles. The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane replies that real conflicts between the vital interests of humans and animals are rare, and that the greatest animal suffering, that of intensive farming, can be tackled without sacrificing any vital human interest (pp. 104–105).

    8. Even giving humans much more weight, scale matters.

    Luciano Carlos Cunha proposes a test: suppose a human being's suffering or death counted ten times more than an animal's. About 60 million people die each year worldwide. Animal farming alone slaughters more than 80 billion land animals a year, and fishing catches, on average, 1.1 to 2.2 trillion wild fish a year (Mood and Brooke, 2024). With numbers like these, even a tenfold weight for humans isn't enough to make animals a lower priority, if what counts is how much harm can be prevented. In this sense, even those who accept that humans count more would have reasons to pay far more attention to animals than is paid today (Cunha, 2022, pp. 127–129). The reasoning doesn't say each person should drop other causes, and point 2 still holds: not eating animals takes resources from no cause.

    9. Proportion bias.

    We tend to prefer solving a large fraction of a small problem to solving a small fraction of an enormous one, even when the second option prevents far more suffering. Solving 1% of a problem that affects trillions may mean more victims spared than solving all of a problem that affects thousands. What matters is how much harm is prevented, not what percentage of the problem is solved (Cunha, 2022, p. 230).

    10. Singer opened his chapter on animals with this objection.

    In Practical Ethics, Peter Singer begins the discussion of equality for animals with the question of how anyone can spend time on animals when equality is denied to so many humans. He likens that attitude to the prejudice that led slaveholders not to take seriously the interests of the people they enslaved (ch. 3). He also holds that changing our attitude toward animals would reduce total suffering so much that it's hard to imagine any other moral change with a comparable effect, a claim that depends on scale and on comparing suffering across species, and that is contested by those who give priority to humans. Singer also links the two causes: in the chapter on rich and poor, he notes that the grain and soy fed to animals would be enough to end hunger if distributed, but adds that hunger is mainly a problem of distribution and poverty, not of production (ch. 8). Today's data keep the picture: about 36% of the calories from the world's crops go to feed (Cassidy et al., 2013), and about 673 million people faced hunger in 2024, according to the annual report by FAO and other UN agencies. Changing diets doesn't end hunger on its own, since hunger depends on income, conflict and access to food, but it doesn't compete with fighting it either.

    11. Only one cause gets asked.

    People devoted to the elderly don't usually hear "why aren't you saving children?", and people who work with children aren't told they should be fighting malaria. Among human causes, it is accepted that each person chooses theirs out of affinity, talent or life history, and nobody demands proof that they picked the most serious one in the world. The demand shows up almost only when the cause is animals. It is the fallacy of priority, also called relative privation, applied with a double standard: in conversation it usually takes the form of whataboutism, where instead of answering the question, someone points to another. One plausible explanation is that the question starts from a speciesist premise, that animal suffering counts for little or nothing, and often works as self-defense: if the animal cause is disqualified, there is no need to think about what one eats. This is what psychology describes as motivated reasoning in the face of the meat paradox (Bastian and Loughnan, 2017). It doesn't apply to everyone: some people ask in good faith, out of real dedication to another cause, and for them points 2 and 5 apply. The same uneven demand appears in attacks on vegans (L1) and in the war version (J21).

    12. By number of victims, animal exploitation is by far the largest oppression caused by humans.

    To the numbers in point 8 we must add farmed fish: an estimated 78 to 171 billion were killed for food in 2019, with a midpoint of 124 billion, not counting those that die during rearing (Mood et al., 2023). Adding land animals, farmed fish and wild fish, that comes to somewhere between 1.3 and 2.5 trillion animals killed per year, most of them fish, in a world of just over 8 billion people. No other form of violence or domination carried out by humans comes close to this scale in number of victims. As for total suffering, it is plausible that it is also the largest, but here the comparison is more uncertain: measuring the suffering of individuals of different species depends on assumptions about each one's capacity for good and bad experiences, and projects that try to estimate these "welfare ranges", such as Rethink Priorities' Moral Weight Project (2023), work with very wide margins of uncertainty and contestable philosophical assumptions. The point is not that human victims matter less, nor to set up a competition between victims; it is to recall the scale, which the phrase "there are more important problems" usually ignores.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: animal activism takes energy

    The most serious version grants that eating plants doesn't compete with other causes, but says animal activism takes time and money that could go to human causes.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It changes the subject.

    The question was about eating animals, not about activism.

    All causes compete for time.

    And no one demands that only the most serious be championed.

    Animal suffering is enormous.

    Billions of sentient beings a year, which makes the cause relevant. By the standard of giving priority to the worse off, it may even be among the most urgent.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument doesn't say animals don't matter; it says other things matter more. But no longer paying for animals to be killed takes nothing away from those other things. The moral question remains: if it harms no one, why not stop?

What the argument gets right

What is true

Human problems are serious and urgent, and no one should be indifferent to hunger, poverty and violence. It's also true that some people present the animal cause in ways that seem to disdain humans, which is a mistake.

But

But no longer eating animals doesn't compete with any of those causes. It's possible to care for people and animals at the same time, and often one helps the other.
