B7

"Without farming, these animals wouldn't even exist"

The answer in one sentence

bringing someone into existence gives no right to kill them. Parents give life to their children and gain no right over their lives because of it. Henry Salt called this argument, in 1914, "the logic of the larder": the idea that whoever loves animals most would be whoever breeds the most of them to eat. And it protects no animal that already exists; it only justifies breeding others to kill. Existing isn't good in itself either: existing in a bad life is bad, and bringing someone into the world for that can be unjust.

Reply

Giving someone life doesn't give the right to take it: your parents gave you life, and that gives them no right over it. Someone who doesn't exist loses nothing by not existing; someone who exists loses everything by being killed. And the sentence protects no cow that exists today: it only justifies breeding others to kill. Would you say breeding dogs for meat is a favor to dogs, or that bringing people into the world to exploit them would be fair to them? And existing isn't good in itself: a life of confinement and suffering is bad for the one who lives it, and should be avoided.

For children

Your parents gave you life. Does that mean they can do whatever they want with you, even hurt you? Of course not! Giving someone life doesn't give the right to take it. It's the same with animals: making a calf be born doesn't give anyone the right to kill it later.

Ask a question back

"If someone had a child only to sell it later, would having given it life justify the sale?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"If no one ate meat, there'd be no cows. We're giving them life."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Existing with a life worth living is better, for a being, than not existing.
  2. P2Farmed animals only exist because they are bred for consumption.
  3. P3If consumption ended, these animals would not exist.
  4. CSo consumption benefits the animals by bringing them into existence.

The strong version has a well-known philosophical formulation, the replaceability argument: if each animal killed is replaced by another with an equally good life, the total amount of well-being in the world does not decrease.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1, which treats non-existence as a loss for someone who doesn't exist, and in the conclusion, which moves from "brought into life" to "may kill".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Bringing into existence gives no right to kill.

    Parents, dog breeders and sanctuaries bring animals and people into existence, and no one considers that this licenses them to kill.

    2. The same reasoning would justify what no one accepts.

    It isn't reasonable to say that breeding dogs for meat is a favor to dogs, because without that trade they wouldn't have been born. Nor that it would be fair to bring people into the world to exploit them, on the grounds that without this they wouldn't exist. In none of these cases does having caused someone to be born improve what is done to them afterwards.

    3. Existing isn't good in itself.

    The argument assumes that any existence is a gift. But existing in a bad way is bad for the one who exists. A life marked by confinement, pain and fear isn't a benefit that makes up for the rest, and bringing someone into the world knowing they will live like that can be unjust to them. That's why it makes sense to avoid creating bad lives, even if it means those individuals never come to exist: no one is harmed by not being born, but someone is harmed by being born to suffer.

    4. Someone who doesn't exist isn't harmed.

    There is no cow who fails to exist and misses life. Whoever loses something is the cow who exists and is killed. The argument compares a real being with an imaginary one.

    5. Henry Salt answered this in 1914.

    In the essay The Logic of the Larder, Salt observed that, by this reasoning, the true lover of animals would be the one whose larder was fullest of them. He called the idea the very negation of reverence for life.

    6. The life given is rarely good.

    About three quarters of farmed land animals live in intensive systems (B4). For them, "we gave them life" means "we gave them a life of confinement".

    7. Extinction is not a harm to the animals.

    The end of farming would reduce the number of animals born to die; it wouldn't kill the ones that exist. Domesticated species could go on existing in smaller numbers, and many were shaped by breeding to serve production, with health problems they wouldn't have on their own.

    8. Killing someone who exists isn't the same as not breeding someone.

    When an animal is killed, there is an individual who loses their life. When an animal is not bred, no one loses anything: there are only possibilities. That's why one death is not "cancelled out" by another birth. If it were, parents could kill a child as long as they had another in its place.

    9. Salt was answering Leslie Stephen, and Singer himself came to doubt the answer.

    Salt's target was the British philosopher Leslie Stephen, who wrote in the 19th century that the pig has the strongest interest in the demand for bacon, because without it there would be no pigs. Salt replied that it's a confusion to compare existence with non-existence, as if one could speak of the good of someone who doesn't exist. Singer accepted that reply in the first edition of Animal Liberation (1975), but later came to doubt it: if it's wrong to knowingly bring into the world a being who will have a miserable life, it's hard to explain why bringing a happy one wouldn't be good (Practical Ethics, ch. 5). Points 4 and 8 depend on this asymmetry between bad lives and good lives, which is still debated in philosophy. But even if bringing a good life into the world were a good thing, that wouldn't give anyone the right to take it away later (point 1).

    10. The limits Singer himself places on the argument.

    In Practical Ethics, Singer calls this reasoning "the replaceability argument" and greatly restricts its reach (ch. 5):

    • It doesn't apply to factory farming, where the animals' lives are more a burden than a benefit to them.
    • On the "total" view, it proves too much. If creating happy lives is good, it would be good to have as many happy beings as the planet can support, which could call for replacing humans with a much larger number of small, happy animals. And if happy people are preferred, the land feeds more people with plants than with animal farming, with the possible exception of arid regions only fit for grazing.
    • It only applies when one animal's death makes another's existence possible. A duck shot by a hunter isn't replaced by any other, unless the population is at the limit of the available food; so killing it is wrong even on the simplest utilitarian count. Singer adds that assuming the hunter kills instantly is almost certainly false. The point applies to hunting for population control and to sport hunting alike (H19, I28).
    11. For self-conscious beings, not even Singer accepts replaceability, and he admits the question is still open.

    In his review of the first edition, the legal philosopher H. L. A. Hart objected that, for a utilitarian, self-conscious beings would also be replaceable: if someone's preferences can be outweighed by those of others, why not by new preferences created to take their place? Singer answers that creating a preference and then satisfying it isn't equivalent to satisfying one that already exists. To explain the difference, he proposes two models. In the "moral ledger" model, creating an unsatisfied preference opens a debit that only its satisfaction cancels; he himself notes that, without adjustments, this model would imply it would be better if no one were born. In the "journey of life" model, cutting short a journey already under way, with plans and hopes invested in it, is worse than preventing it from starting. Singer says both are metaphors, not a solution, that the replaceability argument was the most criticized in the book, and that no fully satisfactory answer has been found so far (ch. 5). And the line between who is and who isn't replaceable depends on knowing who is self-conscious, a question on which he himself asks for the benefit of the doubt.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: replaceability

    The most serious version says that, for beings without a sense of themselves over time, what matters is the total amount of good experiences in the world. If an animal with a good life is killed and replaced by another with an equally good life, the total doesn't change. Peter Singer discussed this idea, though with many caveats.

    Singer distinguishes self-conscious beings, who are aware of themselves existing over time and have desires about the future, from merely conscious beings (Practical Ethics, ch. 5). For him, fish are the clearest case of an animal that is conscious but not a person. About birds he is cautious: he calls the premise that chickens aren't self-conscious questionable, and the premise that ducks aren't dubious. As for mammals, he grants full protection against killing categorically only to the great apes; for whales, dolphins, monkeys, dogs, cats, pigs, cattle and others, he thinks extending it is defensible with varying degrees of confidence, perhaps to all mammals, depending on how far we're willing to give the benefit of the doubt. In theory, then, raising animals without self-awareness with lives worth living, killing them painlessly and replacing them with others who would only be born because of that might not be wrong in itself. Singer grants that, as a piece of critical-level reasoning, the argument may be sound.

    In practice, though, he recommends refusing to kill animals for food unless it's needed to survive. The reason he gives isn't fear of a relapse: killing animals for food leads us to see them as objects at our disposal, and it's hard to cultivate equal consideration for the interests of those we keep eating for pleasure (ch. 5, end of the section on killing non-self-conscious animals). He places this rule at the intuitive level of the philosopher R. M. Hare, the level of principles that should guide daily life, as distinct from the critical level, where theoretical hypotheses are examined (ch. 4). Earlier, he had noted that raising animals without suffering might be possible on a small scale, but doesn't seem feasible on the scale needed to feed urban populations (ch. 3). The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane reads the rule as a slippery-slope argument, the fear that treating birds and fish as resources would lead back to intensive farming, and finds that argument weak: a society that had changed enough to farm only free-range animals would be unlikely to go back (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 39–41). The criticism hits that reading, which is weaker than the reason given in the book.

    Outside academic philosophy, the most widely read version is the journalist Michael Pollan's ("An Animal's Place", New York Times Magazine, 2002). Pollan reports that, for Singer, a happy life on a good farm adds happiness to the world, provided the slaughtered animal is replaced by another, and adds an argument of his own: domesticated species prospered because they allied themselves with humans, and their good, which is living according to their own nature, can't be achieved apart from us. The mutualism argument is discussed in B16.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It proves too much.

    Applied to beings without self-awareness, it would also hold for human newborns. Almost no one accepts this (A19, B2).

    It requires good lives.

    Replaceability only works if each animal has a life worth living, which doesn't describe most animal farming.

    It treats individuals as containers.

    Each animal stops being someone and becomes a place where well-being is stored. That is exactly what an ethics of individuals rejects: Tom Regan criticizes utilitarianism precisely for seeing animals as "receptacles" of well-being rather than as individuals with value of their own (Cochrane, 2010, p. 45).

    Singer himself doesn't use it to defend today's consumption.

    He considers it inapplicable to factory farming.

    The answer shouldn't depend on the practical rule.

    On Cochrane's reading, the only barrier against killing birds and fish would be the fear of a relapse, and a criticism like his would be enough to knock it down. The reason Singer gives is a different one, that eating animals leads us to treat them as objects, but it too is a rule for practice, which leaves the thesis intact at the theoretical level. The firmer answer lies elsewhere: death is a harm to whoever loses their future, whether or not they have plans for it (B2), and the real conditions of farming are far from the good lives the thesis assumes (point 2). Besides, the line between self-conscious and merely conscious is uncertain: magpies recognized their own image in a mirror in a 2008 study, and cleaner fish passed a version of the mark test in 2019, though what these tests show is still debated.

    Even on Singer's own terms, the counter-reply covers very little.

    It requires, all at once, an animal without self-awareness, a good life, a painless death, no suffering for other animals and a death that makes another animal's birth possible. Singer concludes that these situations are special and don't cover most of the deaths humans inflict on animals every year (Practical Ethics, ch. 5).

    Pollan's argument swaps the individual for the species.

    That the species prospers through farming is no benefit to the animal born to be slaughtered, and the good of living according to one's own nature would be greater if that life weren't cut short so early (A10, B16). Pollan himself grants that industrial farming, where most meat comes from, doesn't offer that good.

What the argument gets right

What is true

There's a real philosophical question here: the ethics of bringing beings into existence is hard, and whether it's good for there to be more good lives in the world divides serious philosophers. It's also true that the end of animal farming would change the relationship between humans and domesticated species, and that this calls for careful thought.

But

But none of this turns birth into permission to kill. If the animal exists, it is already someone, and what matters is what is done to it, not the fact that it was brought into the world for that purpose.
