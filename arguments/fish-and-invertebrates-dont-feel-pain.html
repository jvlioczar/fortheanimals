A6

"Fish and invertebrates don't feel pain"

The answer in one sentence

fish have nociceptors, change their behavior for long periods after injury and respond to painkillers; crustaceans and cephalopods came to be recognized as sentient in British law after a review of more than 300 studies. Doubt about them is selective: it survives because they don't scream, have no facial expressions and die far from us. About insects, the uncertainty is real, and this chapter says so plainly.

Short answer

Fish have pain receptors with properties similar to ours, discovered in 2003 in rainbow trout. After an injury, they change their behavior for hours, stop eating and respond to painkillers. Hermit crabs abandon their shells after small shocks and trade safety from predators for escape from the stimulus, a kind of decision no reflex explains. After a review of more than 300 studies, the United Kingdom came to recognize octopuses, lobsters and crabs as sentient beings.

For children

Fish don't make noises like we do, but that doesn't mean they don't feel. When a fish gets hurt, it stops eating, breathes faster and rubs the hurt part, just as you rub a scraped knee. Many fish also look after their young and learn to avoid places where they got hurt. Scientists have found that they have, in their bodies, the same pain sensors we have.

Ask a question back

"If they feel nothing, why do ethics committees require anesthesia for operating on fish in the lab?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Fish don't feel pain, and shrimp even less."

Strongest version (steelman). It's the same structure as A5, applied to the hardest cases. It was defended by James Rose (2002) and Brian Key (2016):

  1. P1Conscious pain depends on cortical processing that integrates nociceptive information and produces the unpleasant experience.
  2. P2Fish don't have a neocortex, and invertebrates have nervous systems even further from ours.
  3. P3Escape, learning and changes in behavior can be explained by nociception and conditioning, without any experience.
  4. CSo catching fish, suffocating them on ice or boiling animals alive causes no suffering.

Three different claims are hidden here, and they should be separated: that there's no nociception (false), that there's nociception without experience (the real debate), and that even if there is experience, it would weigh little (a claim no one quantifies).

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1, which raises one particular structure to a universal requirement, and at P3, which ignores precisely the behaviors science uses to tell reflex from experience.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    Three different claims travel together in this argument. Separating them already settles much of the discussion.

    ClaimStatus
    There's no nociception: the body doesn't even detect the harmful stimulusFalse for fish, and also for decapods and cephalopods
    There's nociception, but without experienceThis is the real scientific debate; the majority position today is against this reading in vertebrates
    There's experience, but it weighs littleNever accompanied by a criterion or a number; a discount declared, not demonstrated
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Fish have nociceptors.

    The study by Sneddon, Braithwaite and Gentle (2003) identified, in rainbow trout, skin receptors that respond to mechanical pressure, harmful heat above 40 degrees and acetic acid. After exposure, the fish breathed faster, took longer to resume feeding and showed anomalous behaviors for a prolonged period, which the authors interpreted as discomfort.

    2. The cortex criterion is arbitrary, and birds prove it.

    Birds don't have a neocortex and practically no one denies they feel pain. The Cambridge Declaration (2012) says exactly this: lacking a neocortex doesn't prevent affective states. Different structures can perform the same function, a common outcome in evolution.

    3. Motivational trade-offs aren't reflexes.

    This is where the evidence in invertebrates gets interesting. Hermit crabs that receive small shocks abandon their shell, and are more reluctant to abandon better-quality shells (Elwood and Appel, 2009). In another experiment, they traded protection from predator risk for escape from the harmful stimulus. Weighing two costs requires central processing, not a reflex arc.

    4. Painkillers change behavior.

    One of the criteria in the review by Sneddon et al. (2014) is exactly this: if altered behavior returns to normal with a painkiller, the reflex explanation loses force.

    5. The law followed the evidence.

    The review led by Jonathan Birch and colleagues (2021), covering more than 300 studies, recommended treating cephalopods and decapods as sentient, and the United Kingdom incorporated this into its law. In the European Union, live cephalopods were already protected under the directive on animals used in research.

    6. The scale makes the error serious.

    Fish are by far the animals we kill in the greatest numbers. Estimates from the fishcount project put the number of fish caught each year at between 790 billion and 2.3 trillion on average from 2007 to 2016, plus tens to hundreds of billions of farmed fish. These are approximate figures, derived from FAO tonnages, but the order of magnitude is clear: if there's experience there, the volume of suffering is the largest there is.

    7. When in doubt, the benefit goes to the animal.

    There's no absolute proof of sentience even for other humans. The question is how to act under uncertainty: if there's a reasonable chance fish and invertebrates feel, the cost of erring against them, at the numbers of fishing and aquaculture, is far greater than that of erring in their favor. And a smaller brain doesn't guarantee less suffering: in animals with fewer cognitive resources, pain may be the main driver of behavior and, for that reason, intense. See Questão de Ética, vol. II, ch. 13 and vol. VI, ch. 8.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: small brains, different architecture

    The serious reply says nociception and behavior change aren't enough. A robot can move away from heat and learn to avoid a path. What would matter is the architecture that produces the unpleasant experience, and a fish's brain would have too few neurons and too little integration for it. In invertebrates, the evolutionary distance would be even greater, weakening the inference by analogy.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The counterexample of birds.

    If lacking a neocortex were enough to rule out experience, birds wouldn't feel pain. Almost no one accepts that conclusion, and comparative neuroscience shows analogous structures performing equivalent functions.

    Motivational trade-offs.

    No reflex weighs costs. The crab that accepts more predator risk to escape the shock is integrating information, which is exactly what the reply demands.

    It's a minority position.

    Key's article (2016) drew more than thirty published commentaries, most opposed, several arguing that lacking a neocortex doesn't imply absence of pain.

    Under uncertainty, scale decides.

    If the skeptic is right, we lose little by treating fish with care. If they're wrong, the error affects hundreds of billions of individuals a year. No reasonable risk calculation recommends betting on the absence of pain.

    The skeptic doesn't act like a believer.

    Research ethics committees require anesthesia for procedures on fish, and laws have begun to ban boiling lobsters alive. Institutional practice has already moved.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Confusing structure and function
Requires a neocortex, ignoring that birds feel without one
False dichotomy
Either pure reflex or experience identical to ours
Unwarranted generalization
Treats fish, octopuses, crabs and insects as a single block, despite very different evidence

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Appeal to ignorance
From the absence of definitive proof it concludes absence of pain
Cherry picking
Cites the minority position and omits the published replies
Empirical myth
Repeats the legend of the three-second memory
Ignoring the asymmetry of risk
Bets on the absence of pain in a case with hundreds of billions of potential victims

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Similarity as criterion
Uses evolutionary distance and lack of facial expression as if they were a moral argument
Discount without criterion
Claims their pain "weighs less" without saying by how much or why

Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "whoever has a brain very different from ours, or doesn't express pain as we do, doesn't feel".

If it applied to…BirdsTap to see

They wouldn't feel pain. Birds don't have a neocortex, and P1's criterion excludes them along with fish

Practically no one accepts this, and the Cambridge Declaration says the opposite

If it applied to…Animals that hide painTap to see

Rabbits, rodents and other prey animals mask signs of pain so as not to attract predators; by the criterion of visible expression, they'd suffer less

Veterinary medicine has learned the opposite: in these species, absence of signs doesn't mean absence of pain

If it applied to…Premature babiesTap to see

Their pain would be dismissed. They don't cry like an adult and don't have a mature cortex

This was the historical justification for surgery with minimal anesthesia until the 1980s

If it applied to…Sedated or paralyzed patientsTap to see

If the criterion is visible expression, someone chemically prevented from reacting wouldn't be suffering

Anesthesiology treats confusing immobility with absence of pain as a serious error

If it applied to…Beings with radically different biologyTap to see

An extraterrestrial life form or a conscious artificial intelligence could be tortured because it doesn't resemble us

Shows that the relevant criterion is functional evidence, not resemblance

Contradictions

Requires anesthesia in research.

Ethics committees demand anesthesia and pain relief for procedures on fish, which only makes sense if there's pain.

Condemns cruelty to the aquarium fish.

Leaving a pet fish without clean water is seen as cruelty; suffocating a fish on ice for twenty minutes isn't.

Uses the animal debate to choose fish.

Whoever turns pescatarian to "reduce suffering" is admitting there's suffering at stake, and merely betting it's smaller.

Treats shrimp and octopus the same.

Octopuses solve complex problems, open jars and use tools, and still get thrown into the same bucket of "invertebrates that don't feel".

Accepts the evidence when it's law.

People living in countries that have recognized decapod sentience don't usually say their parliament has gone mad; they just don't apply the conclusion to their own plate.

What the argument gets right

What is true

There has been exaggeration on both sides of this debate, and it's worth recognizing what the skeptic gets right. Evolutionary distance really does weaken inference by analogy: the more distant the nervous system, the less reliable it is to draw conclusions from our own case. The evidence also isn't uniform. For fish, decapods and cephalopods, it's strong. For insects, the review by Gibbons and colleagues (2022) itself shows an uneven picture, with some orders satisfying six of eight criteria and others lacking enough research. For bivalves such as oysters and mussels, the absence of a central nervous system makes doubt legitimate, the subject of chapter F7.

But

The mistake is using the uncertainty of one case to dismiss them all, and treating "doesn't look like me" as if it were a reason. The right question isn't whether a fish resembles us, but whether there's evidence that someone is there for whom it is bad. For the aquatic animals we kill by the billions, the answer has already changed laws.
