D11

"Jesus ate fish"

The answer in one sentence

what a historical figure did in their context doesn't decide what we should do in ours. In first-century Palestine, fishing was one of the main means of survival. Today we have alternatives. And Christianity itself never understood imitating Jesus as copying his diet, but as following his values, such as compassion for the most vulnerable.

Short answer

Jesus lived in first-century Palestine, where fishing was a means of survival and there weren't the alternatives we have today. Christians don't copy everything Jesus did, like how he dressed, traveled or worked: they follow his values. And compassion for the vulnerable is at their center. The question isn't what Jesus ate back then, but what those values ask of us today.

For children

Jesus lived a long time ago, in a place where people fished so they wouldn't go hungry. Today we have stores and lots of other foods. Following Jesus isn't doing everything he did, like wearing sandals or living without a fridge. It's trying to be kind like him, including to those who are weaker.

Ask a question back

"Is following Jesus copying what he ate, or living the values he taught?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Jesus ate fish. Jesus multiplied the fish. In the Bible they sacrificed lambs."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1According to the Gospels, Jesus ate fish and took part in the Passover, which included lamb.
  2. P2Jesus is the perfect moral model for Christians.
  3. P3What the perfect moral model did can't be wrong.
  4. CSo eating animals isn't wrong.

P1 is accepted by most readings of the Gospels. P2 is a premise of faith. The problem lies in P3: what someone did in one historical context doesn't automatically carry over to another.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3 and in the assumption that copying actions is the same as following values.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses imitating actions with following values:

    What Jesus did in his contextWhat Christians do today
    Traveled on foot or by donkeyUse cars and planes
    Lived without modern medicineUse hospitals and vaccines
    Ate what was available in first-century GalileeHave access to foods from all over the world
    Preached compassion and care for the weakThis is what following Jesus is understood to mean
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's an appeal to authority.

    A respected figure having done something doesn't prove it's right in every context.

    2. The context was different.

    In first-century Galilee, fishing was one of the bases of the local economy, alongside bread, olive oil and legumes. There were no B12 supplements or the variety of foods that today make it safe to give up all animal products.

    3. Following Jesus is following values, not copying habits.

    No Christian tradition requires living as in the first century. It would be strange for diet to be the only exception.

    4. The values point in another direction.

    Compassion, care for the vulnerable and refusing unnecessary violence are central to Christianity. Many Christians, from various traditions, are vegetarian or vegan for these reasons.

    5. Animal sacrifices were abandoned.

    Christianity itself set aside the temple's animal sacrifices, which shows biblical practices involving animals aren't seen as permanent.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Pope Francis, *Laudato Si'* (2015)Rejects absolute dominion over other creaturesShows the tradition doesn't read the relationship with animals as a license
    The Gospels (Luke 24:42-43; John 21)Describe Jesus eating and offering fishBiblical text; these passages are historically debated
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: if it were wrong, Jesus wouldn't have done it

    The most serious version says that, since Jesus was without sin, nothing he did could be morally wrong, and so eating fish can't be wrong in itself.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Not wrong there isn't not wrong here.

    An action can be justified by necessity in one context and not in another.

    This isn't about condemning Jesus.

    It's about asking what to do today, with today's alternatives.

    Fishing for food isn't industrial animal farming.

    Nothing in the Gospels resembles confining billions of animals.

Contradictions

Doesn't copy Jesus in anything else.

Not in clothing, transport or profession.

Doesn't practice animal sacrifice.

Which is also in the Bible.

Recognizes compassion as a Christian value.

And it extends to the most vulnerable.

What the argument gets right

What is true

For people of faith, Jesus's example is central, and it's legitimate to seek guidance in it. The Gospels do describe Jesus eating fish, and most Christian traditions don't require vegetarianism.

But

But following Jesus never meant copying what he ate. It means living the compassion he taught, and many Christians conclude that, today, that compassion includes animals.
