F8

"What about meat that gets thrown away?"

The answer in one sentence

eating something that would be thrown away anyway, without creating demand, is a different case from buying meat. But it's a rare case that doesn't describe everyday eating, and it still has a cost: it reinforces the idea that animals are food. Besides, waste isn't an argument for continuing to produce; it's one more reason to produce less. It's also the reasoning of freeganism, which recovers discarded food, including animal products, so as not to feed consumption: a serious position that holds for genuine leftovers, but not for everyday meat.

Short answer

It's true that eating something that would be thrown away anyway, without anyone buying more because of it, doesn't increase demand. It's a rare and different case. But it doesn't describe everyday life: most meat is bought, and every purchase creates demand. And even leftovers have a cost: eating them reinforces the idea that animals are food, for whoever eats and whoever watches. Waste is a reason to produce less, not to keep producing.

For children

If there's a piece of meat left over at a party and it's going in the trash, eating it or not changes nothing for that animal, who has already died. But if people get used to thinking "it's fine to eat it", they keep buying more meat. And then more animals die. The best thing is to have less meat left over, by buying less.

Ask a question back

"Was most of the meat you eat leftovers that would have gone in the trash?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"If it'll be thrown out, better to eat it. Party leftovers don't count."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1The harm to the animal happens in raising and slaughter, not in eating.
  2. P2If the meat has already been produced and will be thrown away, eating it doesn't increase demand.
  3. P3If there's no increase in demand, eating that meat causes no new harm.
  4. CSo eating meat that would go in the trash isn't wrong.

P1, P2 and P3 may be true in specific cases. The argument is stronger than it looks. The problem is that it only holds for those cases, and even in them there are indirect effects.

The argument is valid and perhaps sound for the strict case. It fails when used to justify ordinary consumption.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument separates consumption from demand:

    SituationDoes it create demand?A common case?
    Buying meat at the storeYesYes
    Ordering meat at a restaurantYesYes
    Eating leftovers that would be binned, unplannedPerhaps notNo, it's rare
    Planning to "take the leftovers" every timeYes, leftovers become expectedNo
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's a rare case.

    Almost all meat consumed is bought. The leftovers case doesn't describe everyday eating (K1).

    2. Recurring leftovers create demand.

    If someone always eats the leftovers, whoever cooks starts counting on it, and the leftovers stop being leftovers.

    3. There are indirect effects.

    Eating meat, even meat that would be thrown away, reinforces the idea that animals are food, for whoever eats and whoever watches. That affects the willingness to change.

    4. Waste is part of the problem.

    According to the FAO, about a third of the food produced in the world is lost or wasted. With meat, that means animals raised and killed for nothing. The answer is to produce less, not to normalize consumption.

    5. The argument changes the subject.

    The question was about buying meat, not about what to do with leftovers.

    6. Freeganism takes the argument seriously, and shows its limits.

    The word freegan combines free and vegan. Freegans are people who, in protest against consumerism and waste, avoid buying and eat largely from discarded food. According to the sociologist Alex Barnard, most participants in the New York freegan group she studied were vegetarian or vegan before becoming freegan, and some of them began eating animal products recovered from the trash, with this chapter's reasoning: if it was going to be thrown away, it creates no demand. It's a consistent position for the strict case, but it holds only for that case. For whoever buys meat, nothing changes, and the indirect effect of treating the animal as food remains. The debate on whether this is compatible with veganism is on the page The definition of veganism, debated.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: wasting it also disrespects the animal

    The most serious version says that throwing away the meat of an animal who has already died is a form of disrespect, and that eating it honors, in some way, the life that was taken.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The animal doesn't benefit.

    Eating or not eating changes nothing for them.

    Respect would lie in not killing.

    Honoring the life taken would mean preventing others from being taken.

    The counter-reply doesn't apply to ordinary consumption.

    It's still about a rare case.

Contradictions

Buys meat, not just leftovers.

The case doesn't describe their own consumption.

Wouldn't eat leftovers from any animal.

Leftover dog meat wouldn't be eaten "so as not to waste it".

Fights waste by producing more?

Waste is an argument for reducing production.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The argument is stronger than many admit. Eating something that really would go in the trash, without creating any demand, is different from buying meat, and some philosophers consider it acceptable. It's also true that food waste is a serious problem.

But

But the case is rare, the indirect effects exist, and waste points to the need to produce less, not to normalizing consumption.
