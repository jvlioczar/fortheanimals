C1

"It's natural to eat meat."

The answer in one sentence

something being natural doesn't tell us whether it's right. Nature also produces disease, infanticide and war, and we correct nature all the time with medicine, glasses and laws. Besides, what is called natural here has nothing natural about it: animals bred to grow fast, confined and slaughtered on an industrial scale. The argument describes our species' past and presents it as a moral rule for the present.

Note: this chapter doesn't discuss whether humans can digest meat, which is true. It discusses whether that makes eating it morally acceptable. The nutritional question is left to family G.

Short answer

Being natural doesn't make something right. Nature also has disease, infanticide and the strong dominating the weak, and no one uses that as a moral rule. We use medicine, glasses and anesthesia, which aren't natural, and we think they're great. Besides, supermarket meat comes from animals genetically selected to produce more, confined and slaughtered on an industrial scale, which is nothing like natural. Being able to eat meat is one thing; needing to and having to are others.

For children

All sorts of things happen in nature. Some animals steal food from others, fight, or leave the weakest behind. Does that mean we can do the same? No. We learned to look after people who get sick and to share with those who have less, and that doesn't come from nature, it comes from thinking about what's fair. Eating animals may have been natural once, but today we can live well without hurting anyone.

Ask a question back

"If being natural makes something right, are C-sections, vaccines and glasses wrong?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Eating meat is natural. Humans have always eaten it; it's part of our nature."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Humans evolved as omnivores, and eating meat has been part of our species' history.
  2. P2Behaviors that are part of a species' nature are legitimate for members of that species.
  3. CSo eating meat is legitimate for humans.

P1 is true. All the weight rests on P2, which makes a move philosophy has examined since the eighteenth century: from a description of how things are to a norm about how they ought to be.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2, which no one applies consistently, and in the word "natural", which changes meaning along the way.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument is the best-known example of the appeal to nature: treating the natural as good and the unnatural as bad. It also runs into what is called Hume's law. David Hume observed, in 1739, that many moral arguments slip without warning from statements with "is" to conclusions with "ought", without explaining how that step is justified. P2 is exactly that step.

    The word "natural" also changes meaning as convenient:

    Sense of "natural"What it meansProblem
    StatisticalWhat most people doIt's the appeal to popularity (D6): slavery was once the rule
    EvolutionaryWhat our ancestors didOur ancestors also lived without anesthesia, writing or rights
    BiologicalWhat the body can doBeing able isn't needing, nor ought (C3)
    Opposite of artificialWhat doesn't pass through human handsIndustrial meat is one of the most artificial products there are
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Nature isn't a moral guide.

    John Stuart Mill, in the essay Nature, published in 1874, put the problem clearly: if imitating nature were a standard of conduct, it would be right to kill, torture and destroy, because nature does so all the time. Either we accept that conclusion, or we admit that being natural was never, in itself, a reason to consider something good.

    2. No one applies P2 consistently.

    Infanticide, rape and violence between groups also appear in other species and in human history. No one concludes they're legitimate. In the other direction, almost everything we're proud of is unnatural: medicine, glasses, laws that protect the weak, the very idea of rights.

    3. What is defended isn't natural.

    Broiler chickens were bred to grow so fast that they're slaughtered at a few weeks old. Dairy cows are made pregnant again and again. None of this exists without technology, confinement and industry. The argument borrows the image of the ancestral hunter to defend a practice he wouldn't recognize.

    4. Nature also teaches the opposite.

    If the species' past defined what is legitimate, the plants our ancestors ate would count just as much, and so would cooperation, empathy and caring for the vulnerable, which are also part of our evolution. Choosing only the part that confirms the habit is convenient selection.

    5. Today there's an alternative.

    The argument ignores the change of context. For anyone with access to a varied diet, meat is no longer necessary: the US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics considers well-planned vegan diets healthy and appropriate for all stages of life.

    6. Research shows what the argument is for.

    Piazza and colleagues found, in 2015, that "natural" is one of the four justifications most used for eating meat, along with normal, necessary and nice. Together they covered between 83% and 91% of the justifications participants gave, and those who most agreed with them felt less guilt and gave animals less moral consideration. The argument works less as a reason and more as relief.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: it's not nature, it's necessity and adaptation

    The most serious version drops "natural is good" and says something more modest: we're omnivores, the human body is adapted to a varied diet, and asking people to give up something so rooted in biology and culture would be asking too much. Nature doesn't dictate morality, but it defines what is reasonable to expect of a human being.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It changes the argument.

    It stops being C1 and becomes about necessity (family G) and difficulty (family J). Both questions are empirical, and the answers are in those chapters.

    Adaptation explains; it doesn't justify.

    We're also adapted to aggression, favoring our own group and fearing strangers, and we expect people to control those.

    What's reasonable to expect has changed.

    Asking a hunter of the past to stop hunting would have been asking too much. Asking someone at the supermarket to choose beans instead of chicken isn't.

    Difficulty doesn't change the harm.

    Something being hard to give up may lessen the blame of those who haven't changed yet. It doesn't make what's done to the animals acceptable.

Contradictions

Uses and approves of what isn't natural.

Medicine, vaccines, glasses, surgery and the internet the argument is posted on.

Rejects what's natural when it's inconvenient.

No one defends infanticide or might makes right on the basis of nature (see C6).

Eats meat that's anything but natural.

Animals genetically selected, medicated, confined and slaughtered on an industrial scale.

Values reason over instinct in everything else.

Controlling impulses is seen as a sign of maturity, except when the impulse is eating meat.

Accepts that context changes what's reasonable.

No one lives like their ancestors in housing, medicine or transport, and only in food does the past become a rule.

What the argument gets right

What is true

There are truths here. Humans are omnivores and can digest meat, and eating animal foods played a role in our species' history. It's also true that habits rooted in biology and culture are hard to change, and that this deserves understanding, not contempt.

But

The mistake is treating that history as permission. Nature explains where we came from; it doesn't decide where we should go. The same species that hunted learned to care for those who can't defend themselves, and that is also part of our nature.
