J13

"Eating out, traveling and parties become impossible"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the social cost exists, but it's usually exaggerated. Vegan options in restaurants, delivery apps and supermarkets grow every year, and adapting is quick: you learn what to order, what to bring and how to talk about it. Parties and gatherings carry on, with one more dish on the table. A social inconvenience, however real, doesn't outweigh an animal's life.

Short answer

The social cost exists, but it's usually smaller than it seems and shrinks over time. Vegan options in restaurants, delivery apps and supermarkets grow every year. At parties you can bring a dish for everyone; when traveling you can look things up in advance. Family and friends usually adapt. People with allergies and religious restrictions do this all the time.

For children

When a child is allergic to peanuts, they still go to parties: they bring a snack, tell their friends, and everyone has fun. It's similar for someone who's vegan. At first you need to get a bit organized, but then it gets easy. And real friends like you for who you are, not for what you eat.

Ask a question back

"Do people with allergies or religious restrictions stop going to parties and traveling?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"What about dinner at my in-laws'? I'll be isolated."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Eating is a social activity, tied to gatherings, parties and travel.
  2. P2Dietary restrictions make taking part harder and can cause conflict.
  3. P3Social isolation is a real cost to wellbeing.
  4. CSo veganism imposes too high a social cost.

P1, P2 and P3 are true to some extent. The problem lies in the conclusion: the cost is usually smaller than imagined and shrinks over time.

The argument is valid if the cost were high and permanent. Its soundness fails because it tends to be moderate and temporary.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument imagines the worst case:

    SituationWhat usually happens
    RestaurantsMany have vegan or adaptable options
    PartiesBringing a dish solves it and often pleases everyone
    TravelResearch in advance and apps make it easier
    FamilyInitial resistance usually fades over time
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The cost is real, but smaller than imagined.

    Most of the difficulties come at the start.

    2. The options are growing.

    Restaurants, delivery apps and supermarkets offer more and more vegan options.

    3. Other people do the same.

    People with allergies, celiac disease, religious restrictions or medical diets live with dietary restrictions and keep socializing.

    4. The social cost shrinks over time.

    Family and friends get used to it, and the person learns to handle the situations.

    5. Inconvenience doesn't justify harm.

    Even if real, social discomfort doesn't outweigh an animal's life.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Faunalytics, study of current and former vegetarians and vegans13% of those who quit cited social issuesShows the social cost is real for some, and that support matters
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: family conflict can be serious

    The most serious version points out that, for some people, family resistance is strong and lasting, and that conflict at home can greatly affect wellbeing.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    That calls for patience and strategy.

    Not for giving up.

    Many families change over time.

    Sometimes the vegan person inspires others.

    Veganism is "as far as is possible".

    Those facing extreme situations may need flexibility (D3).

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The social cost is real, but it's a cost to the person who changes, not a moral reason to carry on. The question remains: does the discomfort of being different at a party justify an animal's death?

Biases

Social conformity

Standing out from the group feels threatening

Classic social psychology literature

Status quo bias

Current social life seems the only possible one

Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988

Bandwagon effect

Following the group's menu seems the only way to belong

Questão de Ética, vol. X, ch. 1

Loss aversion

Losing the familiar dish weighs more than gaining others

Contradictions

Such as allergies or religion.

Such as moving city or job.

Values family.

And families usually adapt.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The social cost is real, and it shouldn't be played down. Eating is a social act, and standing apart from it can cause conflict and loneliness, especially at first. Those going through this deserve support, not judgment.

But

But the cost is usually smaller and more temporary than imagined, and it shrinks with time and with growing options. It isn't a reason to keep harming animals.
