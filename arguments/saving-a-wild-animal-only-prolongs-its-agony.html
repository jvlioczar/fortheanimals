N11

"Saving a wild animal only prolongs its agony"

The answer in one sentence

the objection starts from a truth, that the lives of many animals in nature are short and hard, and draws the wrong conclusion from it. In most cases, what makes an animal suffer is the same thing that kills it, such as hunger and disease. Treating that cause prolongs life and, at the same time, removes the suffering. Living longer also raises the chance of having a life worth living. And part of the help doesn't prolong any life at all: it only prevents births that would end in early death.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Reply

Notice: almost always, what makes an animal suffer is what kills it: hunger, thirst, disease, injury. Treating that doesn't stretch out the suffering; it ends it. And many animals live badly precisely because they die young; living longer raises the chance of a good life. You're right about one thing: badly done help can indeed prolong agony, which is why it must be well thought out, not abandoned.

For children

If a little bird is hungry and we give it food, it doesn't get more time to feel hungry: it stops feeling hungry. Really helping means taking away what's hurting it, not just making the animal last longer.

Ask a question back

"When a hungry person is given food, would you say that prolongs their hunger?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"If life out there is so bad, saving the animal just gives it more time to suffer. Better to let nature take its course."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Prolonging a life that contains more suffering than wellbeing is worse for the one living it.
  2. P2The lives of most animals in nature contain more suffering than wellbeing.
  3. P3Helping a wild animal consists in prolonging its life.
  4. CSo helping wild animals is, in general, worse for them.

P1 is acceptable. P2 is the very thesis of many who defend helping, which gives the objection rhetorical force: it seems to turn their argument against them. The weak point is P3, together with the assumption that the bad life would stay bad after the help.

The argument is valid, but P3 is false as a general rule, and the conclusion confuses "prolonging life" with "prolonging suffering".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The objection treats three kinds of help with different effects as if they were the same:

    Kind of helpEffect on lifeEffect on suffering
    Treating a cause of suffering that also kills (hunger, disease)ProlongsReduces
    Prolonging life without treating another source of sufferingProlongsMay increase
    Preventing births (contraception)Doesn't prolongPrevents future early deaths

    The argument only works for the second kind. And that is precisely the kind of help a serious proposal avoids.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. What makes them suffer is what kills them.

    Starvation, dehydration, infections and parasitic diseases cause suffering and death at the same time. When the cause is treated, the animal lives longer and stops suffering from it. Cases where an ill causes suffering without leading to death exist, but they are the minority (Cunha, 2022, pp. 260–261).

    2. Dying young is part of the problem, not the solution.

    In species with many offspring, most die soon after birth. That is largely why suffering weighs so heavily in these lives: there's no time for anything else. A longer life, with the causes of suffering treated, has a better chance of a positive balance (Cunha, 2022, p. 261).

    3. Not all animals live badly.

    In species with few offspring and much parental care, such as elephants, zebras and deer, the chance of a reasonable life is greater. Water, food, vaccines and treatment raise that chance (Cunha, 2022, p. 260).

    4. Part of the help prolongs no life at all.

    Wildlife contraception is already practised, and it prevents the birth of young that would die soon afterwards (N19). If the problem is that too many animals are born only to die early, this help goes straight to the cause.

    5. The human test.

    No one says a child with malaria in a poor region shouldn't be treated because they'll stay poor. We treat the disease and fight poverty. The "saving only prolongs it" reasoning is never used against humanitarian aid; it's used only when the victims are animals.

    6. Half-hearted help can make things worse, and that's an argument for research.

    The philosopher Catia Faria gives the example of an animal with parasites: feeding it without treating the parasites may prolong its suffering (Faria, 2016, p. 107, cited by Cunha, 2022, p. 260). The answer is to help thoroughly and on the basis of study, not to stop helping.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: then killing would be better

    The most serious version drops the idea that saving prolongs the agony of the animal itself and turns to its offspring. A rescued animal of a species with hundreds of young will reproduce, and most of those young will die early. If the goal is to reduce suffering, killing animals, or letting them die, would reduce the number of births and therefore of early deaths. Either the proposal accepts this, and looks monstrous, or it refuses, and looks inconsistent.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Saving needn't increase reproduction.

    With dogs and cats, we don't say they shouldn't be saved because they might have litters. We save them and neuter them. In a scenario where helping wild animals were taken seriously, the same care about reproduction would come with it (Cunha, 2022, pp. 278–279).

    Killing doesn't guarantee fewer births.

    If the space left by killed animals is taken by others with even more offspring, the result may be worse (Cunha, 2022, p. 279).

    The effects on people weigh heavily.

    Wild animals are already killed for environmental reasons or human interests, and those reasons assume that animals matter little. If animal advocates approve of killing, they reinforce that view and blur the proposal with what it criticizes. That's why even those who look only at consequences have reasons for a general rule against killing (Cunha, 2022, pp. 279–280).

    The debate is real.

    Some suffering-focused authors, such as Brian Tomasik, see habitat reduction as something that may lower total suffering, and others reject any intervention, on one side or the other. Cunha's position, against killing and in favour of research and preventing births, is one among several; sterilization without consent is itself contested by those who see it as an echo of eugenic policies. What the counter-reply doesn't show is that killing is the only consistent way out.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Hasty generalization
Takes the case of incomplete help as if it were all help
Equivocation
Treats "prolonging life" as if it were "prolonging suffering"

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Double standard
Never uses the argument against helping poor or sick humans

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Unfounded certainty
Claims to know that helping always makes things worse, on a topic where the proposal itself calls for research

Biases

Omission bias

Letting die seems more acceptable than acting, even with a worse outcome

Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991

Status quo bias

The natural course of things is taken as a starting point that needs no justification

Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988

In-group bias

The reasoning is accepted for animals and rejected for humans

Caviola, Everett and Faber, 2019

Contradictions

Supports humanitarian aid in poor regions.

Without thinking that treating a disease only prolongs poverty.

Thinks it's right to take an injured animal to the vet.

Which is already prolonging a life in order to end suffering.

Neuters their own cat instead of letting it die.

Which is exactly the combination of saving and preventing births.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that prolonging a life without treating what makes it suffer can make things worse, and that there are cases where euthanasia is the kindest choice: wildlife rehabilitation centres adopt it when an animal has no prospect of a reasonable life. It's also right that, in species with many offspring, saving individuals without thinking about reproduction may increase the number of deaths.

But

But that calls for better help, not for no help. Treating the cause of suffering, attending to reproduction and researching before acting at scale answers what the objection gets right.
