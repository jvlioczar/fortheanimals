L4

"Sentientists are even crazier than vegans"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

finding an idea strange does not show it is wrong, nor that those who hold it are unbalanced. It is a label that spares us from examining the argument, a relative of L3, and it recurs every time a movement widens the circle of who counts. Some sentientist proposals may be speculative and debatable, and vegans themselves disagree with some of them, which deserves serious debate. But the fair test is another one: take the same scenario and apply it to human beings, and see whether the answer would be accepted.

Short answer

Finding something strange is not the same as it being wrong. Many ideas that seem obvious today were once treated as crazy, and those who defended them were called radical. What matters is the argument. If some sentientist proposal seems exaggerated, let's discuss it: what is the premise, and what happens if we apply the same reasoning to humans? Many people who agree with veganism disagree with part of sentientism, and that is legitimate. What is not legitimate is replacing the discussion with a label.

For children

When someone has an idea we have never heard, it's easy to think it's silly. But new ideas can be right too. A long time ago, people thought it was strange that everyone should have the same rights. The way to find out is to listen to the reason for the idea and see whether it makes sense, not to laugh at whoever had it.

Ask a question back

"Which specific part do you think is wrong, and why? If it were the same case with people, would you think the same?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Vegans are already radical, but sentientists are worse: they want to save even insects, even a gazelle from a lion. That's crazy."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Some sentientist proposals, such as helping wild animals, considering insects or taking digital minds into account, stray far from common sense.
  2. P2When an idea strays far from common sense, it is a sign that something went wrong in the reasoning.
  3. P3Those who hold these ideas are not reasoning well.
  4. CSo sentientists are even more extreme than vegans, and it isn't worth taking them seriously.

P1 is true: some proposals really are unusual. P2 has some truth in it: how strange a conclusion seems can signal that the premises are worth revisiting. The problem lies in P3 and in the conclusion: strangeness is a weak sign that does not replace analysis, and the attack moves from the idea to the person. The argument is invalid.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    Three things are mixed in the sentence:

    PartWhat it does
    "Sentientists are even more radical"Compares groups by degree of strangeness
    "Crazy"Replaces the argument with a label about the person
    "Not worth taking seriously"Uses strangeness to dispense with examination
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Strangeness is not error.

    The strangeness of a conclusion is a sign, not a proof. Many conclusions that seem obvious today seemed absurd when first proposed. When someone shows that a premise is flawed, that is an argument. Saying the conclusion sounds odd is not.

    2. It is an attack on the person, and the same pattern applies to every movement.

    In 1963, religious leaders called Martin Luther King's actions "unwise and untimely", and he answered, in the Letter from Birmingham Jail, the label of extremist as well. Those who question customs are often called radical. That does not show they are right, but it does not show they are wrong either.

    3. The same scenario applied to humans.

    Helping the victims of a natural disaster, an illness or an accident is not crazy. Extending that to animals who suffer for the same reasons is applying the same principle, not a delusion: what needs justification is treating differently someone who feels in the same way. The field's proposals, such as researching suffering in nature, vaccinating and rescuing, are careful, and most organizations ask for caution and study (N11).

    4. Calling someone "crazy" is a problem of its own.

    Using a mental disorder as an insult reinforces stigma, and the attack doesn't answer the argument (L3). Nor does it work as a criticism of sentientists that some vegans disagree with them: the movement's internal debate exists, and it is healthy.

    5. Some proposals do deserve criticism.

    There are, indeed, speculative sentientist ideas, and the family note recalls that many vegans disagree with parts of them. Good criticism goes to specific points, such as the moral weight of insects (N14), parasitism (N13) or intervention in nature (N6), and does without the label.

    6. Strangeness and common sense are historical yardsticks.

    Sentientism is a consistent application of a criterion most people already accept for humans: what matters is who can feel. Movements for justice, such as abolitionism and suffragism, were called radical in their time. The common sense of an era measures the custom of that moment, not the truth.

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: when reasoning leads to absurd conclusions, there is an error

    The most serious version says: in philosophy, it is common to reject a premise when it leads to a clearly absurd conclusion. If sentientism leads to helping insects or caring for parasites, perhaps the error lies in the principle.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Absurdity has to be shown.

    Strangeness is a starting point, and the next step is to say why the conclusion is absurd, not just that it seems so.

    The same rule would bring down other causes.

    If any strange conclusion refuted the principle, the historical struggles for equality would have been refuted before they began.

    The burden stays on both sides.

    Whoever says something is absurd must also explain which moral reason distinguishes the human case from the animal case.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The moral question isn't who is more "crazy" or who seems more radical. It is whether there is a good enough reason to disregard the suffering of those who feel. That question applies equally to vegans, sentientists and those who disagree with them.

Contradictions

Accepts strange ideas from the past, but rejects those of the present.

The same strangeness was said of causes that are defended today.

Demands arguments from sentientists, but answers with a label.

Says they are even worse than vegans, but the criticism is always of the group, never of a proposal.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It is right that some sentientist proposals are unusual, and that distance from common sense calls for care and good reasons. It is right that the internal debate is legitimate: many vegans disagree with parts of these ideas, and it is worth discussing, point by point, what they imply.

But

But this shows that the proposals need to be debated, not that those who hold them are unbalanced. The criticism that helps is the one that points to the flawed premise, not the one that gives a label.
