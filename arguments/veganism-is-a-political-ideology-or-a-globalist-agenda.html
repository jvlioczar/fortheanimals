J18

"Veganism is a political ideology or a globalist agenda"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the argument assesses no reason at all; it just associates veganism with something the person rejects. It's guilt by association. Veganism has supporters on the left, on the right and outside politics, and it rests on a simple question: is it fair to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to? Conspiracy theories about "making us eat bugs" don't answer that question.

Short answer

Saying an idea is "leftist" or "globalist" doesn't show it's wrong; it's guilt by association. Veganism has supporters on the left, on the right and outside politics. Its question is simple: is it fair to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to? No one is proposing to force anyone to eat insects. Disagreeing with governments or organizations doesn't answer the question about the animals.

For children

Sometimes, when we don't like an idea, we say it's "something people we don't like believe". But that doesn't show whether the idea is right or wrong. Veganism's question is simple: is it fair to hurt animals when we don't need to? People of every kind can answer that question.

Ask a question back

"If someone you like defended veganism, would the answer to the question about the animals change?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"It's a leftist thing. They want us to eat bugs. They want to control what you eat."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Some international organizations and governments propose reducing meat consumption.
  2. P2These proposals are part of broader political agendas I disagree with.
  3. P3Those who promote veganism are, knowingly or not, serving these agendas.
  4. CSo veganism should be rejected as a political instrument.

P1 is true in some cases. The problem lies in P3 and the conclusion: an idea's origin or allies say nothing about whether it's correct.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, and the argument is a genetic fallacy and guilt by association.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument swaps assessing an idea for assessing its supposed allies:

    What the argument doesWhat it should do
    Associates veganism with rejected groupsAssess veganism's reasons
    Attributes hidden intentionsExamine the facts about the animals
    Rejects by originJudge by content
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's guilt by association.

    An idea doesn't become wrong because people we dislike defend it.

    2. It's the genetic fallacy.

    An idea's origin doesn't determine its truth.

    3. Veganism cuts across parties.

    There are vegans of many political positions, and concern for animals appears in very different religious, philosophical and cultural traditions.

    4. The conspiracy theories have no basis.

    There's no proposal to force populations to eat insects. There is research on alternative protein sources, which is something else.

    5. There are right-wing vegans, and animal protection laws passed by conservative governments.

    On average, people further to the right eat more meat and give up vegetarianism more often, but the same research shows there are conservative vegetarians and vegans. In the United Kingdom, the law recognizing animals as sentient beings was passed in 2022 by a Conservative government. The Political objections page shows that neither liberalism, nor libertarianism, nor left-wing theories offer, on their own terms, a reason to exclude animals.

    6. There's no imposition under way.

    No government is forcing anyone to be vegan. Arguing that exploiting animals is wrong isn't the same as imposing a law, and the debate about what the state should do is a different one (E2).

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Hodson and Earle, 2018People further to the right eat more meat and are more likely to lapse from vegetarianism, but the sample includes conservative vegetarians and vegansAppetite, 120
    United Kingdom, Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022Recognizes vertebrate animals, octopuses, squid, crabs and lobsters as sentient beingsPassed under a Conservative government
    Pew Research Center, diet in India (2021)39% of Indian adults describe themselves as vegetarian, largely for religious reasonsShows that refusing meat doesn't belong to one political camp
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: there are real public policies against meat

    The most serious version points out that governments and organizations discuss targets for reducing meat consumption, and that being wary of those policies is legitimate.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Being wary of policies is different from rejecting the moral question.

    One can disagree with a policy and still stop eating animals.

    Individual choice doesn't depend on policy.

    No one needs a government to change their own plate.

    The question about the animals remains.

    No political wariness answers it.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument shifts the focus from the animals to politics. But the pig, the hen and the cow belong to no party. The moral question is the same for anyone, of any persuasion: is it fair to make them suffer and die when we don't need to?

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's legitimate to be wary of policies imposed from above, and some proposals for dietary change can be poorly communicated or authoritarian. It's also true that the subject has been politicized, and that vegans sometimes reinforce that polarization.

But

But the question about animals comes before party politics. Anyone can examine it, and its answer doesn't depend on who asks it.
