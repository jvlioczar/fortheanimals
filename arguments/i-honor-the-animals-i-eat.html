C13

"I honor the animals I eat"

The answer in one sentence

gratitude is a sincere feeling in the person who eats, but it changes nothing for the animal: they lose their life all the same. Honoring someone usually means respecting their interests, and an animal's greatest interest is to go on living without suffering. When eating animals isn't necessary to survive, the clearest way to honor them is not to have them killed. Many Indigenous traditions thank the hunted animal in subsistence settings, and that deserves respect; but meat bought at the supermarket comes from anonymous animals, bred and slaughtered on an industrial scale.

Short answer

The gratitude is sincere, but it changes nothing for the animal: they lose their life all the same. Honoring someone usually means respecting what matters to them, and what matters most to an animal is staying alive and not suffering. When we don't need to eat animals to live, the clearest way to honor them is not to have them killed.

For children

Saying thank you is lovely. But if someone took your favorite toy and then said "thank you very much", the toy would still be gone. For the animal, a thank-you doesn't give their life back. The best way to show you care about them is to let them live.

Ask a question back

"If the animal could choose, would they prefer to be thanked or to stay alive?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I don't eat on autopilot. I thank the animal before every meal and waste nothing. I honor the animals I eat."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1We are part of nature, and every living thing feeds on others.
  2. P2The moral problem lies in waste, indifference and cruelty, not in the act of eating.
  3. P3Someone who eats with gratitude, without waste, recognizes the animal's value.
  4. CSo eating animals with that attitude is morally acceptable.

P1 is true, but doesn't say what we should do (see C7). P2 is the decisive premise and is false: for the animal, the main problem is being killed. P3 describes the attitude of the one who eats, not what happens to the one who is eaten.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    What the argument changesFor whomWhat stays the same
    The attitude of the one who eatsFor the one who eatsThe animal's death
    WasteFor the environment, in partBreeding, transport and slaughter
    Awareness of where food comes fromFor the one who eatsWhat the animal loses
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Gratitude lives in the one who eats, not in the one who is eaten.

    The feeling can be real and deep, but it doesn't reach the animal. They were bred, transported and slaughtered the same way.

    2. Honoring means respecting interests.

    When we say we honor a person, we mean we take seriously what matters to them. Killing them and then thanking them wouldn't be honoring them. The same holds for an animal who has an interest in living.

    3. Not wasting is good, but doesn't solve the main thing.

    Using the whole animal reduces waste, but doesn't reduce the number of animals killed.

    4. Context matters.

    In many Indigenous traditions, thanking the hunted animal is part of a subsistence relationship without easy alternatives, and those traditions deserve respect (see D7). Someone who buys meat at the supermarket, with alternatives available, is in a very different situation: the animal came from an industrial, anonymous supply chain.

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: indifference is the real evil

    The most serious version says the problem with modern animal farming is turning animals into things, and that eating mindfully and with gratitude restores a lost relationship of respect.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Indifference is a problem, but not the only one.

    An animal killed with respect is still dead.

    Awareness leads to a question.

    Someone who truly thinks about the animal ends up asking whether they need to kill them.

    Respect shows in what we do.

    If the attitude changes nothing for the animal, the respect stays in words.

Contradictions

And takes from them what they have most to lose.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that indifference toward animals is a problem, that wasting is worse than using, that thinking about where food comes from is better than eating on autopilot, and that subsistence traditions that thank the animal deserve respect.

But

But the gratitude of the one who eats changes nothing for the one who is eaten. When killing isn't necessary, the clearest way to honor an animal is to let them live.
