M3

"Being vegan limited me when eating at my relatives' homes"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the experience of someone who tried to be vegan and gave up, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism depended on being easy for everyone, one person's difficulty would be enough to make it stop counting. But someone's difficulty, which deserves to be heard and helped, doesn't change what happens to the animals: it calls for practical support, not a new moral conclusion.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the discomfort of eating at family homes is one of the most real costs of veganism, because it touches on affection, tradition and belonging. But it has well-known solutions: bringing a dish to share, talking beforehand, eating whatever on the table is vegan. In the Faunalytics survey, 13% of former vegetarians and vegans cited social issues as a reason for giving up, and 63% didn't like standing out. Awkwardness at the table, however real, doesn't outweigh an animal's life.

Short answer

This is one of the most real costs, because it involves affection, not just food. But there are ways out: letting people know beforehand, bringing a dish for everyone and eating what's already vegan on the table, like rice, beans and salad. Many families adapt over time, especially when they realize the person isn't rejecting anyone. The discomfort of a few meals doesn't outweigh the lives of animals.

For children

At grandma's house, food is a way of showing love. Someone who doesn't eat meat can bring a tasty dish for everyone to try, and explain kindly that they don't eat animals because animals feel pain. That way, the family stays together, and no one has to hurt an animal.

Ask a question back

"If you brought a dish to share, would your family turn it down?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Being vegan limited me. Every time I went to my mom's or my grandma's, it was a problem. They got upset, and I ate almost nothing."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Shared food is an important part of family bonds.
  2. P2Refusing the family's food causes hurt and distance.
  3. P3Family bonds are an important good, worth protecting.
  4. CSo it's reasonable to leave veganism to preserve family life.

P1, P2 and P3 are largely true. The problem lies in the conclusion: it assumes there are only two ways out, refusing everything or eating everything, and that the good of family bonds outweighs the lives of the animals served.

The argument is valid, but not sound: there are other ways to preserve family life, and the cost to the animals is left out of the account.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument imagines only two paths, when there are several:

    PathEffect on the familyEffect on the animals
    Refusing everything, without talkingHurtSpares animals
    Eating everything so as not to hurt anyoneNo conflictDoesn't spare them
    Talking beforehand and bringing a dishLittle or no conflictSpares animals
    Eating whatever on the table is veganLittle conflictSpares animals
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's the food being refused, not the person.

    Much of the hurt comes from the family feeling that their affection was rejected. Explaining calmly, praising what you do eat and thanking them for the invitation changes the conversation a lot.

    2. Bringing a dish changes the dynamic.

    Instead of arriving with a restriction, the person arrives with a contribution. Many families end up trying it and even asking for the recipe.

    3. Many family tables already have plenty that's vegan.

    Rice, beans cooked without meat, side dishes, salad, vegetables, fruit. In many cases, it's enough to ask for some of the beans to be set aside before the bacon goes in.

    4. Other restrictions are already respected.

    Families adapt to relatives with diabetes, allergies, celiac disease or religious restrictions. Adapting to veganism is the same kind of thing.

    5. The social cost is real, but it usually shrinks.

    Over time, the family gets used to it, and the person also learns what to do. In the Faunalytics survey, 34% of former vegetarians and vegans gave up in the first three months, precisely when these situations are still new.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Faunalytics, 2014Social issues were the reason for giving up for 13%; 63% didn't like standing out; 34% gave up within three monthsSurvey conducted in 2014 in the United States
    MacInnis and Hodson, 2017Omnivores rated vegetarians and vegans as negatively as, or more negatively than, several groups that are common targets of prejudiceHelps explain why the pressure is strong
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: family matters more than a food choice

    The most serious version says: family bonds are one of the most important goods in life. A food choice shouldn't cost someone their relationship with their mother or grandmother.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    You rarely have to choose.

    There's almost always a way to keep family life going without eating animals. The choice between family and veganism is usually a false one.

    It isn't just a food choice.

    There are others affected: the animals. The fair comparison isn't between family and a taste, but between the comfort of a few meals and the lives of animals.

    Giving in on everything doesn't protect the family.

    Strong relationships can handle differences. Hiding your own convictions to avoid conflict also has a cost.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Even if eating at family homes became harder, the question would remain the same: is it just to make animals suffer and die to avoid discomfort at the table? The family's affection is real, and it can be kept without that.

Contradictions

The family adapts to other restrictions.

Diabetes, allergies, religion.

The person themselves thought animals mattered.

So much that they became vegan.

Says family is what matters most.

But accepts the idea that the family wouldn't tolerate a difference.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that food is part of affection, that refusing it can hurt and that family bonds matter a great deal. It's also right that many vegans don't know how to handle these situations and end up creating unnecessary conflicts.

But

But the problem has a solution: talking, a dish to share and patience. The good answer to someone who went through this is to share strategies, not to judge.
