N6

"Meddling with nature is wrong; we could make everything worse"

The answer in one sentence

the risk of making things worse is real, and that's exactly why the serious proposal isn't to start intervening, but to study first: this is what's called welfare biology. Medicine can also make things worse, and the response was to create research, trials and caution, not to abandon the sick. Besides, we already meddle with nature all the time, for ourselves; what's missing is taking into account the animals that live in it.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

The risk of making things worse is real. That's why no serious person proposes meddling with ecosystems at random: they propose studying first, testing on a small scale, and only expanding what works. It's what medicine does with risky treatments. And we already meddle with nature all the time for ourselves; the question is whether the animals living in it count too.

For children

When a doctor isn't sure which medicine will work, they don't leave the person untreated: they study, run tests and try carefully. It's the same with animals in nature. Scientists need to study a lot before helping, so as not to make things worse. But studying is already a way to start helping.

Ask a question back

"Since treatments can go wrong, should we close the hospitals, or should we research so we get it wrong less often?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Every time humans meddle with nature, it goes wrong. Let nature take care of itself."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Ecosystems are complex systems, with knock-on effects that are hard to predict.
  2. P2Human interventions in ecosystems have already caused disasters, such as introducing species that became pests.
  3. P3An intervention to reduce suffering could end up increasing it.
  4. CSo we shouldn't intervene in nature to help animals.

P1, P2 and P3 are true and need to be taken seriously. The problem is the conclusion: from the risk of getting it wrong, what follows isn't a ban on acting, but a requirement to act with knowledge.

The argument is invalid: the correct conclusion from the premises is "we shouldn't intervene without study", not "we should never intervene".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument starts from a truth and draws too big a conclusion from it:

    What is trueWhat doesn't follow
    Poorly planned interventions can make everything worseEvery intervention will make everything worse
    Ecosystems are complexThe complexity can never be studied
    We should be cautiousWe should stand still forever

    The proposal of welfare biology, suggested by the economist Yew-Kwang Ng in 1995, is precisely to answer risk with knowledge: studying the wellbeing of wild animals and the effects of interventions before scaling them up.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Risk calls for study, not omission.

    In every field where acting is risky, such as medicine, engineering or public policy, the response was to create methods for getting it wrong less: research, trials, small and reversible interventions.

    2. We already meddle with nature, a lot.

    About half of the planet's habitable land is used for agriculture. We dam rivers, build cities, plant forests, reintroduce and eliminate species. We do this for human interests or environmental goals. What rarely enters the calculation is the wellbeing of the animals affected.

    3. Not intervening also has consequences.

    Omission isn't neutral: it lets existing suffering continue. Comparing "intervening" with "doing nothing" as if the second option had no cost is an accounting error.

    4. The human test.

    When a human population faces an epidemic, we know interventions can have unexpected effects. The response is to study and act carefully, not to let the epidemic run its course because "meddling makes it worse". Applying the rule of omission only to animals is deciding by species.

    5. Some kinds of help have low risk and known effects.

    Rescuing animals in floods, treating the injured and vaccinating against specific diseases are limited, studied interventions that are already practised.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: we'll never know enough

    The most serious version says: ecosystems are so complex that we'll never have enough knowledge to predict the consequences. Humility calls for not intervening.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Never knowing everything isn't the same as never knowing anything.

    In medicine and ecology we act on partial knowledge all the time, and knowledge grows precisely through research and controlled experience.

    Humility cuts both ways.

    We also don't know whether omission is the best option. Assuming not acting is always safe is a form of overconfidence.

    Small interventions teach.

    Starting with what is local, reversible and observable lets us learn without gambling whole ecosystems.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Hasty generalization
From some disasters it concludes every intervention goes wrong
False dilemma
Either large-scale intervention without study, or nothing

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Omission bias
Treats not acting as a cost-free option

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Double standard
Accepts meddling with nature for human interests, but not for the animals

Biases

Omission bias

Harms from not acting seem less serious

Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991

Status quo bias

The current state of nature seems the safe default

Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988

Appeal to nature

The natural seems balanced and good

Linked to C1 and N3

Ambiguity effect

An intervention with an uncertain outcome is rejected in favor of not acting, whose bad outcome is already known

Ellsberg, 1961

Extrapolations

Contradictions

Accepts farming, cities and dams.

Large interventions in nature, made by us.

Supports environmental programmes that kill animals.

Such as controlling invasive species, which are also interventions (N3).

Trusts medicine.

Which is also risky and advances by studying its own mistakes.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets a lot right: ecosystems are complex, interventions have caused disasters, and anyone who wants to help wild animals needs humility and method. Grand, hasty proposals would be irresponsible.

But

But risk is a reason to study, not to give up. And if we accept meddling with nature for so many human reasons, there's no reason to exclude precisely the good of the animals that live in it.
