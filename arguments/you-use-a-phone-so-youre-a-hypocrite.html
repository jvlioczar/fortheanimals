L11

"You use a phone, so you're a hypocrite"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the inconsistency of someone defending an idea doesn't make the idea false. The argument attacks the person and leaves the question untouched. Besides, veganism never promised purity: its own definition speaks of avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable". Doing what's possible in an imperfect world isn't hypocrisy; hypocrisy would be demanding of others what you don't even try to do.

Short answer

Even if I were inconsistent, that wouldn't make what I'm saying false: the animal suffers just the same, whoever points it out. And veganism never promised purity: the Vegan Society's definition speaks of avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable". There's no phone today without impact, but there is a lunch without animals. Doing what's possible isn't hypocrisy; it's what we expect of anyone with a principle.

For children

Imagine a classmate says "you shouldn't drop litter", and the next day accidentally drops a piece of paper. Does that mean dropping litter has become right? No. The dropped paper shows they make mistakes sometimes, like everyone, but the rule is still right. It's the same with animals: no one can be perfect, but that doesn't make it right to hurt them.

Ask a question back

"If I were perfect, would you stop eating meat?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"You use a phone made with minerals mined by children, you drive a car, and you eat plants harvested by machines that kill animals. What right do you have to lecture me?"

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Whoever asserts a moral principle should follow it consistently.
  2. P2Vegans also take part in practices that harm animals and humans.
  3. P3Whoever doesn't follow their own principle has no authority to demand it of others.
  4. CSo vegan criticism needn't be taken seriously.

P2 is partly true: no one lives without causing some harm in a complex economy. The problem lies in P3 and the conclusion.

The argument is invalid in the form it's usually used. From the inconsistency of the speaker it doesn't follow that what they say is false. This is the classic error of tu quoque ("you too").

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument shifts the subject from the question to the person. There are two different questions:

    QuestionAbout whatWhat the tu quoque answers
    Is eating animals wrong?The practiceNothing
    Is the critic consistent?The personPerhaps not, but that doesn't change the first answer

    Tu quoque is a form of ad hominem: instead of assessing the argument, it points to the conduct of whoever makes it. A doctor who smokes and says smoking is harmful is right, though inconsistent.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The truth doesn't depend on who says it.

    If eating animals causes avoidable harm, that's true whether the criticism comes from a perfect vegan, an inconsistent one or someone who still eats meat. The messenger's conduct doesn't change the animal's condition.

    2. Veganism doesn't promise purity.

    The Vegan Society's definition, in use since 1988, describes veganism as a philosophy that seeks to exclude, "as far as is possible and practicable", all forms of exploitation of and cruelty to animals. The criterion was never perfection, but possible effort.

    3. Possible and practicable aren't the same for everything.

    For most people today there's no phone free of minerals from problematic supply chains: in 2016, Amnesty International documented child labor in mining the cobalt used in phone batteries. But for most people there is a meal without animals. Comparing a choice without an alternative to a choice with one is a false equivalence.

    4. Hypocrisy is something else.

    A hypocrite is someone who demands of others what they don't try to do, or pretends to follow a principle they don't. Someone who recognizes their limits and does what they can is being consistent with a realistic principle, not hypocritical.

    5. Taken seriously, the argument silences everyone.

    Since no one is perfectly consistent, no one could criticize any injustice. Those who use the tu quoque against vegans also use phones, and still criticize other things.

    6. Where the objection holds, it asks for more, not less.

    If the problem with the phone is child labor, the consistent conclusion is to care about that too, not to stop caring about animals. The argument uses one harm to justify another.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: it's not about logic, it's about credibility

    The most serious version grants that the tu quoque refutes nothing. But it says credibility matters in practice: whoever preaches what they don't practice loses listeners' trust, and a movement that asks a lot of others should be exemplary. Besides, vegans sometimes talk as if they were free of all harm, and it's fair to point out when that isn't true.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Credibility affects persuasion, not truth.

    It may be harder to listen to someone inconsistent, but what they say is still true or false on its own merits.

    The criticism of purity talk is right, and has already been made.

    Vegans who present themselves as perfect are wrong, and this guide says so in the bad arguments vegans use. But correcting the rhetoric doesn't change the question about the animals.

    Demanding a perfect example is demanding the impossible.

    No justice movement would ever have started if only perfect people could speak.

    The counter-reply applies to whoever uses it.

    If inconsistency removes the standing to criticize, someone who eats meat and says they love animals (E11) loses it too.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Pointing out the speaker's inconsistency doesn't answer what was said. Even if every vegan were a hypocrite, the question would remain the same: is it fair to make animals suffer and die when we don't need to?

Contradictions

Criticizes other injustices using a phone.

People who use the argument also denounce corruption, violence or inequality, without considering themselves hypocrites.

Doesn't worry about the child labor they cite.

The phone's harm appears only as ammunition against vegans, not as a reason to change.

Accepts advice from imperfect people.

We listen to doctors who smoke, teachers who make mistakes and parents who don't follow everything they teach.

Demands perfection only of vegans.

The same standard is never applied to those who champion other causes.

Concedes the point by attacking the person.

If there were an answer to the question, there'd be no need to bring up the phone.

What the argument gets right

What is true

There's something fair here. Vegans also cause harm, and there's no way to live in a complex economy without taking part in some problematic supply chain. When someone talks as if they were above that, the criticism holds, and humility is a virtue the movement needs to cultivate. It's also true that consistency matters: whoever defends a principle should try to live it.

But

But pointing out someone's flaw doesn't answer the question. The animal suffers just the same, and no one needs to be perfect to recognize it. The consistency that can be asked for is doing what's possible, and no longer eating animals is, for most people, within reach today.
