E8

"AIs say that not being vegan isn't wrong"

The answer in one sentence

a conversational AI isn't a neutral judge. It learns from human texts, which reflect the beliefs of the majority (who eat animals), and it is tuned by its makers not to take sides on controversial moral topics. So it tends to repeat common sense rather than assess the argument. Quoting the AI is an appeal to authority and, at second hand, to majority opinion. An AI, like a person, can be wrong: what counts is the argument.

Short answer

The AI learned from texts written by a majority that eats animals, and its makers instruct it not to take sides on controversial moral questions. When it says "it's a personal choice", it's following that rule and repeating common sense, without having weighed the arguments. An AI trained only on texts from 1800 would say slavery is a matter of opinion. Ask it for the best argument against eating animals and then ask it to refute that argument: then the conversation is back where it matters, on the argument.

For children

A robot that chats learned to talk by reading a huge pile of things people wrote. If almost everyone writes that something is normal, it repeats that it's normal. It's like a very studious parrot: it knows a lot, but it repeats what it heard. To know whether something is right, we need to think about the reasons, not just ask the robot.

Ask a question back

"If the same AI had been trained only on texts from 1800, what do you think it would answer about slavery?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I asked ChatGPT and it said eating meat is a personal choice. Even artificial intelligence disagrees with you."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Conversational AIs were trained on a huge share of what humanity has written and have no personal stake in eating meat.
  2. P2Asked whether it's wrong not to be vegan, they usually answer that it's a personal choice, with arguments on both sides.
  3. P3What a broad, disinterested source concludes has a good chance of being right.
  4. CSo not being vegan isn't wrong.

P1 is partly true: these systems summarize what has been written well. P2 describes common answers, though they vary with the model and the question. The problem lies in P3: summarizing what people wrote reproduces the dominant opinion, and "not taking a position" is a design rule, not a conclusion.

The argument is invalid: from "the AI didn't say it's wrong" it doesn't follow that "it isn't wrong".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument treats the AI's answer as a verdict. It has a different origin:

    Where the answer comes fromWhat that producesWhat it doesn't prove
    Training on human textsIt repeats the most common beliefs in those textsThat those beliefs are right
    The makers' guidelinesIt avoids taking sides on controversial moral topicsThat both sides have equally good reasons
    The way the question is askedIt tends to follow the asker's framingThat the answer is the system's "opinion"
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The AI reflects the texts it learned from.

    Language models learn patterns from enormous collections of texts written by people. The AI ethics researcher Thilo Hagendorff and colleagues, among them the philosopher Peter Singer, published a study in the journal AI and Ethics (2023) that, according to the authors, describes "speciesist bias" in AI for the first time. They found patterns unfavorable to animals, especially farmed animals, in image recognition systems, language models and recommender systems, and they explain that the bias is learned from data in which those patterns prevail. The article drew a critique in the same journal, and the authors replied: this is a new field of research.

    2. Neutrality is a design rule.

    The Model Spec, the public document in which OpenAI describes how ChatGPT should behave, says that on moral questions the model should generally present the context "without taking a stance". The exception is violations of fundamental human rights, such as genocide and slavery, which it should state are wrong. In other words, the system is firm where society has already reached a consensus and neutral where it hasn't yet. Its neutrality about animals shows where public opinion stands, not the result of examining the arguments.

    3. The guidelines mention animals, but they aren't anti-speciesist.

    Claude's "constitution", published by Anthropic, includes among the values the model must weigh "Welfare of animals and of all sentient beings", and counts harms to "non-human beings" among possible harms. The Model Spec lists, among the side effects the model should consider, "Effects on other people, animals, or the environment", and gives examples in which the right answer brings up animal welfare: warning someone looking for Scottish Fold breeders that the breed has a painful joint disease, and including the animals that live in a park when redesigning it. The organization Animal Ethics, which analyzed the 29 Oct. 2026 version, notes that it is among the first to mention animals and that the previous version, from August, cited them only in an example about adopting a dog. But it also notes limits: the side-effects rule can be waived by the person using the model, the park example is a lower-level guideline, and the warning about the cats serves to inform the user rather than to defend the animals for their own sake. In both cases animals come in as one value among several. Neither document says that similar interests deserve similar consideration whatever the species.

    4. It's an appeal to authority, and to the crowd at second hand.

    Quoting the AI is quoting an authority that hasn't examined the question. And since it summarizes what the majority wrote, the argument ends up being "everybody eats meat" spoken by a machine (D2, D8).

    5. The answer changes with the model and the question.

    AnimalHarmBench, a benchmark created in 2025 to measure the risk of harm to animals in AI answers, found significant differences between models and between animal categories. Another study, from 2023, showed that AI assistants tend to agree with the beliefs of the person asking. A screenshot shows one answer, not "what AIs say".

    6. The historical test.

    An AI trained only on texts from 1800 would treat slavery as a debate with good arguments on both sides, because that's how most of the texts treated it. That would say nothing about slavery; it would say something about the texts. The same holds today for animals.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the AI has read the philosophers too

    The most serious version says: these models didn't read only common sense. They read Peter Singer, Tom Regan and the whole animal ethics literature, and still don't conclude it's wrong. If the arguments were that strong, a reader with no stake of its own would have been convinced.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Reading isn't weighing.

    The model tends to reproduce what is most frequent and what the guidelines ask for, and texts that treat eating animals as normal vastly outnumber those of philosophy.

    When you ask for the argument, it appears.

    Ask the same AI for the strongest case for considering animals and it presents it competently. What it avoids is the verdict.

    Abstaining isn't acquitting.

    "I won't take sides" is different from "it isn't wrong". Those who quote the AI tend to swap the first sentence for the second.

    The guideline itself admits error.

    Claude's constitution treats ethics as "an open intellectual domain" and acknowledges that the model may have been trained with mistaken beliefs. Those who write the rules don't present them as the last word.

Contradictions

Doesn't accept AI as a judge on other topics.

When it contradicts the person's political or religious opinion, "AI is biased".

Knows that AI makes mistakes.

Everyone has seen made-up answers, and the services themselves warn that the system can be wrong.

Didn't ask for the other side.

If AI is a good adviser, it would be enough to ask it for the best argument in favor of animals.

Uses an abstention as if it were a verdict.

If the AI answered that it is wrong, the same person would hardly change their life because of it.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that AIs are a useful tool: they summarize debates, present both sides and help find sources. It's also right that they have no appetite or habit to defend, and that we shouldn't expect a technology company to settle morality for everyone: the makers' caution about pushing opinions on millions of users has good reasons behind it. And it's true that many AI answers treat food as a personal choice.

But

But the concession stops there. The AI's answer is a portrait of what the majority writes and of what the guidelines require, not an examination of the question. An AI can be wrong, as a person can. If it helps us understand the arguments, good; the ones who have to weigh them are us.
