F14

"My vegan burger came with cheese by mistake, so I might as well eat it"

The answer in one sentence

it's a common situation: you order a vegan burger, or ask for no mayo because the house mayo has egg in it, get home and find it came with cheese, egg mayo or butter. It's too late to send it back. In a one-off mistake like this, the reasoning is largely right: the ingredient has already been made and paid for, the sale was recorded as vegan, and eating it or binning it doesn't change what the restaurant will buy tomorrow. Binning it saves no animal and wastes the rest of the meal too. But the first thing to do is tell the restaurant, because that's the only step that changes anything. If that's not possible, removing the ingredient and eating the rest, eating it as it came or simply not wanting to eat it are all reasonable choices. Giving it to someone who isn't vegan seems to solve it, but sends the opposite message: offering an animal product as food shows you treat it as acceptable, and the other person won't think you care. The limit is that this holds for a one-off mistake, not as a rule: it doesn't cover "I ask for it without and, if it comes with, I eat it", nor does it extend to the cheese on the shelf, whose purchase makes the shop restock. And the dilemma doesn't show inconsistency: veganism seeks to avoid exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable", and someone else's mistake isn't your choice.

Note: leftovers that would be thrown away are covered in F11; this chapter is about a vegan order that came with an animal ingredient by mistake

Reply

In a one-off mistake, you're largely right: the cheese or mayo has already been made and paid for, the sale was recorded as vegan, and binning the burger saves no animal. What changes something is telling the restaurant: ask for a remake or a refund, or leave a review. That records the mistake and shows there are customers who want the vegan option done right. If that's not possible, scraping off the mayo or cheese and eating the rest, eating it as it came or not eating it are all reasonable choices; giving it to someone who isn't vegan suggests you don't care. Just don't turn the exception into a rule: the cheese at the supermarket has also "already been made", but buying it is what makes the shop restock.

For children

Imagine you ordered a sandwich without cheese, because you don't want cows to suffer, and it came with cheese. That was someone else's mistake, not yours. Throwing the sandwich in the bin doesn't help the cow, because the cheese has already been made. What helps is telling the shop it came wrong, so they're more careful next time. After that, you can take the cheese off and eat the rest, eat it as it came, or not eat it if you don't want to. Giving the sandwich to a friend seems kind, but then your friend will think you don't care about the cheese. What's not OK is ordering without cheese and hoping it comes with cheese.

Ask a question back

"If binning it doesn't help animals, what does? Telling the restaurant, or letting the mistake slide?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"I ordered a plant-based burger with no mayo, because the house mayo has egg in it. I got home and there it was. I can't send it back. If I bin it, the hen gets nothing out of it, and I'm wasting food. So I might as well eat it."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1The animal ingredient has already been made and paid for, and the sale was recorded as a vegan meal.
  2. P2Eating the burger or binning it doesn't change what the restaurant will make or buy in future.
  3. P3Binning it also wastes the bun, the plant-based patty and the resources used to make them.
  4. CSo, in a one-off mistake, eating it is at least as acceptable as binning it.

The premises are generally true for a one-off mistake, and the conclusion follows. The problem arises when the conclusion is stretched into "so any product that's already been made can be consumed", or when the mistake stops being a mistake and becomes a habit.

The argument is valid and, in the narrow case, sound. It fails when generalized: to what's on the shelf, to what's offered at a party, or to the order that "always comes wrong".

Full answer

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Telling the restaurant is what changes something.

    A complaint records the mistake, may get you a remade meal or a refund, and shows there are customers who want the vegan option done right. Eating it or binning it sends no signal at all to whoever got it wrong.

    2. In a one-off mistake, eating it doesn't increase demand.

    The sale went through as a vegan meal. The restaurant won't buy more cheese or more eggs because you ate what came by mistake.

    3. Binning it doesn't undo the harm and wastes the rest.

    The cheese or egg has still been produced, and goes in the bin along with the bun, the plant-based patty, and the water, land and work used to make them. According to the FAO, about a third of the food produced in the world is already lost or wasted.

    4. Often you can remove the ingredient and eat the rest.

    Scraping off the mayo or taking off the slice of cheese saves the bun, the plant-based patty and the vegetables, and the little animal product left over goes in the bin. When the ingredient is mixed in, like melted cheese or sauce through the middle of the burger, it can no longer be separated, and the choice goes back to eating it as it came or not eating it.

    5. Giving it to someone who isn't vegan sends the wrong message.

    It looks like the option that wastes nothing: the person would eat a burger with animal products anyway. But offering the cheese or egg mayo to someone else treats it as acceptable food, and that's how they'll read it: if you're handing it out, you can't care that much. Quietly eating what came by mistake is a mistake that stays with you; giving it away endorses, in front of someone, the product you refuse. One caveat: if the person already knows why you're vegan and the gesture leaves no doubt, the effect is smaller, but the message is still ambiguous.

    6. Not eating it is also reasonable.

    Some people feel disgust, and there's a moral reason to refuse even when the harm is done. Think of a human case: if an ingredient came from a system that exploits people, many would refuse to eat it, even knowing it would change nothing. The refusal isn't about consequences, but about not treating the victim as a product (F11). No one is obliged to eat what they see as the product of exploitation.

    7. The limit: it only holds for a one-off mistake.

    If it becomes a habit ("I ask for it without and, if it comes with, I eat it"), the mistake stops being a mistake, and the restaurant loses its reason to get it right. If a place gets it wrong almost every time, ordering there again is already a choice.

    8. It doesn't extend to what's on the shelf.

    The cheese at the supermarket and the meat at the butcher's have also "already been made". The difference is that buying them is exactly what makes the shop restock (K1, K2). What makes the mistake acceptable, no effect on demand, is what every ordinary purchase lacks.

    9. What others see counts too.

    Eating a burger with cheese in front of others can give the impression that "vegans eat cheese when they feel like it". Explaining what happened avoids the misunderstanding.

    10. The dilemma doesn't show inconsistency.

    Veganism seeks to avoid exploitation and cruelty "as far as is possible and practicable", and doesn't promise purity (L12). Someone else's mistake isn't your choice. Thinking about what to do next is a sign that a person takes animals seriously, not that veganism is inconsistent.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: then it applies to anything that can't be undone

    The most serious version says that if vegans can eat the cheese that came by mistake, because "it can't be undone", then they can eat the egg cake already sitting at the party, the leftover cheese in a colleague's fridge, the pizza someone ordered and no one wants. There's always some animal product that "has already been made". So the criterion would be useless.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The question is whether your choice changes the future.

    In a one-off mistake, it doesn't. At a party, it does: whoever bought the food counts on people eating it and, if vegans eat it, the host buys it again next time.

    Accepting what's offered teaches the opposite.

    Declining kindly and explaining shows the host what to buy next time (J12). Eating it teaches that it makes no difference.

    Genuine leftovers are a different case, and already debatable.

    Eating what really would be thrown away, with no one counting on it, is the case in F11, and even there it has limits: recurring leftovers become demand.

    The criterion separates the cases well.

    It doesn't say "anything already made can be eaten", but "what you eat mustn't make anyone produce more". Most cases that seem "impossible to undo" fail this test.

    When in doubt, not eating it costs little.

    When it's unclear whether the gesture will create demand, declining rarely harms anyone.

Contradictions

"If the vegan eats it, they're a hypocrite."

And if they bin it, it's waste: the criticism accepts no answer.

Says a mistake proves veganism is inconsistent.

And wouldn't say a vegetarian stops being vegetarian because a restaurant put bacon on their plate by mistake.

Uses "it's already made" for the wrong burger.

And not for the cheese at the supermarket, which is also already made, and which they'd keep buying.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The argument is right about the one-off mistake. Binning it saves no animal, wastes food, and the blame for the mistake doesn't lie with whoever ordered correctly. Vegans don't need to turn a mistake into a moral crisis.

But

But the same reasoning doesn't work for what you buy at the supermarket or accept at a party, because there the choice changes what gets produced. The best thing to do is tell the restaurant, decide without guilt whether to remove the ingredient, eat it as it came or not eat it, without offering the product to others as if it were acceptable, and not turn the exception into a rule.
