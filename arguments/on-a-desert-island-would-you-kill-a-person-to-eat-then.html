F2

"On a desert island, would you kill a person to eat, then?"

The answer in one sentence

the question is a counter-attack on the answer in F1. If necessity allows killing an animal, and species doesn't matter, why not a human? It's a serious question, and it has two coherent answers. The more solid one: necessity doesn't make it right to kill an innocent being who threatens no one, human or animal; it can only excuse the person who does it, which is why we don't blame or punish them. The other: necessity would justify it in both cases, but only in artificial scenarios. Neither answer touches the life of someone with a supermarket. And anyone who refuses to kill the human but accepts killing the animal needs to name a difference other than species.

Short answer

It's a fair question, and there is a consistent answer for both cases. For many philosophers, in an emergency like that, necessity doesn't make it right to kill someone innocent who threatens no one. It can excuse the person who does it, which is why we don't blame or punish them. That holds for the human and for the animal. English courts already ruled, in 1884, that necessity is no defense for killing a castaway and eating him. And when the survivors of a plane crash in the Andes, in 1972, ate the bodies of those who had already died, the world understood their despair, but no one took it as permission to kill. And none of this changes the life of someone with a supermarket: there, there's no necessity, no justification and no excuse.

For children

Imagine two people lost on an island, very hungry. One of them kills the other to eat. That's still wrong, because the other person didn't do anything bad. But we understand the despair, and maybe we wouldn't punish the one who did it. With an animal it's similar: the animal didn't do anything bad to anyone either. And at home, with food in the fridge, nobody is on that island.

Ask a question back

"If you wouldn't kill the human, what difference, other than species, allows killing the animal?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"You said you'd eat an animal on a desert island. So would you kill a person to eat? If species doesn't matter, it's all the same."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Many animal advocates accept that, in extreme necessity, killing an animal for food is permissible.
  2. P2Anti-speciesism says that species, on its own, doesn't change the weight of someone's interests.
  3. P3So, in an equivalent necessity, killing a human for food would also be permissible.
  4. P4But killing an innocent human for food is not permissible.
  5. CSo anti-speciesism is incoherent: it must either accept killing humans, deny the necessity exception, or give up the idea that species doesn't matter.

P1 describes well the position of several animal rights philosophers, such as Peter Singer, Tom Regan and Alasdair Cochrane. P2 is the definition of anti-speciesism. P4 is accepted by almost everyone, and by the law too. The tension is real. But P1 has a weaker reading: necessity excuses the person who kills, without making the act right. On that reading, the four premises sit together without contradiction.

The argument is valid and points to a real problem. Its soundness fails in the step to C: there is a coherent way out that requires neither killing humans nor blaming people who are starving.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The question forces a choice. There are two coherent answers and one incoherent one:

    AnswerAbout the humanAbout the animalIs it coherent?
    Necessity excuses but doesn't justifyWrong, but excusableWrong, but excusableYes
    Necessity justifies in both cases, only in extreme scenariosPermissible only in artificial situationsPermissibleYes, but at a high cost
    Necessity justifies only for the animalForbiddenPermissibleOnly if there's a difference other than species

    A 2025 paper by Joshua Jarvis-Campbell, published in the journal Philosophia, examines exactly this problem. It starts from the case of people who farm animals in extreme poverty and asks: if necessity justifies raising and killing animals, why not humans? The paper argues that the first two ways out are plausible.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Anti-speciesism doesn't say everything is equal.

    It forbids discounting someone's interests because of their species. It doesn't forbid considering real differences. But the paper notes that these differences, such as suffering more or having more plans for the future, are contingent: one can always imagine a case where they don't exist. In that case, an anti-speciesist must give the same answer for the human and the animal.

    2. Justifying isn't excusing.

    A justified act is a right act. An excused act is still wrong, but the person who does it deserves no blame or punishment, because of the pressure they were under. The paper gives the example of a soldier who, with a gun to their head, is forced to order an attack on innocent civilians. The attack is wrong, but it's reasonable to excuse the soldier.

    3. Many philosophers already think this way about humans.

    Authors such as Helen Frowe, Michael Otsuka and Judith Jarvis Thomson hold that you may not kill an innocent bystander, not even to save your own life. The paper argues that there's a strong case for extending this protection to animals. And it notes that killing an animal for food is more like killing that innocent bystander than like responding to a threat. On this reading, on the island, killing the human would be wrong but excusable. So would killing the animal.

    4. The law confirms the part about humans.

    In R v Dudley and Stephens (England, 1884), two castaways killed and ate a 17-year-old, Richard Parker, after many days adrift in a lifeboat. The court ruled that necessity is no defense to a charge of murder. The death sentence was later commuted to six months in prison.

    5. The Andes tragedy shows the same limit.

    On 13 October 1972, Uruguayan Air Force Flight 571, carrying an amateur rugby team and companions, crashed in the Andes with 45 people on board. The survivors spent 72 days trapped in the snow, and 16 were rescued. To avoid starving to death, they ate the bodies of companions who had already died in the crash or in an avalanche; no one was killed for that. According to the accounts, they made a pact: whoever died allowed the others to use their body. The public's first reaction was shock, but it gave way to understanding, and a priest who heard their confessions told them that, in those circumstances, they had no reason to condemn themselves. The case shows, with all the respect these people deserve, two things. Extreme necessity changes our judgment: we understand the survivors and don't blame them. But it doesn't become a general rule: what was accepted was eating those who had already died, in a situation with no way out, not killing someone to eat, nor eating humans anywhere else.

    6. The second way out exists, but it's weaker.

    The paper also argues that one can accept that killing a human would be permissible in a scenario that removes every relevant difference. That scenario would be so strange that nobody really knows what they'd think of it. It's a coherent answer, but it accepts that killing an innocent human could be permissible in some imaginable situation, and many people find that cost too high.

    7. Neither answer reaches the supermarket.

    Both ways out are about people with no alternative. Someone with plant food within reach has no necessity, and therefore neither justification nor excuse. The island question changes nothing in everyday life (F1).

    8. The question turns back on whoever asks it.

    Anyone who refuses to kill the human but accepts killing the animal in the same situation needs to say why. If the answer is "because it's human", that's giving weight to species itself (A12, A13). If it's some other difference, it needs to be stated and defended. And, as a rule, the person asking kills animals, through what they buy, with no necessity at all.

    9. The experts disagree.

    Singer, Regan and Cochrane accept that necessity makes killing animals permissible. Gary Francione holds that it remains wrong but is excusable. The paper itself says it doesn't prove which way out is correct, only that both are plausible.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: so someone raising a chicken in poverty is doing wrong?

    The most serious version says the first way out is cruel. If killing an animal out of necessity is wrong, then a family in extreme poverty raising chickens to eat would be doing wrong, and saying that to people who are going hungry seems unfair and elitist.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Wrong isn't the same as blameworthy.

    Excusing means precisely not blaming or punishing. The paper says it is very plausible that these people should be excused, given the circumstances they are in.

    We already think this way about humans.

    The soldier threatened with death does something wrong, and still we don't blame them.

    The other reading doesn't blame anyone either.

    Singer, Regan and Cochrane say necessity makes the act permissible. In neither reading are people without alternatives blamed.

    The priority lies elsewhere.

    The paper points out that industrial farming holds about 74% of farmed land animals, according to Our World in Data, and that it isn't necessary. It also says it would be absurd to stop poor people from farming animals while the rich go on exploiting animals on a massive scale.

Contradictions

Demands coherence from the vegan.

And doesn't explain why they'd kill the animal and not the human.

Finds killing a human out of necessity monstrous.

And kills animals with no necessity at all.

Uses the island to accuse the vegan.

But doesn't live on an island.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The question is right to demand coherence. If anti-speciesism says species doesn't matter, it needs to say what it would do with a human in the same situation, and many animal advocates have never thought about it. The answer "necessity justifies" leaves a door open that needs examining.

But

But the demand has an answer. The more solid one is that necessity doesn't make it right to kill an innocent, human or animal, and only excuses the person who acts. And that answer changes nothing for someone who has alternatives: without necessity, there's neither justification nor excuse.
