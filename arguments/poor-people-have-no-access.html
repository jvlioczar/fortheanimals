J2

"Poor people have no access"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

lack of access to food is a real barrier for some people, and hunger is a serious problem. But that barrier doesn't apply to the person raising the objection, who usually has access to shops and chooses what they eat. And the basis of a vegan diet, rice, beans, potatoes and vegetables, is precisely the most accessible food. Veganism also isn't "a thing for rich white people": in India, about 39% of adults describe themselves as vegetarian, and many cultures have ancient vegetarian traditions.

Note: this chapter also answers "Veganism is a privilege of rich white people", which was merged into it.

Short answer

Hunger and lack of access are real problems, and people who are hungry have no choice. But veganism is addressed to those who can choose, and the person raising the objection almost always can. The basis of a vegan diet, rice, beans, potatoes and vegetables, is the most accessible food. And veganism isn't "a thing for rich white people": in India, about 39% of adults describe themselves as vegetarian, and there are meat-free traditions in many cultures.

For children

Some people don't have enough money even to buy enough food, and that's very unfair. People who are hungry don't have a choice. But many people do have a choice and can eat rice, beans and vegetables, which are cheap vegan foods. And in many countries, such as India, millions of people have gone without meat for a very long time.

Ask a question back

"Are you among the people who have no choice about what they eat?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Tell that to people who are going hungry. There's no vegan store in poor neighborhoods. Veganism is a middle-class thing."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Many people live with food insecurity or in areas with little access to fresh food.
  2. P2For them, choosing what to eat is a luxury.
  3. P3Requiring veganism of everyone ignores these inequalities.
  4. CSo veganism is a privilege, not a moral obligation.

P1 and P2 are true and serious. The problem lies in P3 and the conclusion: veganism is addressed to those who can choose, and most people who use the argument can.

The argument is valid for those who really have no choice. It fails when used by those who do.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument uses the situation of those without a choice to speak about those with one:

    SituationIs there a choice?Does veganism apply?
    Hunger and severe food insecurityNoNo: "as far as is possible and practicable"
    Limited access, but some choicePartlyAs far as possible
    Access to shops and income to chooseYesYes
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It uses those without a choice to justify those with one.

    The same pattern as D3. Veganism already allows exceptions for those who can't.

    2. The vegan basics are accessible.

    Rice, beans, potatoes, oats and vegetables are among the cheapest and most available foods in most countries (J1).

    3. Refusing meat isn't exclusive to rich white people.

    In India, about 39% of adults describe themselves as vegetarian, and 81% follow some restriction on eating meat. Vegetarianism isn't veganism, and India consumes a lot of milk; but the figure shows that not eating animals is far from being a habit of a white elite. There are vegetarian traditions in Hinduism, Jainism, Buddhism, Rastafarianism and many other cultures.

    4. The argument usually comes from those who have a choice.

    That doesn't invalidate it, but it shows it speaks about other people: for whoever says it, the moral question still stands.

    5. Hunger doesn't depend on veganism, but livestock doesn't help.

    Hunger is above all a problem of poverty and distribution, not a lack of food. Even so, livestock farming uses most farmland to produce a small fraction of calories (H4), and a more plant-based system could feed more people with the same land.

    6. Exploiting animals is what is elitist.

    The charge of elitism holds for some lines of industrialized vegan products, which do cost more. But veganism is not aimed at specific groups: it is the refusal to exploit those who can suffer. Treating someone as usable because they have less power is exactly the logic of superiority we associate with elitism, and it is the logic that sustains animal exploitation.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Pew Research Center, diet in India (2021)39% of Indian adults describe themselves as vegetarian; 81% follow some restriction on meatA survey of about 30,000 adults
    The Vegan Society, definition of veganismVeganism seeks to exclude exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable"The exception is already in the definition
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the vegan movement is sometimes elitist

    The most serious version grants that veganism is possible for many, but criticizes how part of the movement presents itself: with expensive products, distant language and little attention to inequality.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's a criticism of form, not content.

    Communication can and should change.

    There are grassroots vegan movements.

    In low-income neighborhoods and communities, there are initiatives promoting affordable plant-based food.

    The criticism doesn't change the moral question.

    For those who can choose, the question remains.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Hunger and inequality are serious problems, and they deserve to be fought. But they don't justify those who can choose continuing to choose the option that requires animals' deaths. For those with a choice, the moral question still stands.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Unequal access to food is real and serious, and the vegan movement needs to pay attention to it, without judging those who have no choice and without presenting veganism as an expensive lifestyle. The criticism of elitism has substance.

But

But a vegan diet can be one of the most accessible, it exists in many cultures, and it's addressed to those who can choose. For them, other people's inequality isn't an excuse.
