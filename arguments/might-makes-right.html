C6

"Might makes right."

The answer in one sentence

the "law of the strongest" describes a natural process, not a moral norm. Using it as a justification is social Darwinism, which once served to justify inequality and violence among humans and was rejected. The whole idea of rights exists precisely to protect the weakest from the force of the strongest.

Short answer

The "law of the strongest" describes what happens in nature, not what is right. Using it as a moral rule is social Darwinism, which once justified slavery and the exclusion of people, and was rejected for that reason. The whole idea of rights exists to protect the weak from the strong. If strength gave rights, anyone stronger than you could do whatever they wanted with you.

For children

At school, can the biggest kid in class push the smaller ones around? No, and that's exactly why there are rules: to protect those who are smaller or weaker. Being strong doesn't give the right to hurt. It's the same with animals: being stronger than them doesn't give people the right to hurt them.

Ask a question back

"If strength gave the right, could someone stronger than you use you?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Survival of the fittest. It's the law of the jungle. The weak serve the strong."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1In nature, the strongest or fittest prevail over others.
  2. P2Humans are, in practice, the most powerful species on the planet.
  3. P3It's natural for the most powerful to use the weaker.
  4. CSo it's legitimate for humans to use other animals.

The strong version rests on a reading of the theory of evolution. But Darwin described how species change, not how we should act.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, which turns a natural process into a norm, and in the idea of force as legitimacy.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument uses evolution as if it were a moral rule:

    What the theory of evolution describesWhat the argument concludes
    Better-adapted organisms tend to leave more offspringThe strongest have the right to use the weakest
    A process with no intention or valueA rule of conduct
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's the move from "is" to "ought".

    Something happening in nature doesn't say whether it's right (C1).

    2. "Fittest" doesn't mean "strongest".

    In biology, fitness is success in leaving offspring, and it often depends on cooperation, not strength. The phrase "survival of the fittest" isn't even Darwin's: it was coined by Herbert Spencer.

    3. Social Darwinism has already been tried.

    The idea that the law of the strongest applies to society was used to justify slavery, colonialism and the exclusion of the poor. Thomas Huxley, Darwin's defender, argued in 1893 that ethical progress consists precisely in combating the natural process, not imitating it.

    4. Rights exist against force.

    Laws, rights and institutions serve to stop the most powerful from doing what they want with the most vulnerable. Accepting the law of the strongest would abolish the very idea of justice.

    5. The argument turns against whoever uses it.

    If strength gave rights, any stronger person or group could use whoever defends the argument.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: it's not a right, it's reality

    The most serious version says this isn't about justifying but about recognizing reality: there have always been, and always will be, those who dominate, and morality is a luxury of those already on top. Demanding that humans give up their power over animals would be naive.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Describing isn't justifying.

    Even if domination is common, the moral question remains.

    History refutes the fatalism.

    Many forms of domination were abolished or reduced, against those who said they were inevitable.

    The argument abandons ethics.

    If morality is a luxury, there's no moral debate left, only power.

Contradictions

Defends rights for themselves.

No one accepts being used by someone stronger.

Supports laws against violence.

They exist to restrain the law of the strongest.

Protects the weak in everything else.

Children, the elderly and the sick are protected precisely because they're vulnerable.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The description holds some truth: nature is full of competition, and humans have enormous power over other species. Pretending that asymmetry doesn't exist would be naive.

But

But recognizing strength isn't accepting it as a rule. Human moral history is, in large part, the history of limiting what the strong can do to the weak. Extending that history to animals continues it; it doesn't deny nature.
