J15

"A vegan world is utopian."

Before answering: an indirect argument

This argument is about cost, practicality and the economy, not whether animals deserve respect. Many activists question this kind of argument in defending animals, because it sidesteps what matters: no one defends the rights of people who face discrimination by saying that treating them fairly is good for the environment.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

When used to justify exploitation, arguments like this one usually fail twice. They get the facts wrong, which often arrive distorted. And they get the morality wrong, because they leap from what is to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it.

So show the facts and correct what is wrong, but always come back to what matters: respect for animals and justice toward them. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the argument confuses the feasibility of a total, immediate change with present individual duty. Perhaps the world will never be entirely vegan, just as crime has never disappeared. But that doesn't change what each person should do today. Many moral changes seemed utopian before they happened, and the relevant question isn't whether everyone will change, but whether I should.

Short answer

Perhaps the world will never be entirely vegan, just as crime has never disappeared. But each person's duty doesn't depend on everyone fulfilling it. Abolishing slavery, giving women the vote and protecting children from labor all once seemed utopian. The relevant question isn't whether everyone will change, but whether I should. And each person who changes spares real animals, today.

For children

Maybe not everyone in the world will stop eating meat. But that doesn't change what each of us can do. Not everyone is kind, and it's still worth being kind. Every animal who stops suffering matters.

Ask a question back

"If the world will never be perfect, does that excuse you from doing what's right?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"That will never happen. It's a hippie dream."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1A fully vegan world would require enormous changes in cultures, economies and habits.
  2. P2Those changes are unlikely in the foreseeable future.
  3. P3It makes no sense to guide conduct by an unattainable ideal.
  4. CSo veganism is a utopia and shouldn't guide behavior.

P1 and P2 may be true. The problem lies in P3: individual duty doesn't depend on everyone fulfilling it.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses two questions:

    QuestionTypeAnswer
    Will the whole world go vegan?PredictionUncertain
    Should I stop eating animals?MoralDoesn't depend on the prediction
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Duty doesn't depend on everyone joining in.

    We don't stop condemning violence because it will never end.

    2. Many changes seemed utopian.

    The abolition of slavery, women's suffrage and the protection of children were treated as dreams before they happened.

    3. Individual impact is real.

    Each person who changes reduces demand and spares animals (K1).

    4. The world can change partially.

    Even without an entirely vegan world, the reduction in animal consumption could be enormous.

    5. The argument changes the question.

    It answers a prediction, not the moral question.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Kagan, *Do I Make a Difference?* (2011)Each purchase has a real expected effect on productionShows the value of individual action
    Our World in Data, land use and dietsA global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%Shows the change is feasible in principle
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: unrealistic ideals breed frustration

    The most serious version says that championing an unattainable ideal can frustrate people and put them off, and that more modest goals would be more effective.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's a strategic question, not a moral one.

    The best way to communicate can be debated without denying the duty.

    Individual duty is attainable.

    Each person can stop eating animals, even if the world doesn't change.

    Ideals guide change.

    Even when they're never fully realized.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question about the world's future is interesting, but it doesn't replace the question about the present: today, for those who can choose, what justifies paying for animals to be killed?

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that an entirely vegan world seems distant, and that cultural changes of this size are slow and uncertain. Being realistic about the pace of change helps plan better strategies.

But

But the distance from the ideal doesn't change what each person can do today. Individual duty doesn't wait for the world to change.
