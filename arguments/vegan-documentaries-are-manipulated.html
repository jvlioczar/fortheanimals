L26

"Vegan documentaries are manipulated"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

some contain real errors and exaggerations, especially on health and on numbers, and that's worth acknowledging: What the Health was criticized even by a vegan dietitian, and the "empty oceans by 2048" forecast in Seaspiracy was called into doubt by the author of the study it cites. Editing and soundtrack are tools of persuasion. But two things need separating: claims made by interviewees, which can be wrong, and footage shot inside farms and slaughterhouses, which shows standard, legal practices described in the industry's own rules (B10). And the moral conclusion doesn't depend on any film.

Short answer

You're partly right: there are documentaries with errors, and I don't defend them. What the Health overstated the health claims, and the 2048 figure in Seaspiracy doesn't hold up. But camera footage from inside a farm isn't an opinion. Gestation crates, the culling of male chicks and the gas stunning of pigs appear in the industry's own rules. And I don't need any film for the main question: animals feel, and we don't need to eat them.

For children

A film can exaggerate in one part and show something true in another. If someone tells a story and makes a number bigger, we correct the number. But if the same film shows a hen stuck in a cramped cage, the cage is real. The best thing is to check each thing, instead of believing everything or believing nothing.

Ask a question back

"Setting aside everything that was exaggerated, is what's left in the footage acceptable to you?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"That's propaganda with sad music. That documentary has already been debunked. Vegan media exaggerates everything."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Vegan documentaries are made by activists, with the aim of persuading.
  2. P2Several of them contain factual errors, inflated numbers and emotional editing devices.
  3. P3A source that gets things wrong and manipulates isn't reliable.
  4. CSo what these films say about animals can be dismissed.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3 and in the conclusion: errors in some claims in some films don't make the footage in others false, nor do they settle the moral question.

The argument is invalid: it generalizes from parts to the whole and from the messenger to the message.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    "Vegan documentary" lumps together very different films, and within each film there are different kinds of content:

    Kind of contentExampleCan it be wrong?
    Health claimsWhat the Health (2017)Yes, and several are
    Projections and numbers"Empty oceans by 2048", in Seaspiracy (2021)Yes; that one was contested
    Footage of production practicesDominion (2018), Earthlings (2005)The footage shows what happens; the fair question is whether it's the standard (B10)
    Editing, narration and soundtrackAll of themThese are tools of persuasion, not evidence
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The errors exist, and acknowledging them is part of the job.

    The American vegan dietitian Virginia Messina wrote in 2017 that What the Health cherry-picks studies and overstates conclusions: it treats hot dogs and cigarettes as equally dangerous because they're in the same carcinogen category (the category indicates the strength of the evidence, not the size of the risk) and suggests a vegan diet cures almost everything. In her view, the film damages the cause's credibility. On Seaspiracy, the BBC's fact-checking team spoke to the lead author of the 2006 study behind the 2048 forecast, the researcher Boris Worm, of Dalhousie University in Canada, who said the paper was 15 years old, used data almost 20 years old, and that several regions had been rebuilding stocks.

    2. An error in a claim doesn't erase an image.

    What an interviewee says about cholesterol doesn't change what the camera recorded in a shed. Dominion was made mainly with hidden cameras and drones on farms and in slaughterhouses in Australia. The hardest scenes show routine procedures.

    3. What shocks is usually in the rulebook.

    The European regulation on killing lists among the permitted methods the maceration of chicks up to 72 hours old, carbon dioxide at high concentration for pigs and the electrical waterbath for poultry. In Brazil, Ministry of Agriculture rules allow gestation crates early in pregnancy and the waterbath for poultry (B9, B10). Anyone who doubts the film can read the regulation.

    4. Persuasion isn't lying.

    Sad music and editing steer emotion, and it's fair to notice that. But every documentary, every news report and every commercial uses these tools. The useful question is whether what's shown is true and representative.

    5. The industry does propaganda too.

    In the United States there are 22 federal programs promoting agricultural products, funded by producers and overseen by the Department of Agriculture; campaigns such as "Got Milk?" and "Pork: The Other White Meat" came out of them. Packaging with cows on pasture and hens roaming free is editing too. Skepticism has to apply to both sides.

    6. The moral conclusion doesn't depend on a film.

    The case for animals rests on two things that can be checked without cinema: they feel, and most people can live well without eating them. A bad documentary is a poor messenger, and that doesn't alter the message (L1).

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: if they get the checkable things wrong, why trust the rest?

    The most serious version says: when a source errs on verifiable points, it's rational to distrust it on the points that can't be verified. The filmmakers had a conclusion ready and chose the worst footage. The ordinary viewer has no way of knowing what's the rule and what's the exception. Given that, suspending judgment is the prudent attitude.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Distrust is reasonable; dismissing without checking is something else.

    Distrust should lead to looking for better sources, such as official rules, production data and the scientific literature, and they confirm the practices shown.

    Standard practices aren't chosen moments.

    A sow in a gestation crate stays there for weeks, not just during the minute that was filmed (B10).

    Suspending judgment doesn't suspend consumption.

    Someone who says "I don't know what to believe" and keeps buying has already picked a side. When in doubt about a serious harm, the prudent thing would be to abstain.

    Selection bias is fixed with transparency.

    If the films show only the worst, the industry would only need to open its doors. In several US states the response was to try to ban filming, and courts struck those laws down (B10).

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Suppose every vegan documentary were bad and none existed. Animals would go on being confined and killed in the same way, and the question would be the same: is it fair to do this to those who feel, when we don't need to? Films are a way of showing; what matters is what happens to each animal when the camera isn't there.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets a lot right. There are vegan documentaries with false or exaggerated claims, mainly about health and environmental numbers, and they're made to persuade, with editing and music in the service of that. Repeating a wrong figure because it helps the cause is dishonest and costs dearly in credibility. This guide has a whole section, "Stop saying this", devoted to exaggerations that circulate among vegans, because correcting our own mistakes is part of defending animals well. The skepticism of whoever raises the objection is a virtue.

But

But it has to go all the way. Once the exaggerations are removed, what's left are real, common, legal practices that anyone can check in the rules. And the reason not to eat animals was never a film.
