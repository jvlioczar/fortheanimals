I15

"Safaris and animal tours are harmless tourism"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

"animal tourism" lumps very different things together. Watching free animals from a distance, in their habitat, is one thing; riding elephants, posing with lion cubs or swimming with captive dolphins is another. A University of Oxford study assessed 24 types of wildlife tourist attraction and found negative welfare impacts in 18 of them, while the vast majority of tourists rated even the worst attractions positively.

Short answer

It depends on the tour. Watching free animals from a distance, in their habitat, can even help protect natural areas. But riding elephants, posing with lion cubs or swimming with captive dolphins depends on capture, breeding and training that make the animals suffer. An Oxford study assessed 24 types of wildlife attraction and found welfare harm in 18, yet most tourists still rated them positively.

For children

Seeing an elephant walking free on the savanna, from far away, is very different from climbing on the back of an elephant trained for it. For the elephant to obey, someone had to teach it with fear and pain. Free animals don't need us close by; the captive ones didn't choose to be there.

Ask a question back

"If the elephant were free to choose, would it carry tourists all day?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Riding an elephant in Thailand was the best part of the trip. The animals looked happy."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Animal tourism brings income to the places that keep the animals.
  2. P2That income can fund conservation and the animals' care.
  3. P3The animals look fine during the activities.
  4. CSo taking part in these tours is harmless, or even good for the animals.

P1 is true. P2 holds for some activities, above all watching free animals. P3 confuses appearance with welfare.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3 and in stretching P2 to every kind of tour.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument puts very different activities in the same sentence:

    ActivityWhat the animal lives through
    Watching free animals from a distanceA free life; the impact depends on distance, number of vehicles and rules
    Riding elephantsTraining to obey, chains, repetitive work
    Posing with lion or tiger cubsCubs taken from their mothers and handled all day, often feeding captive breeding
    Swimming with dolphins in tanksCaptivity in a tiny space for animals that travel long distances
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Most of the attractions assessed cause harm.

    Moorhouse and colleagues, from the University of Oxford's conservation research unit, assessed 24 types of wildlife tourist attraction. In 18, the welfare impact was negative, affecting between 230,000 and 550,000 animals; in 14, the conservation impact was negative too.

    2. Tourists don't notice.

    In the same study, at least 80% of tourists typically left positive TripAdvisor reviews, even for the attractions with the worst welfare standards. The impression that "the animals looked happy" is not a good measure.

    3. A calm animal isn't always a well animal.

    A cub lying still in someone's lap may be exhausted, scared or trained not to react. Elephants that carry tourists were conditioned to obey.

    4. The money feeds the supply.

    Paying for direct contact keeps up the demand that leads to capturing and breeding more animals for the same use.

    5. Watching from a distance is something else.

    Seeing free animals, without touching or feeding them, can fund protected areas. The objection mixes what can help with what clearly harms.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Moorhouse et al., 2015, *PLOS ONE*Of 24 types of attraction assessed, 18 had negative welfare impacts and 14 negative conservation impacts; at least 80% of tourists left positive reviewsUniversity of Oxford's conservation research unit
    National Geographic, 2015Report on the study, with examples such as elephant rides and tiger encountersJournalism
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: tourism funds conservation

    The most serious version points out that, in many countries, tourism income is what pays for national parks, rangers and the communities that live alongside wild animals. Without it, habitats could become pasture or farmland.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    That holds for watching, not for contact.

    Funding for protected areas comes mostly from seeing free animals, not from riding or holding them.

    Conserving the species doesn't license exploiting the individual.

    The trained elephant gets nothing from being the attraction (A10).

    There is tourism that helps without harming.

    Just choose activities with no contact, with distance rules and with no captive animals.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The underlying question is the same as for any use of animals: do their interests count, or only the experience of whoever pays? Watching without interfering respects the animals; turning them into a holiday toy doesn't.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Hasty generalization
What holds for watching is extended to contact

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Appearance as proof
"It looked happy" is taken as welfare

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

The tourist's pleasure above the animal's interest
The photo outweighs the animal's life

Biases

Contradictions

Says they love animals.

And pays for them to be kept captive and trained.

Prefers free animals in documentaries.

And chooses to see them chained on holiday.

Wouldn't accept the same with dogs.

A ride on dogs trained by force would be cruelty.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that tourism can fund habitat protection, and that watching free animals, with distance rules, can be a respectful way to get to know them. It's also true that many tourists go to these attractions because they like animals.

But

But that same liking calls for choosing the tours where the animal doesn't pay the price: no contact, no captivity, no animals trained to entertain.
