A19

"Human babies have the potential to be rational"

The answer in one sentence

having the potential for something doesn't confer the rights of those who already have it. A future president doesn't command the army today. The argument also leaves out the humans who will never have that potential, and ignores that what demands protection now is present sentience, not future capacity.

Note: this chapter doesn't enter the abortion debate, where the same argument appears. The focus is the logical structure as applied to animals

Short answer

Potential doesn't transfer rights. As the philosopher Joel Feinberg wrote, a potential president of the United States is not on that account commander-in-chief. Potential capacity generates, at most, potential rights.

Besides, the argument doesn't cover all humans: someone with anencephaly, advanced dementia or in a permanent vegetative state doesn't have that potential, and remains protected. In other words, potential isn't doing the work. What protects any of them is the present capacity to feel.

For children

Can a boy who wants to be a pilot fly a plane today? No. He'll be able to when he grows up and learns. Being able to be something in the future isn't the same as being it now. And look: what stops us from hurting the baby isn't what they'll be one day. It's that they feel pain today. And the little pig feels it today too.

Ask a question back

"And a person who will never have that potential? Do they lose protection? If not, what protects them?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"The baby will grow up and think; the cow never will."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Having the potential to develop a morally relevant capacity confers, from now on, the status of those who already have it.
  2. P2Human babies have the potential to become rational and self-aware.
  3. P3No animal has that potential.
  4. CBabies have full moral status and animals don't.

The argument comes in after A15 fails: since babies aren't rational, it appeals to what they will be. It's the second attempt to keep babies in and animals out, A18 being the first.

There's a stronger version that speaks not of status but of harm: killing a baby deprives them of a whole future of experiences, and that future would be richer than an animal's. That version is dealt with in (d) and (e), and it leads straight to B2.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1, which commits the logical error pointed out by Joel Feinberg, and at P3, which treats "potential" selectively. P2 is true for most babies, but not for all, and that detail brings down the rest.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The core of the problem is P1, and the classic diagnosis is Joel Feinberg's: potential capacity generates potential rights, not actual rights. A potential president of the United States is not, for that reason, commander-in-chief.

    CasePotentialCurrent status
    Medical studentWill be a doctorCan't operate today
    16-year-oldWill be able to vote and, in many countries, drive at 18Doesn't vote today
    CandidateMay become presidentCommands nothing today
    BabyWill be rationalIsn't rational today, and is protected anyway

    The last row is the decisive one. If the baby is protected now, and potential doesn't transfer status now, then the protection comes from something else. That something else is present sentience, which the baby has and the animal has too.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The logical error of potentiality.

    From "X will be Y" it doesn't follow that "X now has the rights of Y". Otherwise every law student could try cases.

    2. It doesn't cover all humans.

    Anencephaly, advanced dementia and a permanent vegetative state are cases with no potential for future rationality. Since these humans remain protected, potential isn't the source of protection. The argument repeats the failure it was trying to fix, and species overlap returns.

    3. Animals have potential too.

    A piglet has the potential for a complex social life, bonds, exploring the environment and years of experiences. Whoever holds P3 has to say that only the potential for rationality counts, and explain why that specific capacity decides questions of pain and death. It's A15 again, now in the future tense.

    4. The cut-off is arbitrary in another sense.

    If potential matters, how far does it go? With technology, somatic cells can be reprogrammed, and animals can be genetically modified. The concept of potential doesn't carry a natural limit with it, and whoever uses it has to stipulate one, usually in the way that produces the desired result.

    5. Present protection answers a present harm.

    When someone feels pain, the harm is now. Talking about the future changes the subject: it turns a question about present suffering into a bet about biographies.

    6. Confusing two debates.

    The harm version (killing deprives of a future) isn't about moral status but about the gravity of death. It can support the claim that killing a human is worse than killing a fish, but not that killing a fish is permitted, nor that confining and mutilating are acceptable. That thread continues in B2.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it offersNote
    Feinberg, "Abortion"A potential president of the United States is not on that account commander-in-chief; potential qualification generates potential rights, not actual onesThe text is about abortion, but the logical point is general, and that's what matters here
    Singer, Practical EthicsA potential X doesn't have the rights of an XCambridge University Press
    McMahan, The Ethics of Killing (2002)The time-relative interest account: the gravity of death depends on how much the individual loses relative to the future they would have hadOxford University Press; basis of the harm version
    Naconecy, Ética e animais (2006) (in Portuguese)Systematizes the ways out of the argument from species overlap, potential among them

    This is a chapter about logic, not data. There's no relevant empirical controversy: no one doubts that human babies usually become rational adults, nor that some humans never will.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: it's not status, it's deprivation

    The serious counter-reply abandons P1 and changes ground. It says: forget status, look at harm. Killing a healthy baby destroys decades of experiences, projects and relationships they would have had. Killing an animal destroys a real but less dense future. So the baby's death would be worse, and the difference would lie in the lost future, not in the species. This is Jeff McMahan's time-relative interest account, and it's the philosophically respectable version of A19.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It already concedes the essential point.

    It now compares lost futures, not species. On that ground, a six-month-old pig killed at slaughter loses years of life they would have had: the harm stops being zero and becomes large.

    It grades death; it doesn't authorize killing.

    That A's death is worse than B's doesn't make B's death permitted, much less in exchange for taste.

    It says nothing about suffering.

    Confinement, mutilations, separation from offspring and fear have nothing to do with a lost future. The counter-reply, even if accepted, leaves the entire critique of industrial farming intact.

    It leads to conclusions the speaker rejects.

    Taken seriously, the deprivation account implies that a young person's death is worse than an old person's, and that the death of a human with a short life expectancy would be less serious. Few accept these consequences without qualifications.

    Potential can increase the duty, not dispense with it.

    If the future a being would have matters, then cutting short the future of a young, healthy animal is exactly what farming does, billions of times a year.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

The potentiality error
Treats potential rights as actual rights
Proves too much
Excludes humans with no potential for rationality
Selective criterion
Only the potential for rationality counts; the potential to live well doesn't
Temporal shift
Answers a present harm with a future property

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Concept without a defined limit
"Potential" doesn't carry a boundary with it; one is stipulated to suit the desired conclusion

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Confusing gravity with permission
From "this death is worse" it concludes "that death is permitted"
Silence about suffering
Talks of a lost future and ignores present pain, fear and confinement

Biases

Narrative thinking

Human biographies are easy to imagine, and a pig's life is rarely narrated

Motivated reasoning

In-group bias

Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "having the potential for a capacity already confers the rights of those who have it".

Contradictions

Doesn't apply potential in any other context.

No one lets a teenager drive because they'll be a driver, or lets a student operate on patients because they'll be a surgeon.

Protects humans without potential.

People in a permanent vegetative state and with advanced dementia remain fully protected, which shows the protection comes from somewhere else.

Ignores animals' potential.

The same person recognizes that a puppy "has its whole life ahead of it" when it's their own dog.

Kills precisely the young.

Farming slaughters animals long before the end of their lives: if the lost future matters, that's the worst possible case, not the most acceptable one.

Uses the argument only against animals.

In debates about the beginning of human life, many people reject the argument from potential; here, the same argument comes back as obvious.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Two things. First, the future really matters: part of what's bad about death lies in what it prevents from happening, and an ethics that ignored this would be poor. Second, there are real differences between biographies: a typical human life contains long-term projects, promises, unfinished work, and losing that is losing something specific.

But

The error lies in two steps. The first is logical: potential isn't actuality. The second is moral: recognizing that one death is worse than another doesn't make the second permitted. And since the argument itself puts the lost future at the center, it ends up condemning the practice it set out to defend, because farming lives off cutting futures short at the start.
