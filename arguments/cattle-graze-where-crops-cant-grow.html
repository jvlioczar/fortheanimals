H4

"Cattle graze where crops can't grow"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the environment and agriculture, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on environmental grounds, low-impact exploitation would be acceptable, even if it killed more sentient beings. And it is already being promoted: insect farming, "sustainable" hunting, "sustainable" leather. It is possible to reduce environmental impact and increase exploitation at the same time.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

part of it is true: about two thirds of the world's pastures aren't suitable for crops. But that land produces very little food: livestock farming uses about 80% of farmland and provides only 17% of the world's calories. And land that can't be cropped could be rewilded, storing carbon and sheltering biodiversity. The question isn't whether the land suits crops, but whether we need it for food.

Short answer

It's true that about two thirds of pastures aren't suitable for crops. But that land produces very little food: livestock farming uses about 80% of the world's farmland and provides only 17% of calories. A global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%, freeing up that land. It doesn't need to produce food: it can go back to being forests, savannas and native grasslands, which store carbon and shelter biodiversity.

For children

In some places, like mountains and dry lands, you can't grow rice or beans. So some people raise cattle there. But those places can also become forests and grasslands with wild animals, which help the planet. And we can grow enough food on the land that's good for planting.

Ask a question back

"If we don't need that land to feed ourselves, why does it have to produce meat?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Cattle turn grass into food. That land is only good for cattle."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Much of the world's pasture isn't suitable for crops, because of terrain, climate or soil.
  2. P2Ruminants can turn grass, which we don't eat, into food.
  3. P3Without cattle, that land wouldn't produce food.
  4. CSo raising cattle on that land is an efficient way to produce food.

P1 and P2 are true. So is P3, in the strict sense. The problem lies in the conclusion: the land doesn't need to produce food, and the amount of food it produces is small for the area it occupies.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails because it assumes all land must produce food, and ignores the alternative of rewilding.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument assumes the land must produce food:

    Use of non-arable landBenefit
    Cattle pastureLittle food per hectare; methane emissions; the animals' deaths
    RewildingCarbon stored in vegetation and soil; biodiversity; water resources
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The land doesn't need to produce food.

    With a plant diet, the world would need far less farmland. Non-arable land could be freed up.

    2. Food production is small for the area.

    According to Our World in Data, livestock farming occupies about 80% of farmland and provides 17% of the world's calories and 38% of its protein.

    3. Rewilding is a valuable alternative.

    Forests and native vegetation store far more carbon than pastures and shelter biodiversity. In Brazil and elsewhere, many pastures occupy land that was once forest or savanna.

    4. Many cattle don't eat only grass.

    Much of livestock farming uses grain and soy feed, which competes with human food (H1).

    5. Efficiency is low.

    Cattle convert about 2% of the energy they consume into meat. Even using grass, production per hectare is very low.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Our World in Data, land use and dietsAbout two thirds of pastures aren't suitable for crops; a global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%; cattle have an energy efficiency of about 2%Based on Poore and Nemecek, 2018
    Our World in Data, land for agricultureLivestock uses 80% of farmland and provides 17% of calories and 38% of proteinGlobal data
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: communities depend on herding

    The most serious version points out that some communities, such as herders in arid regions, depend on livestock to live, and have no economic alternative.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    That justifies exceptions.

    Communities without alternatives are in a different situation from someone buying meat at the supermarket (D3).

    Those who use the argument don't live on that land.

    Supermarket meat doesn't come from dryland herders.

    Transitions can be planned.

    Public policy can support communities through change, as in other sectors.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Even if cattle make use of land with no other agricultural use, they are still raised to die. The argument talks about efficiency, but doesn't answer the moral question: if we don't need this meat to live, what justifies the animals' deaths?

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

False dichotomy
Either the land produces meat or it's good for nothing

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Omitting data
Ignores how little livestock produces per area
Ignoring rewilding
Doesn't consider the ecological value of freed land

Biases

Status quo bias

The current use of land seems the only possible one

Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988

System justification

Livestock farming seems the natural order of the countryside

Contradictions

Eats meat from feed-fed animals.

Which doesn't come from grass alone.

Values forests and savannas.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The argument has a true part: many pastures aren't suitable for crops, and ruminants can make use of grass. In some regions and communities, herding plays a real economic and cultural role, and that should be respected in a transition.

But

But the land doesn't need to produce meat. With plant diets, the world would need far less land, and those areas could return to nature, benefiting the climate, biodiversity and the animals themselves.
