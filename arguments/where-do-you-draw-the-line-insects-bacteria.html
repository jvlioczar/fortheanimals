A15

"Where do you draw the line? Insects? Bacteria?"

The answer in one sentence

the difficulty of drawing an exact line doesn't erase the clear cases. We don't know exactly when day turns into night, and yet noon isn't midnight. Cows, pigs and chickens are clearly on the side of those who feel, and the evidence for fish is strong. In borderline cases, such as insects, the answer isn't to give up: it's to act with precaution proportional to the uncertainty.

Short answer

Not knowing exactly where the line falls doesn't mean there's no difference. We don't know the exact moment someone becomes old, and yet a baby isn't an old person. Cows, pigs and chickens are clearly on the side of those who feel, and the evidence for fish is strong; the real doubts are in cases like insects. And in doubtful cases, the sensible answer is caution, not pretending there's nothing there.

For children

When the day is ending, it's hard to say the exact minute it became night. But no one mixes up noon and midnight. It's similar with animals: it's hard to know if an ant feels, but it's easy to know that a dog, a cow and a pig feel. Not knowing everything doesn't mean we know nothing.

Ask a question back

"Since we don't know exactly where childhood ends, is there no difference between a baby and an adult?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"If animals matter, what about ants? And the bacteria you kill washing your hands? Where do you draw the line?"

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1A moral criterion is only acceptable if it lets us draw a clear line between those who count and those who don't.
  2. P2Sentience doesn't let us draw that line: it's gradual and uncertain in borderline cases.
  3. C1So sentience isn't an acceptable moral criterion.
  4. C2Without a clear criterion, there's no basis for condemning the use of animals.

P2 is partly true: there's real uncertainty about insects and other invertebrates. The problem lies in P1, which demands of a moral criterion a precision no criterion has.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1. It's the so-called continuum fallacy, or line-drawing fallacy: concluding that if there's no exact boundary, there's no difference.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument starts from a truth, that there are hard cases, and draws from it a conclusion about the easy cases.

    BeingEvidence of sentienceSituation
    Mammals and birdsStrongClear case
    Fish, cephalopods, decapod crustaceansStrong or realisticClear or nearly clear case (A6)
    InsectsRealistic possibility, with uncertaintyBorderline case
    PlantsPractically noneClear case on the other side (A14)
    BacteriaNoneClear case on the other side

    The animals farming exploits most are in the first two rows. The uncertainty of the middle rows changes nothing about them.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. A fuzzy boundary doesn't erase clear cases.

    Almost every important concept has fuzzy boundaries: childhood, baldness, wealth. That has never stopped anyone from telling a child from an adult. Demanding an exact line from a moral criterion is demanding something no criterion offers.

    2. Science already distinguishes clear cases.

    The 2024 New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness stated that there is strong evidence of conscious experience in mammals and birds, and a realistic possibility in all vertebrates and in many invertebrates, including cephalopods, decapods and insects.

    3. Uncertainty has a method.

    The philosopher Jonathan Birch proposes treating beings with sufficient evidence as "sentience candidates" and adopting proportionate precautions. The question stops being "does it feel or not?" and becomes "how likely is it, and how large is the harm at stake?".

    4. Bacteria and insects aren't the case of farming.

    The argument changes the subject: from billions of animals bred and killed on purpose to accidental, microscopic harms. Accidental, unavoidable harm is different from intentional, avoidable harm (F4, F9).

    5. "If everything matters, nothing matters" doesn't follow.

    That many beings matter to some degree doesn't make them all matter equally or cancel the differences. The right conclusion is to weigh, not to give up.

  3. c

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the line decides who eats what

    The most serious version says that, in practice, some line has to be drawn to decide what may be eaten, and that any line, whether at vertebrates, insects or bivalves, will be partly arbitrary. If vegans also draw an arbitrary line somewhere, why would theirs be better than that of someone who draws it at the human species?

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Not every line is equally arbitrary.

    A line that follows the evidence of sentience, even with a margin of error, is far less arbitrary than a line at the species, which leaves out beings who clearly feel (A10).

    Erring on the side of caution is better than erring on the side of disregard.

    If the line has to have a margin, the margin should protect those who may feel, not expose those who certainly do.

    Doubt about borderline cases doesn't protect farming.

    Cows, pigs and chickens aren't on the borderline.

    Veganism already deals with this.

    It seeks to avoid harm "as far as is possible and practicable", without demanding certainty about every being (L4).

Biases

Motivated reasoning

The difficulty of the boundary is used to dismiss the clear cases

General literature on motivated cognition

Scope insensitivity

Billions of clearly sentient animals get diluted in a discussion about bacteria

Linked to K1

In-group bias

The only line accepted without discussion is the species line

Caviola, Everett and Faber, 2019

Empathy gap

The line tends to be drawn where empathy runs out: the more distant an animal is from us, the less it moves us

Miralles, Raymond and Lecointre, 2019

Belief digitization

A yes or a no is demanded for each animal, when the evidence only allows degrees of probability

Johnson, Merchant and Keil, 2020

Contradictions

Distinguishes clear cases everywhere else.

The same person knows the difference between a child and an adult without needing the exact minute.

Protects their own dog without any doubt.

Uncertainty about ants doesn't make them doubt that the dog feels.

Accepts the species line, which is the most arbitrary.

Demands an exact line from vegans and accepts one that excludes those who clearly feel.

Condemns animal cruelty.

That already presupposes knowing which side of the line animals are on.

What the argument gets right

What is true

The question is a good one. There's real uncertainty about the sentience of insects and other invertebrates, and that uncertainty has enormous practical consequences, given how many of these animals there are. It's also true that any moral criterion requires some degree of judgment in borderline cases.

But

But the existence of hard cases doesn't make the easy cases hard. The right response to uncertainty is proportionate precaution, not indifference. And farming doesn't exploit borderline beings: it exploits cows, pigs, chickens and fish, about whom the doubt is minimal.
