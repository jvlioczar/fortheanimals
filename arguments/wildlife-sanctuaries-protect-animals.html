I14

"Wildlife sanctuaries protect animals"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the word "sanctuary" is not regulated, and many places that use it are zoos or wildlife parks: they breed, buy and display animals, and sell photos and contact with them. In Australia, several "wildlife sanctuaries" are attractions of this kind. Real sanctuaries exist and help animals, but they can be recognized by simple criteria: they rescue, they don't breed or sell animals, and they don't turn them into a product for visitors.

Short answer

The word "sanctuary" isn't regulated, and many places that use it are zoos: they breed and buy animals and charge for photos and contact. In Australia, Lone Pine Koala Sanctuary let visitors hold koalas until 2024. A real sanctuary rescues animals that can't go back to the wild, doesn't breed or sell them, and doesn't let the public handle them. Those deserve support; the others are zoos by another name.

For children

A real sanctuary is like a shelter for animals that were hurt or can't go back to the wild. They live there in peace, not to be held by visitors. If a place makes babies be born so the public can see them, it's more like a zoo than a shelter.

Ask a question back

"If the animals were rescued, why are babies born there every year?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"It's not a zoo, it's a sanctuary. The animals are protected there, and you can even hold a koala."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Sanctuaries exist to protect animals.
  2. P2This place is called a sanctuary.
  3. P3Visiting and paying helps keep that protection going.
  4. CSo visiting this place, and interacting with the animals, is good for them.

P1 holds for real sanctuaries. P2 is just a name: it doesn't say what the place does. P3 depends on what the money pays for.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2: the name doesn't guarantee the practice.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses the name with the practice. A few criteria tell the two kinds of place apart:

    QuestionReal sanctuaryZoo called a sanctuary
    Where do the animals come from?Rescues, seizures, animals that can't return to the wildOwn breeding, purchase, swaps with other parks
    Does it breed animals?NoYes, often with babies as an attraction
    Does it sell or trade animals?NoYes
    Can the public hold or touch them?NoYes, often for an extra fee
    Is the animal at the center?Yes: welfare decides what is doneNo: the visitor experience decides
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The name isn't regulated.

    There is no general law defining "sanctuary". The Global Federation of Animal Sanctuaries, the main body that accredits sanctuaries, defines a true sanctuary by the absence of exploitation: no commercial trade in animals, no invasive research and no unescorted public contact with wild animals.

    2. In Australia, the term is used by wildlife parks.

    Lone Pine Koala Sanctuary, in Brisbane, is a wildlife park opened in 1927 that let visitors hold koalas and pose for photos. The practice ended in July 2024, and other Queensland parks still offer it.

    3. Breeding is not rescuing.

    Organizations such as IFAW point out that a true sanctuary does not breed animals. When there are babies every year, they are the product, not the rescue.

    4. The contact serves the visitor.

    Being held, passed from hand to hand and photographed is stressful for wild animals. The contact exists because it sells, not because the animal needs it.

    5. Real sanctuaries prove the point.

    In a survey of wildlife tourism, the only types of attraction judged positive for both welfare and conservation were genuine sanctuaries. The difference lies in the practice, not the name.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: some of these parks do conservation

    The most serious version points out that some parks called sanctuaries take in injured animals, run veterinary hospitals and take part in conservation programs for endangered species.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The good work doesn't need the bad part.

    Hospitals and rehabilitation don't depend on selling koala photos or breeding animals for display.

    Conserving the species is not respecting the individual.

    The animal on display gets nothing from being the face of the campaign (A10).

    The criterion still applies.

    Where there is real rescue, support it; where there is breeding, trade and paid contact, the name doesn't change what the place is.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question isn't whether the place has a nice name, but whether the animals are there for themselves or for the public. A true sanctuary exists for the animals; a zoo, for its visitors.

Contradictions

And watches babies being born for display.

Says the place protects the animals.

And pays to handle them.

Would reject the same practice with children.

No one calls a place a shelter if it charges to hold the children it takes in.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Real sanctuaries exist, do valuable work and deserve support: they take in animals that can't return to the wild or that were rescued from exploitation, including farmed animals. And some parks do real rehabilitation.

But

But the name isn't enough. The criteria are simple and public: where the animals come from, whether there is breeding, whether there is trade, whether there is paid contact.
