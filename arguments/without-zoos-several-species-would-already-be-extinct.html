I16

"Without zoos, several species would already be extinct"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the argument has real cases on its side. The Arabian oryx, Przewalski's horse, the California condor and the scimitar-horned oryx disappeared, or nearly disappeared, from the wild and only came back because there were individuals in captivity. But captive breeding was decisive for a small share of recovered species: according to a survey published in Science and later revised, it was a major action for 13 of the 68 vertebrate species that moved out of threatened categories. Most conservation comes from protecting habitats. And reintroduction programs involve a small fraction of the animals kept in zoos, nearly all of whom are displayed to the public and never return to the wild. Finally, the argument treats extinction as the worst of evils without asking how the individuals who keep the species in existence actually live. An analogy helps show the logic: no one would accept keeping a small human community confined for life just to preserve their language or lineage.

Reply

There are real cases: the Arabian oryx, Przewalski's horse, the California condor and the scimitar-horned oryx were saved thanks to individuals in captivity. But they aren't the majority: captive breeding was a major action for 13 of the 68 vertebrate species that moved out of threatened categories, and most conservation comes from protecting habitats. Reintroduction programs involve a small fraction of zoo animals, and breeding a threatened species to return it to the wild is different from keeping thousands of animals on display. And is avoiding extinction enough? Would you accept confining a group of people for life just to preserve their language or lineage? Each animal there has only one life, and it matters too.

For children

Some species almost disappeared, and were only saved because people looked after a few animals and then released them into the wild. That's wonderful. But most animals in zoos aren't threatened and will never go back to the wild. And the best way to save animals is to look after the forests and grasslands where they live. It also matters to ask whether each animal is doing well, not only how many of the species are left.

Ask a question back

"How many of the animals in this zoo will ever go back to the wild?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"If it weren't for zoos, lots of animals would have vanished already. Many species only exist today because someone kept a few in captivity and released them later."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Several species became extinct in the wild and survived only in captivity.
  2. P2Captive breeding made it possible to reintroduce some of these species.
  3. P3Without institutions that keep animals in captivity, these species would have disappeared.
  4. CSo zoos are necessary, and keeping animals in them is justified.

P1, P2 and P3 are true for some cases. The conclusion overgeneralizes: it justifies breeding for conservation where it's needed, not the display of thousands of animals from species that aren't threatened.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails in the step from "some programs saved species" to "zoos in general are justified".

Full answer

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The success stories are real.

    The Arabian oryx became extinct in the wild in 1972 and was reintroduced in Oman from 1982. Every remaining wild California condor was captured in 1987, and the species returned to the wild in 1992. The scimitar-horned oryx, declared extinct in the wild in 2000, was reintroduced in Chad from 2016 and reclassified as "Endangered" in 2023.

    2. But they aren't the majority.

    A survey published in Science in 2011 identified captive breeding as a major action in the recovery of 13 of the 68 vertebrate species whose extinction risk fell, a figure revised by the authors themselves after it was challenged. Most recoveries came from protecting habitats, fighting poaching and controlling invasive species.

    3. Most zoo animals aren't involved.

    The same study shows that zoos keep a share of threatened species, but most animals on display belong to species that aren't threatened and will never be reintroduced (see I15).

    4. Breeding for conservation doesn't require display.

    Programs that work usually involve specialized breeding centers, protected areas and planned releases. This can be done without turning the animals into an attraction.

    5. The species isn't the individual.

    Conservation programs look after the species, and not always after each animal's welfare: individuals deemed surplus have been killed in European zoos, such as the giraffe Marius in Copenhagen in 2014. For the guide, conservation matters as a means of protecting the lives of real sentient beings (see A10).

    6. Returning animals to the wild doesn't guarantee a good life.

    Most animals in the wild don't live well: they spend their lives under threat of hunger, cold, disease, parasites and predators, and in most species almost all young die shortly after birth. So, from the individuals' point of view, increasing the number of animals in the wild isn't automatically a gain. Conservation that looks only at a species' numbers can ignore each animal's suffering (see N1 and N2).

    7. Extinction is overvalued when no one asks how the individuals live.

    The argument treats the survival of the species as a good so great that it would justify any living conditions for the animals who keep it going. But a species doesn't feel anything; individuals do. An analogy with humans helps test this logic. Imagine a small community, the last to speak a language or to carry a lineage. We value that language and that lineage, and losing them would be regrettable. Even so, no one would accept keeping that community confined for life, with their reproduction controlled by others and open to visitors, just so the language or lineage wouldn't disappear. We would understand that preservation should serve the people, not the people serve preservation. The analogy doesn't compare the worth of humans and other animals; it shows that, when what matters is someone's good, preserving a kind doesn't justify sacrificing the good of individuals.

    There is serious disagreement here. In environmental ethics, holist views attribute value to species and ecosystems in themselves, not only to individuals, and many conservationists accept sacrificing some animals' welfare for the species' survival. The "compassionate conservation" movement, by contrast, argues that conservation should protect populations and individuals at the same time, and has been criticized by other conservation biologists. Even those who value the species in itself need to explain why that value would outweigh each animal's lifetime of confinement.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: without captivity, these species wouldn't exist

    The most serious version says that for species that only survived in captivity there was no alternative, and that rejecting zoos would mean accepting extinction.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It justifies breeding for conservation, not the zoo as an attraction.

    The cases cited depend on specific programs, not on displaying animals to the public.

    The most effective response is still habitat.

    Without protected areas there is nowhere to reintroduce to, and the species stays stuck in captivity.

    It's a controversial question among animal advocates.

    Some accept breeding programs aimed at reintroduction and at each individual's welfare, and some reject all captivity. What doesn't hold up is using a few success stories to justify zoos in general.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question is whether saving a species justifies keeping animals confined for display. When captive breeding is the only way to prevent an extinction, and is done with the aim of returning animals to the wild and with each one's welfare in mind, the discussion is legitimate. But that describes a small part of what zoos do.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that species like the Arabian oryx, Przewalski's horse, the California condor and the scimitar-horned oryx only exist in the wild today because there were individuals in captivity, and that captive breeding can be the last barrier against an extinction.

But

But these cases are few, almost always depend on habitat protection, and don't justify keeping animals on display in zoos in general.
