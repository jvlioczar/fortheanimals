L32

"Look what you're missing"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the barbecue photo in the comments of a vegan post shows the plate, but doesn't answer the question the post asked: what happened to whoever became the plate. Most vegans have eaten meat and know how it tastes; they didn't stop because they didn't know the flavor, but because they know the cost. And it's curious that those who share the photo of the steak rarely want to share the one from the slaughterhouse.

Short answer

I know the taste; I ate meat my whole life before I changed. I didn't stop because I didn't know the flavor; I stopped because I know the cost. The photo shows the plate, but not the animal. If the photo of the plate can be shared, why not the one of the slaughter?

For children

If someone showed off a beautiful cake made with money they secretly took from another person, the cake would still look nice, but no one would think it was right. Seeing tasty food doesn't change what happened for it to exist. Vegans know meat tastes good; they chose not to eat it because the animal wanted to live.

Ask a question back

"Would you also post a photo of how this animal lived and died?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

A photo of a juicy steak in the comments of a vegan post, captioned "Look what you're missing".

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Veganism requires giving up pleasures most people value highly.
  2. P2No one would accept such a great loss without a very strong reason.
  3. P3Showing the lost pleasure makes the size of the sacrifice clear.
  4. CSo veganism asks too much, and those who adopt it are missing something worthwhile.

P1 has a true core: changing eating habits has a cost, especially at the start. The problem lies in P2 and P3: the reason exists, and the size of the pleasure says nothing about the size of the harm on the other side.

The argument is invalid: from something being pleasurable it doesn't follow that it's just.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The taunt assumes vegans reject meat because they don't know or underestimate the pleasure. But the vegan decision usually has a different structure:

    What the photo assumesWhat actually happens
    The vegan doesn't know what they're missingMost ate meat for years and know the flavor
    Pleasure is the only factorPleasure is weighed against the animal's life and suffering
    Seeing the plate will change their mindCraving isn't an argument: wanting something doesn't make the act just
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Knowing the taste doesn't change the cost.

    The vegan question was never "does meat taste good?". It was "does the flavor justify what is done to the animal?" (J5).

    2. The photo hides half the story.

    It shows the result and leaves out the process. It's the same logic as "I don't want to see those videos" (E12): the plate can be seen; the animal can't.

    3. Temptation isn't a reason.

    Craving something is common for people who've changed a habit, and it doesn't show the old habit was right. People who quit smoking also crave a cigarette when they see one.

    4. "Missing out" is relative.

    The vegan stops eating one ingredient but still eats well. The animal, if eaten, loses everything.

    5. It's often a provocation meant to annoy, not to persuade.

    It offers no reason at all; it just tries to stir up craving or a reaction.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Piazza et al., 2015"Nice" is among the four most common justifications for eating meatThe "4Ns"
    Bastian et al., 2012Being reminded that meat comes from an animal that could feel causes discomfort, and people tend to reduce the mind they attribute to itHelps explain why only the plate appears in the photo
    Brehm, A Theory of Psychological Reactance (1966)Threatened freedom leads to reasserting the behaviorThe challenging tone of the taunt
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the sacrifice shows veganism is too hard

    The most serious version says: if even vegans have cravings, veganism demands constant renunciation, and a morality that demands so much isn't reasonable for most people.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Cravings are common in any change.

    That shows changing costs something at first, not that the change is wrong.

    Cravings fade.

    Food preferences change with exposure, and many vegans report losing the craving over time.

    Difficulty isn't injustice.

    That something is hard explains why many people haven't changed yet, but doesn't make what is done to the animal just (J3).

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Even if the photo stirred a craving in every vegan in the world, the question would remain the same: does the desire to eat a dish justify the life and death it required?

Contradictions

Shows the plate, but not the animal.

Whoever shares the steak wouldn't share the slaughterhouse.

Respects people who quit other habits.

No one sends a photo of a cigarette to someone who quit smoking and thinks it's a good argument.

Admits pleasure is the reason.

By appealing to flavor, offers no other justification.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that changing eating habits has a cost, and that this cost shouldn't be denied or ridiculed. Many vegans miss certain flavors, especially at first, and saying so honestly helps more than pretending there's no craving at all.

But

But the wish to eat isn't a moral reason. What's lost on one side is a dish; on the other, it's a life.
