A14

"Plants feel too."

The answer in one sentence

there's no evidence that plants have experience: they have no neurons or brain, and reacting to stimuli isn't the same as feeling. But even if they did feel, the argument would turn against whoever uses it: animal farming consumes far more plants than a plant-based diet, because the animals have to be fed. Whoever cares about plants should, for that very reason, eat plants.

Short answer

Plants react to their environment, but there's no evidence they feel: they have no neurons, no brain, and nothing that plays their role. And even if they did feel, eating animals would be worse for plants, because animals eat a lot of plants before they become food: about a third of the calories grown by the world's crops go to animal feed. If plants matter, the conclusion is to eat plants.

For children

A plant turns toward the sun, but it doesn't feel the sun the way you feel warmth on your face. It has no brain and no nerves, which are the parts of the body that let us feel pain. A little pig has them. And there's more: to raise a little pig, you have to give him lots of plants to eat. So people who eat plants directly end up using far fewer plants than people who eat animals.

Ask a question back

"If plants did feel, wouldn't it be better to eat the plants than to eat those who eat lots of plants?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"What about lettuce, doesn't it suffer? Plants are living beings too."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Plants react to their environment in complex ways: they perceive light, touch and damage, and communicate chemically.
  2. P2If these reactions are a form of feeling, eating plants also causes harm.
  3. P3If every way of eating causes harm, there's no moral difference between eating plants and eating animals.
  4. CSo veganism has no moral advantage.

P1 is true: plant biology is sophisticated. The argument fails at P2, which confuses reaction with experience, and at P3, which ignores quantities.

The argument is valid in form, but P2 has no support in the evidence and P3 is false even if P2 were true.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument depends on treating two different things as the same:

    ConceptWhat it isExample
    ReactionA response to a stimulus, without anyone feeling itA thermostat switches on the air conditioning; a plant turns toward the light
    ExperienceThere being something it is like, for someone, to be in that stateFeeling pain, fear or pleasure

    Sentience is the capacity to have good or bad experiences. It requires some structure capable of integrating information into an experience, and in animals that's linked to the nervous system.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. There's no evidence of experience in plants.

    In 2019, a group of plant biologists published a review concluding that plants neither possess nor require consciousness: they have no neurons or brain, and there's no evolutionary reason for them to have developed costly mental faculties. The same authors don't deny that plants have complex behavior, which is another matter.

    2. Reacting isn't feeling.

    Plants react to touch, light and damage, as many mindless systems do. The word "communication", used for chemical signals between plants, is a metaphor: it doesn't imply that anyone is listening or suffering.

    3. Even if they felt, eating animals would be worse.

    Animals have to eat plants to grow. About 36% of the calories from the world's crops go to animal feed, and only about 12% of those calories reach people as meat, milk and eggs. Eating plants directly uses far fewer plants.

    4. The argument proves too much.

    If plants' possible capacity to feel cancelled every moral difference, there'd be no difference between eating a lettuce and eating a dog, or a human. Almost no one accepts that.

    5. The doubt tends to be selective.

    Concern for plants almost always comes up as a reply to veganism, not as a reason to change how one treats them. That doesn't refute the argument, but it suggests it isn't the real reason of those who use it.

    6. If plants mattered, eating animals would be the worst of both worlds.

    Animals raised for consumption eat far more plants than people would eat directly. Whoever takes seriously the idea that plants count should, for consistency, eat fewer animals, not more. Taken to the end, the conclusion would be fruitarianism, not a barbecue.

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: we don't know where consciousness begins

    The most serious version doesn't say plants feel. It says we don't know where consciousness ends, that there are theories, such as some versions of panpsychism, that attribute some degree of experience to very simple systems, and that out of humility we shouldn't draw boundaries so confidently.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Humility calls for proportion.

    Admitting uncertainty isn't treating all hypotheses as equally likely. The evidence of sentience in vertebrates is strong; in plants, practically nil.

    Even under uncertainty, the conclusion is the same.

    If there's some chance plants feel, eating fewer plants, which means eating plants and not animals, is still the choice that causes less harm.

    Uncertainty about plants doesn't erase certainty about animals.

    A remote doubt about one case doesn't dissolve what we know about another.

Contradictions

Doesn't treat plants as beings that feel.

No one worries about pruning a tree or mowing the lawn.

Wouldn't accept eating a dog by the same reasoning.

If plants and animals were equal, there'd be no reason to hesitate.

Eats more plants by eating animals.

The concern, if it were real, would lead to eating plants directly.

Only raises the question in front of vegans.

Concern for plants doesn't appear in any other context.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Plants are fascinating organisms, with responses to their environment more sophisticated than was once thought, and research on them deserves attention. It's also true that the science of consciousness has real uncertainties, and that humility is a good attitude toward them.

But

But the available evidence points in a clear direction: those with a nervous system can feel, and those without one very probably can't. And even if plants did feel, the conclusion wouldn't change: the way to cause them less harm would be to eat plants, not animals fed on them.
