M6

"Vegans are annoying and bossy; I don't want to be part of that group"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the experience of someone who tried to be vegan and gave up, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism depended on being easy for everyone, one person's difficulty would be enough to make it stop counting. But someone's difficulty, which deserves to be heard and helped, doesn't change what happens to the animals: it calls for practical support, not a new moral conclusion.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

being policed, pressured or attacked by other vegans is a real experience, and it drives people away. But you can leave a group without leaving the principle: not exploiting animals doesn't require joining anyone, visiting any forum, or even liking other vegans. Animals are not the activists, and the behavior of some humans doesn't change what happens to them. Someone who left because of the people can change company, or keep none at all, and go on not eating animals.

Short answer

I'm sorry it went that way, and you're not the only person who says so: there are hostile groups, and no one has to put up with them. But notice that the quarrel was with people, not with animals. You can leave the group, mute the accounts, never go to another meetup, and still not eat animals. When someone goes back to eating meat, the ones who pay are the animals, not the annoying vegans.

For children

Imagine you like looking after animals and you join an animal protection club. But some kids in the club are bossy and argue about everything. You can leave the club, and that's fair. But the animals didn't argue with you. You can keep looking after them on your own, or with other friends.

Ask a question back

"Was it the group or the idea that pushed you away? Can you keep the idea without being part of the group?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I left because of the vegans themselves. It's constant policing, everyone trying to be purer than everyone else. I don't want to be mistaken for those people."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Some of the vegans I spent time with were hostile: they watched, pressured and humiliated anyone who slipped.
  2. P2No one is obliged to stay in a group that harms them.
  3. P3Being vegan means being part of that group.
  4. CSo I have no reason to stay vegan.

P1 may be true, and often is. P2 is true. The problem lies in P3: veganism is a practice (not using animals, as far as possible), not a membership. The practice can be kept without the group, as many people do.

The argument is valid, but not sound: P3 confuses a principle with the people who defend it.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument bundles three things that can be separated:

    ThingWhat it isCan it be dropped without dropping the others?
    The groupPeople, accounts, forums, collectivesYes
    The labelCalling yourself vegan in publicYes (M7)
    The practiceNot eating or using animalsIt's the only one that reaches the animals
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Leaving the group isn't leaving the principle.

    No one needs a membership card to not eat animals. The practice happens at the store, in the kitchen and on the plate, not in meetings. Many vegans have never taken part in any collective.

    2. Animals are not the activists.

    In L1 the guide already deals with people who reject veganism out of dislike for vegans: it's the messenger effect, judging an idea by who says it. Here the case is more delicate, because the person knew the group from the inside. Even so, the structure is the same: the hen in the cage is no better off because an activist was rude in a comment thread.

    3. "Vegans" are many different groups.

    There are welcoming collectives and quarrelsome ones, people who cook together and people who inspect each other. Online, the most aggressive voices show up more, and stick in memory more, than the thousands of people who simply live their lives without eating animals.

    4. Hostility inside the movement is a real and recognized problem.

    A 2019 study of 17 animal rights activists in the United States, by Paul Gorski, Stacy Lopresti-Goodman and Dallas Rising, listed among the causes of burnout factors from within the movement itself: hostility between activists, racism and sexism in organizations, and a culture that demands sacrifice and looks down on self-care. This guide also criticizes infighting and purity policing (K8, L5, L28, L29). People who complain about them are right to complain.

    5. Those who gave up were usually on their own.

    In the 2014 Faunalytics study, 84% of former vegetarians and vegans weren't actively involved in any group or organization. In a later study by the same organization, which followed 222 people through their first six months of change, social strategies helped most those who knew few vegetarians or vegans. What tends to be missing is good company, not more policing.

    6. There are practical ways out.

    Change groups. Prefer in-person gatherings, such as potlucks and volunteer days at sanctuaries, to online arguments. Follow recipe accounts instead of controversy accounts. Or have no community at all: a recipe notebook and a B12 supplement are enough.

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: a movement is known by its fruits

    The most serious version says: if an idea produces so many intolerant people, maybe something is wrong with the idea. An ethic that makes people worse at dealing with others doesn't deserve support.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    No cause would pass that test.

    Human rights movements, religions, parties and fan communities all have intolerant members. If that refuted ideas, none would be left.

    The principle doesn't tell anyone to humiliate anyone.

    "Don't make animals suffer unnecessarily" contains no instruction to police your peers. Those who police do so on their own account, and often against what the movement itself recommends.

    If the criticism holds, it calls for reform, not for abandoning the animals.

    A hostile movement needs to change. That conclusion is about the movement. About what is done to animals, it says nothing.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Suppose every vegan in the world were unbearable. Sows would still be in crates, fish would still suffocate on boats, and the question would be the same: is it fair to do this to someone who feels, when it can be avoided? The answer doesn't depend on how likeable the person asking is. Someone who left because of the people has every right to protect themselves from them, and can do that without making the animals pay the price.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Ad hominem (against a group)
Rejects the practice because of the behavior of those who defend it
False dichotomy
Either I stay in the hostile group, or I go back to eating animals

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Hasty generalization
Takes a few groups, usually online ones, for all vegans

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Shifting the cost
The complaint is against humans, and the animals pay

Contradictions

The quarrel was with people, and the response hits the animals.

Those who caused the hurt feel nothing when the other person goes back to eating meat.

Criticizes bossiness, but lets the group decide.

If the practice depends on approving of other vegans, they are still in charge of the plate.

In other cases, the person separates things well.

Someone who leaves a gym because of arrogant members doesn't conclude that exercise is bad for you.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that hostile vegans and groups exist, that purity policing hurts and that it drives people away, dedicated activists included. It's also right that no one should stay where they are mistreated, and that the movement has a duty to look in the mirror: welcoming people who slip works better, and is fairer, than humiliating them.

But

But what the argument justifies is leaving the group, not going back to funding what happens to animals. They weren't in the argument, they policed no one, and they are the only ones who lose when hurt caused by people turns into a change on the plate.
