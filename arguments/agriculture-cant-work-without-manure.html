H10

"Agriculture can't work without manure"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the environment and agriculture, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on environmental grounds, low-impact exploitation would be acceptable, even if it killed more sentient beings. And it is already being promoted: insect farming, "sustainable" hunting, "sustainable" leather. It is possible to reduce environmental impact and increase exploitation at the same time.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

agriculture works with green manure, composting, crop rotation with legumes, which fix nitrogen in the soil, and mineral fertilizers. Besides, manure comes from animals fed on crops: it returns to the soil part of the nutrients that were taken out to grow their feed. Without animals, far less land would be needed, and nutrients could go straight from plants back to the soil.

Short answer

Agriculture has many ways to fertilize: green manure with legumes, which fix nitrogen from the air into the soil, composting plant waste, crop rotation and mineral fertilizers. Manure doesn't create nutrients: it returns to the soil part of what the animals ate, and those animals eat crops. Without livestock, far less land would be needed, and nutrients could go straight from plants back to the soil.

For children

Plants need food to grow, which comes from the soil. Manure helps, but it isn't the only way. You can make fertilizer from plant scraps, leaves and peels, and grow beans and other plants that make the soil stronger. That way, plants grow well without needing animals.

Ask a question back

"If manure comes from animals that eat crops, where do the nutrients in manure come from?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Without animals there is no fertilizer. Organic farming depends on cattle."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Plants need nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium.
  2. P2Animal manure is a traditional source of these nutrients, especially in organic farming.
  3. P3Without animals, there wouldn't be enough manure.
  4. CSo agriculture depends on raising animals.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3 and the conclusion: there are other sources of nutrients, and manure doesn't create nutrients, it only returns part of what the animals ate.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails because it ignores the alternatives and where the nutrients in manure come from.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument treats manure as a source of nutrients, when it's a middleman:

    StepWhat happens
    Feed cropTakes nutrients out of the soil
    The animal eats the feedUses part of the nutrients; the rest becomes manure
    Manure returns to the soilReturns part of what was taken out

    Without the animals, plant residues could go back to the soil directly.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Manure is a middleman.

    It returns to the soil nutrients that came from the plants the animals ate, with losses along the way.

    2. There are well-known alternatives.

    Green manure, composting, rotation with legumes, cover crops and mineral fertilizers are established farming practices.

    3. Legumes fix nitrogen.

    Beans, soy, peas and other legumes host bacteria that capture nitrogen from the air and leave it in the soil.

    4. Without livestock, far less land would be needed.

    A global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%. Less cropland means less need for fertilizer.

    5. Vegan farming exists.

    There are growers who farm without animal inputs, sometimes called "veganic", and even a certification for it, the Biocyclic Vegan standard, used mainly in Europe.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Our World in Data, land use and dietsA global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%Based on Poore and Nemecek, 2018
    Embrapa, green manure as a source of nitrogen (in Portuguese)Legumes fix nitrogen in the soil; green manure and composting replenish organic matterA publication by Embrapa, Brazil's agricultural research agency
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: manure is a useful byproduct

    The most serious version grants that there are alternatives, but says that as long as there are animals, using their manure is better than wasting it.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Using it doesn't justify producing it.

    That manure is useful isn't a reason to raise animals.

    Excess manure is a problem.

    In regions of intensive livestock farming, manure pollutes rivers and soils.

    The transition can be gradual.

    Agriculture can reduce its dependence on manure as livestock farming declines.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Agriculture doesn't depend on raising animals to kill them. If there are ways to fertilize without them, the argument doesn't justify livestock farming, and the moral question returns to the center.

Contradictions

Eats vegetables grown with mineral fertilizers.

Most conventional farming uses mineral fertilizer.

Ignores the cropland for feed.

Which also needs fertilizer.

Doesn't demand manure for all the food they eat.

The criterion only comes up against vegans.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Manure is indeed a traditional and useful fertilizer, and integrating animals and crops has a long history. A transition to systems without animals requires knowledge and investment, and doesn't happen all at once.

But

But agriculture doesn't depend on animals. Plants can feed the soil directly, and without livestock, far less land would be needed to feed the world.
