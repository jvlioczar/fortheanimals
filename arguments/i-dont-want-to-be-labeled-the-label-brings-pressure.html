M7

"I don't want to be labeled; the label brings pressure"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the experience of someone who tried to be vegan and gave up, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism depended on being easy for everyone, one person's difficulty would be enough to make it stop counting. But someone's difficulty, which deserves to be heard and helped, doesn't change what happens to the animals: it calls for practical support, not a new moral conclusion.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the discomfort with the label is real: people who call themselves vegan get watched ("doesn't that have milk in it?") and judged by a stereotype. But the label is optional, and the practice is what matters: you can avoid eating animals without announcing yourself as anything. The word "vegan" is a communication tool, handy in a restaurant, not a compulsory identity. Dropping the name doesn't require dropping the practice. And other people's scrutiny is about the consistency of whoever wears the label, not about what is right to do to animals.

Short answer

I get it: once you say "I'm vegan", you become a target for label inspectors and ready-made jokes. So drop the word, if it weighs on you. No one needs it to order the dish without meat or to do the shopping. Just don't drop the animals along with it; they have nothing to do with the scrutiny. The people watching you wanted to catch you in a contradiction. That's a game about you, not about what is right to do to them.

For children

Some people don't like nicknames, and nobody has to have one. If you don't drop litter, you don't need to wear a badge saying "friend of nature". You just don't drop it. With animals it's the same: you can choose not to eat them without using any name. The animal doesn't care about the name, only about what happens to them.

Ask a question back

"If no one ever asked you again what you are, would you go on not eating animals?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"When I said I was vegan, everyone watched me. Any slip became a joke. Now I'd rather eat without giving it a name, and without anyone inspecting me."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1When I introduced myself as vegan, I was watched, pressured and reduced to a stereotype.
  2. P2No one is obliged to carry a label they don't want.
  3. P3Without the label, there's no reason to keep the practice.
  4. CSo I can go back to eating animals.

P1 is usually true, and research backs it up. P2 is true. The problem lies in P3: the label is a name, and the practice is what one does. The reasons for not eating animals lie in the animals, and they are still there when the name leaves the scene.

The argument is valid, but not sound: P3 treats the word and the practice as if they were the same thing.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument answers an image problem with a change of practice:

    Problem reportedWhere it comes fromWhat solves it
    "Everyone watched me"Other people's reaction to the labelNot announcing it, or giving short answers
    "Any slip became a joke"The demand for total consistency (L12)Remembering that the label never promised perfection
    "I don't want to be reduced to a word"The vegan stereotype (L1)Describing what you do instead of saying what you are

    None of the three solutions involves going back to eating animals.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The label is optional; the practice is what reaches the animals.

    To the animal, it makes no difference what the person calls themselves. What changes the animal's life is what gets bought and eaten.

    2. The label is a communication tool.

    The word is useful for reading a menu, letting a host know, or finding a product. Outside those moments, it can stay in the drawer. "No meat, dairy or eggs for me, please" works without any name.

    3. The scrutiny is about consistency, not about what is right.

    Someone asking "but don't you take medicine tested on animals?" is testing the person, not the idea. It's hypocrisy hunting, covered in L11 and L12. Even if the test found a flaw, it would show that the person is imperfect, like everyone, not that animals have stopped feeling.

    4. The stigma exists, and that's where the problem lies.

    In 2017, psychologists Cara MacInnis and Gordon Hodson showed that meat eaters rate vegetarians and vegans negatively, especially those motivated by animals, and that vegans report experiencing that negativity. In 2019, researchers Kelly Markowski and Susan Roxburgh, of Kent State University in the United States, ran five focus groups with college students and found non-vegans who described vegans in negative terms and expected to be judged and excluded if they changed their diet. Wanting to escape the label is an understandable reaction to a real prejudice (E14 shows the other side: people who admire and keep their distance).

    5. Dropping the name is different from dropping the commitment.

    In 2017, psychologists Daniel Rosenfeld and Anthony Burrow proposed a model in which dietary identity has several dimensions: how central the diet is to the person is one thing, and how they think others see people who eat that way is another. The distinction helps here, because the data suggest the inner commitment counts. In the 2014 Faunalytics survey, 58% of former vegetarians and vegans didn't see the diet as part of their identity. In a 2022 study by the same organization, 16% of those who didn't identify with the diet gave up within six months, against 8% of the rest. These are associations, not proof of cause. But they point to a path: you can do without the public badge and keep the decision on the inside.

    6. There are practical ways out.

    Say what you do ("I don't eat animals") instead of what you are. Don't justify yourself at every meal: "I prefer it this way" is answer enough. Choose who you tell. And take the inspector's question lightly: "I'm not perfect, I just try not to harm them."

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: I want to decide meal by meal, with no rule

    The most serious version says: the problem isn't just the word, it's the rule behind it. Labels are straitjackets. I'd rather decide case by case, with common sense, without a fixed commitment that puts me on trial at every slip.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Deciding case by case suits tastes, not harm to others.

    No one decides meal by meal whether to be honest or to respect the neighbors. When there is a victim, we adopt rules precisely so as not to depend on the mood of the day.

    Without a rule, case by case tends to follow habit and the table around you.

    The data on identity and giving up point that way, with the caveats already made.

    The freedom to have no label doesn't cover what is done to animals.

    A person is free to call themselves nothing. The animal is still someone, whether or not there is a name for the person who spares them.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The label, the scrutiny and the jokes are matters between humans. The question that counts sits on the other side of the plate: is it fair to make an animal suffer and die when it can be avoided? That question has the same answer for someone who calls themselves vegan, for someone who calls themselves nothing, and for someone who has never heard the word.

Contradictions

Rejects other people's scrutiny, but lets it decide.

Going back to eating animals to escape the inspectors gives them the last word.

Does other right things without a badge.

Doesn't steal, doesn't lie, and doesn't go by "honestist".

Says they'd rather eat without giving it a name.

And that's possible: just keep eating as before, without the name.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that the label has a cost: it attracts scrutiny, jokes and a stereotype that research confirms. It's right that no one is obliged to present themselves with an identity, and that many people feel better describing what they do than saying what they are. It's also right that the demand for perfection placed on anyone who calls themselves vegan is unfair.

But

But all of that is a reason to drop the word, not the practice. The name is a tool, to be used when it helps. The animals aren't in the label: they are in what goes on the plate, with or without a name.
