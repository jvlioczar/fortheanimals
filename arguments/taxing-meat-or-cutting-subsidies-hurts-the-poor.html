J3

"Taxing meat or cutting subsidies hurts the poor"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

concern for people on low incomes is legitimate: taxes on food weigh more heavily on poor families' budgets. But meat is already artificially cheap. It receives subsidies and tax exemptions, and its price doesn't include the costs production passes on to society, such as pollution, emissions, antibiotic resistance and disease risk, nor the gravest cost, the suffering and death of animals. Protecting the poor doesn't require keeping this system: subsidy money can make beans, lentils and other plant proteins cheaper, and there are mechanisms to return taxes to low-income families.

Short answer

Concern for the poor is fair. But meat is already artificially cheap: it receives subsidies and tax exemptions, and its price doesn't include pollution, emissions, health risks, or the suffering and death of animals. Worldwide, about 540 billion dollars a year go to supporting agricultural producers, and most of it distorts prices or causes harm. That money could make plant proteins cheaper, and there are ways to return taxes to low-income families. Protecting the poor and ending subsidies for exploitation aren't opposite choices.

For children

Some foods seem cheap because the government helps pay for them, and because nobody charges for the damage they do, to nature and to animals. That help could be used to make beans, lentils and other plant foods cheaper, so everyone eats well without animals suffering.

Ask a question back

"If the goal is to feed the poor well, why should public money make meat cheaper, and not beans?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"They want to raise taxes on meat? The rich will keep eating steak, and the poor will have no meat on their plate."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Taxes on food weigh proportionally more on poor families' budgets.
  2. P2Meat is an important source of protein and has strong cultural value.
  3. P3Taxing meat or cutting its subsidies would raise its price.
  4. CSo taxing meat or cutting its subsidies would hurt the poor.

P1 is true, and it's the serious part of the argument. P2 is true as to cultural value, but protein can come from cheaper plant foods. P3 is true. The conclusion, however, assumes the policy amounts only to making meat more expensive, and ignores that the money can be redirected and that there are ways to compensate people on low incomes.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails in assuming there are no alternatives or compensations.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    What the argument saysWhat's trueWhat's left out
    Food taxes weigh more on the poorConsumption taxes tend to be regressiveThere are compensation mechanisms
    Meat would get more expensiveThe price would riseToday's price is artificially low
    The poor would lose proteinMeat is a source of proteinBeans and other legumes are cheaper sources
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Meat is already artificially cheap.

    According to a 2021 report by FAO, UNDP and UNEP, support to agricultural producers worldwide amounts to about 540 billion dollars a year, and 87% of it distorts prices or causes environmental and social harm. Much of it sustains animal farming and feed grains.

    2. The price doesn't include what production costs.

    Pollution, emissions, deforestation, antibiotic resistance and the risk of diseases from animals are paid by society, not by the buyer. These are what economists call externalities.

    3. The gravest cost isn't even counted.

    The suffering and death of animals appear in no price calculation, because animals are treated as the product, not as someone who bears a cost.

    4. The money can be redirected.

    Cutting harmful subsidies doesn't require making food more expensive in general: the same resources can make legumes, grains, fruit and vegetables cheaper, and support new proteins, such as plant-based, fermented and cultivated ones.

    5. There are ways to protect people on low incomes.

    Brazil's tax reform, for example, provides for returning part of the taxes to low-income families registered in the government's social registry. Policies like this make it possible to change prices without penalizing the poor.

    6. A purely environmental tax can make things worse for animals.

    If the tax were proportional only to emissions, chicken and fish would become relatively cheaper, and the number of animals killed could rise (see J9). A policy that takes animals seriously has to include them in the calculation.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: meat is part of culture and food dignity

    The most serious version says that, for many poor families, being able to eat meat is a sign of dignity after years of deprivation, and that making it more expensive would take away an achievement.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Food dignity is real and important.

    It calls for enough, healthy and affordable food, not for one specific kind of food.

    The achievement can be expanded, not taken away.

    Making plant proteins cheaper and securing income broadens access to good food.

    Animals pay too.

    Some people's dignity can't depend on the suffering and death of others when there are alternatives.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question isn't whether the poor should pay more for food, because they shouldn't. It's whether public money should keep making cheap a product that harms society and requires the suffering and death of animals, when it can feed people in other ways.

Contradictions

Defends the poor.

And defends public money making meat cheaper, not beans.

Talks about food security.

And ignores the health and environmental costs of animal farming, which hit the poor hardest.

Rejects a tax on meat.

And accepts that society pays its costs in other ways.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that food taxes weigh more on the poor, that meat has cultural value, and that any change must protect people on low incomes.

But

But meat is already artificially cheap, because it doesn't pay what it costs, to society or to animals. Protecting the poor and ending subsidies for exploitation can go together.
