L5

"Veganism isn't 100% cruelty-free"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's the nirvana fallacy. Veganism never promised purity: its own definition speaks of avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable". Reducing harm doesn't require eliminating it entirely. That no life is wholly innocent doesn't make all choices equal: between causing a lot of harm and causing a little, the difference matters.

Short answer

Veganism never promised purity. Its definition speaks of avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable". Greatly reducing harm is different from reducing none. That no life is wholly innocent doesn't make all choices equal: someone who eats plants causes far fewer deaths than someone who eats animals, including in harvests (H2).

For children

No one can be perfect. But we can try to hurt as little as possible. Imagine two classmates: one pushes others on purpose, and the other sometimes bumps into people by accident. They aren't the same, are they? Vegans try not to hurt animals on purpose, even if sometimes something happens by accident.

Ask a question back

"If no one can avoid all harm, does it make sense to avoid none?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"No vegan is 100% vegan. So veganism doesn't exist."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Even vegans cause harm to animals: in harvests, in transport, in industrial products.
  2. P2Veganism presents itself as a cruelty-free way of life.
  3. P3If veganism doesn't keep that promise, it's inconsistent.
  4. CSo vegans have no standing to criticize meat eaters.

P1 is true. The problem lies in P2, which attributes to veganism a promise it doesn't make, and P3, which treats imperfection as inconsistency.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2 and P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument attacks a promise veganism doesn't make:

    What the argument attributes to veganismWhat veganism says
    Total purity, zero harmAvoid exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable"
    Absolute moral superiorityChoose the option that causes the least harm
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It's the nirvana fallacy.

    Rejecting an improvement because it isn't perfect.

    2. Veganism doesn't promise purity.

    The classic definition already recognizes limits.

    3. The differences in harm are enormous.

    Vegan diets use far less land and cause far fewer deaths, including in harvests (H2).

    4. Intention and avoidability matter.

    Accidental, unavoidable harms differ from intentional, avoidable ones (F4).

    5. The argument works against any ethics.

    No one is perfectly honest or generous, and that doesn't make honesty and generosity meaningless.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    The Vegan Society, definition of veganismVeganism seeks to exclude exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable"Shows purity was never promised
    Our World in Data, land use and dietsA global vegan diet would reduce farmland by about 75%Shows the difference in harm
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: some vegans present themselves as pure

    The most serious version points out that some vegans do present themselves as cruelty-free, and that this self-image is false.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's a criticism of presentation, not principle.

    The principle doesn't promise purity.

    The criticism doesn't favor meat eaters.

    If no diet is pure, the one that causes less harm is still better.

    Honesty about limits strengthens veganism.

    And many vegans practice it.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    That no one is perfect doesn't answer the question about the animals. Among imperfect choices, one causes far more deaths than the other. The question remains: why choose the one that causes more?

Contradictions

Doesn't demand perfection of any other ethical practice.

Knows the difference in harm between diets is large.

Doesn't reduce their own harm.

Even while recognizing everyone causes some.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that no life is entirely harm-free, and that some vegans overstate things by presenting themselves as pure. Recognizing limits is part of an honest ethics.

But

But imperfection doesn't make choices equal. Between causing a lot of harm and causing a little, the difference still matters, and that's what veganism pursues.
