K13

"Everyone helps the cause they care about; you can't compare them"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about effectiveness and individual responsibility, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended only by each person's impact, anyone who proved their purchase changes nothing would be let off. But no one accepts this for other harms: taking part in an injustice is still wrong even when each person's share is small.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

we compare causes all the time when resources are limited: hospitals triage, health systems compare the cost of each treatment, families decide where to spend. There are well-known criteria for this, such as the scale of a problem, how neglected it is and how much of it can be solved. Applied without bias, they point to animals: the victims number in the tens of billions a year and the resources devoted to them are tiny. Choosing a cause out of affinity can be legitimate and keeps people going, but it doesn't make comparison impossible. And the opposite extreme, "always help the most serious problem", is also wrong, because it forgets what can actually be solved and how much each extra resource still achieves.

Reply

I agree that everyone has their own affinities, and that helping a cause you care about is better than helping none. But saying you can't compare doesn't match what we do every day. A hospital triages. A health system decides which medicines to pay for. We compare because time and money are limited. And when we compare, animals stand out: tens of billions are killed every year on land alone, more than a trillion fish, and almost no resources go to them. That doesn't oblige anyone to drop their own cause. It just shows that comparison is possible, and worth doing.

For children

Imagine you have a bucket of water and there are two fires. At one, almost everyone is already throwing water. At the other, which is much bigger, nobody is helping. Both matter, but your water makes more difference where nobody is helping. You can like one side more and still think about where your help does the most good.

Ask a question back

"If you had to choose where your help prevents the most suffering, would you still find it impossible to compare?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"Everyone helps the cause they like. You prefer animals, I prefer children. You can't compare."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Different causes involve different kinds of victims, suffering and values.
  2. P2There is no precise common measure for comparing these kinds of harm.
  3. P3Without a common measure, any ranking of causes is arbitrary.
  4. CSo each person should help the cause they like, and no choice is better than another.

P1 is true. P2 is partly true: precision really is hard. The problem lies in P3: from "there is no precise measure" it doesn't follow that "no comparison is possible". Many comparisons are clear even without exact numbers, such as helping a thousand victims versus helping ten in the same situation.

The argument is valid in form, but P3 is false, so it isn't sound.

There is also an opposite version that shows up in the same debate: "Always help the most serious problem first". It accepts comparison, but uses a single criterion and ignores the others.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    QuestionWhat the objection assumesWhat it leaves out
    Can causes be compared?No, they're incomparableWe compare all the time when resources are scarce
    What guides the choice?Personal affinityNumber of victims, severity, neglectedness and tractability
    Does affinity matter?It's the only criterionIt matters as motivation, but doesn't set the size of the problem
    And the opposite version?Only severity countsWhat can be solved and how much each extra resource still achieves

    The philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha lists three common answers to the question of which causes to support: prioritize human causes; choose the cause one most sympathizes with; or treat all causes as equally important. He proposes a fourth: aim for the greatest good possible with the resources available, which requires comparing causes, problems and strategies (A Journey Through Animal Ethics, vol. VIII, ch. 1).

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Comparing is what we do when resources are scarce.

    Triage in an emergency room decides who is seen first. NICE, the body that assesses treatments for the UK's public health service, compares the cost of each treatment with the quality-adjusted life years it brings, using reference values of £25,000 to £35,000 per quality-adjusted life year. Nobody says these decisions are easy, but nobody concludes they are impossible.

    2. There are well-known criteria.

    Cunha sets out the main ones: the severity of the situation, measured by the number of victims and how bad each one's situation is; tractability, that is, how much of the problem can be solved with the resources available; the size of the benefit each resource produces; long-term effects; tackling causes, not just symptoms; and the degree of neglectedness (vol. VIII, ch. 1; vol. XII, ch. 9). The organization 80,000 Hours sums up the three central ones as scale, neglectedness and tractability, and treats their combination as an estimate of the good done per unit of resources (Wiblin, 2016).

    3. Lack of precision isn't impossibility.

    Cunha acknowledges that the criteria can point in opposite directions and that there are several methods for weighing them. But he notes that attempting these assessments, even without precision, tends to give better results than deciding at random. 80,000 Hours itself warns that its estimates are highly uncertain and should be read with common sense. Uncertainty calls for care, not for giving up. Demanding perfect precision before comparing is a form of the nirvana fallacy.

    4. Applied to animals, the criteria weigh heavily.

    More than 80 billion land animals are slaughtered for meat each year, and an estimated 1.1 to 2.2 trillion wild fish are caught each year (Mood and Brooke, 2024). According to Animal Charity Evaluators, more than 99% of the animals killed by humans are farmed animals, and only about 3% of donations to animal charities in the United States go to organizations devoted to them; the organization itself says these figures are broad indicators, not precise statistics. Cunha adds that most donations go to human causes and concludes that the animal cause meets the priority criteria very well (vol. VIII, ch. 2).

    5. Neglectedness multiplies the effect of each contribution.

    When almost no one works on a problem, there are still many easy, cheap opportunities to make a difference. As more people join, the harder tasks are what's left, and each additional person achieves less. This is called diminishing marginal returns (vol. VIII, ch. 1.7). Cunha also notes that the most neglected problems are often ones most people don't even know exist, because the media and social movements talk about the ones that already get attention.

    6. Affinity is legitimate, but it doesn't measure the problem.

    Caring about a cause helps sustain effort for years, and 80,000 Hours counts personal fit, meaning one's skills, connections and passions, as a factor in choosing a career. But affinity says something about the helper, not about the size of the problem. A person can stay with their cause and still recognize that another one has more victims and less help. Nobody needs to prove they chose the most serious cause in the world; the point is only that comparison exists (J20).

    7. The opposite version is also wrong.

    "Always help the most serious problem" uses severity alone. Cunha gives an example: problem A has 900,000 victims and problem B has 500,000, all in equally bad situations. By severity, A comes first. But if the available resources can help only 400,000 victims of A, or 450,000 of B to the same degree, then B is the choice that helps more. The same goes for the size of the benefit: relieving many victims a little can achieve less than relieving slightly fewer victims a lot. And if everyone rushes to the most serious problem, the second most serious is left with no one (vol. VIII, ch. 1.3, 1.4 and 1.7; vol. XII, ch. 9).

    8. Comparison also applies within the animal cause.

    The same criteria help choose problems and strategies among animals. Cunha concludes that the most serious and most neglected problems are those of animals raised for food, especially aquatic animals and invertebrates, those of wild animals suffering from natural processes, and those concerning the future (vol. VIII, ch. 3). Many people who love animals think first of dogs and cats. And helping wild animals is a topic some vegans treat as "a different cause" (N14). The guide discusses the general framework on the page about Effective altruism.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the calculation is cold and leaves things out

    The most serious version comes from critics of effective altruism. The philosopher Iason Gabriel (2017) brings together the main objections: effective altruism would be insensitive to justice, failing to give due weight to equality, urgency and rights; its methods would carry a bias that leads to mistaken conclusions, for example in favor of what is easy to measure; and it would struggle to bring about systemic change. Besides, comparing suffering across species involves a lot of uncertainty.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It criticizes a method, not comparison.

    The objections call for better criteria, ones that include rights, equality, urgency and structural change. None of them shows that comparing is impossible.

    Cunha's criteria already include these concerns.

    He allows methods that give more weight to those who are worse off and values tackling causes, such as speciesism, not just symptoms.

    Uncertainty cuts both ways.

    And the difference in scale is so large that the animal cause stays relevant even with heavy discounts on each animal's weight (J20).

Contradictions

Says causes can't be compared.

But compares all the time when deciding where to spend their own time and money.

Accepts triage in a hospital.

But rejects the same reasoning when the comparison includes animals.

Says all causes are equal.

But rarely chooses the animal cause, which has more victims and fewer resources.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that affinity matters: people who care about their cause help for longer and with more energy. It's right that comparing causes is hard, that there is a lot of uncertainty and that no one should be shamed for having chosen to help someone. And the opposite version is right that severity counts.

But

The mistake is concluding that comparison is impossible. It is imperfect, but possible, and, done without excluding animals in advance, it shows that they are among the most numerous and most forgotten victims.
