L13

"Vegans shouldn't use artificial intelligence"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the accusation bundles three things: that AI harms the environment, that it harms animals, and that it isn't natural. The first has some truth in it and deserves to be taken seriously: data centres, of which AI is one part, used around 1.5% of the world's electricity in 2024, and that use is expected to double by 2030. But veganism isn't a promise of zero impact: it is the refusal to exploit animals "as far as is possible and practicable". The second accusation is vague, and applies to any technology, including the critic's own phone. The third is the appeal to nature, which settles nothing. And there's an irony: of the choices each person makes, the one that weighs most on the environment and on animals is precisely what's on the plate.

Note: this chapter is about the charge of inconsistency. The idea that an AI's opinion settles the moral question is E8

Short answer

Veganism doesn't promise zero impact. It promises not to exploit animals when that can be avoided. AI does have an environmental cost that is growing and worth discussing: data centres used around 1.5% of the world's electricity in 2024. But livestock systems account for around 12% of all human-caused greenhouse gas emissions, according to the FAO, and kill billions of animals every year. If the criterion were environmental consistency, it would weigh far more on people who eat meat than on people who use AI. And "it isn't natural" doesn't say whether something is right: the critic's phone isn't natural either.

Question back: "If using AI takes away someone's right to defend animals, where does that leave someone who eats meat and uses AI?"

For children

Almost everything we use takes some energy: lights, the fridge, the phone, the computer. Nobody can live without leaving any mark on the world. Veganism doesn't promise that. It asks something simpler: not to hurt or kill animals when we can avoid it. And of everything each of us chooses every day, what weighs most on the planet and on animals is what goes on our plate.

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"A vegan using ChatGPT? AI uses an absurd amount of energy and wrecks the environment. And it isn't even natural. Where's the consistency?"

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Veganism presents itself as a way of reducing harm to the environment and to animals.
  2. P2Artificial intelligence consumes a lot of energy and water and depends on mining and infrastructure that harm ecosystems.
  3. P3Anyone who uses AI contributes to those harms.
  4. CSo vegans who use AI are inconsistent, and veganism shouldn't be taken seriously.

P2 is partly true, and the scale is growing. P1 is imprecise: veganism is, above all, the refusal to exploit animals, not a promise of zero environmental impact. The leap to C is the central problem.

The argument is valid only in the version "whoever causes any harm is inconsistent". Its soundness fails at P1 and at the final leap: using something that has an impact doesn't make inconsistent someone who refuses something else, with a far larger impact and involving the death of animals.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The accusation carries three different criticisms, with different weights.

    CriticismWhat's true in itWhy it doesn't lead to the conclusion
    AI harms the environmentData centres' electricity use is real and growingVeganism doesn't promise zero impact, and the impact of diet is larger
    AI harms animalsMining and infrastructure affect ecosystems, as with any electronicsIt applies to every industrial product, including the accuser's
    AI isn't naturalNothingNatural isn't a synonym for good (C1)
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Veganism isn't a promise of zero impact.

    The most-used definition speaks of excluding the exploitation of animals "as far as is possible and practicable". Nobody, vegan or not, can live without causing some impact. What veganism refuses is something specific: using and killing animals when there is an alternative.

    2. Scale matters, and it points the other way.

    According to the International Energy Agency, data centres, of which AI is only a part, consumed around 1.5% of global electricity in 2024, and are expected to reach just under 3% in 2030. According to the FAO, livestock systems account for around 12% of human-caused greenhouse gas emissions. The two measures aren't identical — one is electricity, the other emissions — but the order of magnitude is clear: if the criterion is environmental consistency, it weighs far more on the plate than on the screen.

    3. The criticism applies to any technology.

    Phones, computers, the internet and the power grid itself depend on mining and affect ecosystems. If using technology with an impact took away someone's right to defend animals, nobody could do it, including the critic (L11).

    4. "It isn't natural" settles nothing.

    Vaccines, glasses, sanitation, writing and medicine aren't natural either. Whether something is natural doesn't say whether it's right; it's the same mistake as "it's natural to eat meat", applied in reverse (C1).

    5. AI can also help animals.

    Researchers already use machine learning to identify and count wild animals in millions of camera-trap images, at a scale that would be impossible by hand. That doesn't erase the cost, but it shows the tool isn't against animals by nature.

    6. Demanding total consistency is a way of not discussing the main point.

    Even if a vegan were inconsistent in using AI, that wouldn't show it's acceptable to exploit animals. The accusation shifts the subject from the victim to the advocate (L12).

  4. d

    The best reply: AI's cost is real and growing

    The serious version isn't the charge of inconsistency. It is the observation that AI's energy and water use is growing fast, that much of the use is superfluous, and that people who care about the environment and about animals should use these tools with judgement.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's reasonable, and it isn't an argument against veganism.

    Using AI with judgement is a recommendation for everyone.

    It asks for proportion, and proportion favours veganism.

    Anyone who wants to reduce impact has, in their diet, a choice with a far larger effect.

    It doesn't touch the moral question.

    The central reason for veganism is not exploiting animals, and that doesn't change with whether or not one uses a tool.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets something right that deserves attention: AI has a real and growing environmental cost, and much of its use is superfluous. People who care about the environment and about animals have reason to use these tools with judgement, and to support demands for efficiency and transparency about energy and water use.

But

But it doesn't follow that veganism is inconsistent. It never promised zero impact, only not to exploit animals when there's an alternative. And of the choices each person makes, the one that weighs most on the environment and on animals is still what goes on the plate.
