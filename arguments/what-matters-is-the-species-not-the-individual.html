A10

"What matters is the species, not the individual"

The answer in one sentence

species don't feel; individuals do. The one who suffers in the slaughterhouse is a particular chicken, not the species Gallus gallus, which, incidentally, has never been more numerous. Worrying about extinction is legitimate, but it doesn't answer the question of what is owed to each animal: we wouldn't accept that the abundance of humans made it acceptable to kill one of them.

Short answer

Species don't feel pain; individuals do. The one who suffers in the slaughterhouse is a particular chicken, not the species. And chickens have never been further from extinction: today, about 70% of the mass of all the birds on the planet is farmed poultry. With humans, no one says killing a person isn't a problem because there are billions of us.

For children

There are millions of children in the world. Does that mean that if one child gets hurt, it's fine, because there are still lots of them? Of course not! Each child feels their own pain. It's the same with chickens: there are lots of them, but each one feels fear and pain on her own.

Ask a question back

"If humanity isn't endangered, does killing a person stop being a problem?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Chickens aren't endangered. As long as the species exists, there's no problem."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1What has value in nature is species and ecosystems, not each organism.
  2. P2Farmed species, such as chickens, pigs and cattle, aren't threatened: they exist in their billions.
  3. CSo killing individuals of these species isn't a moral problem.

P2 is true. The strong version rests on a legitimate environmentalist view that values biodiversity. The problem lies in P1, when it's used to answer a question about individuals.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1: species are categories, and categories can't be harmed. The one who has interests is the individual.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument swaps the subject of the question:

    QuestionSubjectWho can be harmed
    Is the species threatened?The speciesNo one, in the sense of feeling: it's a category
    Is it wrong to kill this animal?The individualThe animal itself

    Concern for species is a question of conservation. The question veganism asks is about what is done to each individual who feels.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Species don't have experiences.

    A species doesn't feel fear, pain or pleasure. Only individuals feel. Saying the species is doing well says nothing about how each of its members is doing.

    2. The abundance is created by the exploitation itself.

    Farmed species exist in huge numbers because they're bred to be killed. In 2018, a survey of the planet's biomass estimated that farmed animals make up about 60% of the mass of all mammals, and farmed poultry about 70% of the mass of all birds.

    3. No one applies the rule to humans.

    Humanity isn't endangered either. If a species' abundance made it acceptable to kill individuals, the same would hold for us.

    4. Conservation and animal ethics answer different questions.

    It's possible to value biodiversity and, at the same time, recognize that each animal matters. The mistake is using one to cancel the other.

    5. Concern for the species tends to be selective.

    The argument appears in defense of breeding abundant species, and rarely to protect the threatened species that farming helps put under pressure, through deforestation and habitat loss.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: nature's value is collective

    The most serious version comes from holistic environmental ethics, associated with Aldo Leopold: what is right is what preserves the integrity, stability and beauty of the biotic community. On this view, the individual matters insofar as it contributes to the whole, and an animal's suffering wouldn't, in itself, be the central question.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Farming doesn't preserve the biotic community.

    It's one of the biggest causes of deforestation and biodiversity loss. By the holistic criterion, farming would be condemned, not defended.

    Pure holism proves too much.

    If only the whole mattered, it would be acceptable to sacrifice humans for the sake of the ecosystem. Almost no one accepts that.

    The environment is a means, not an end.

    Preserving or changing an ecosystem makes moral sense insofar as it improves the lives of real sentient beings (see the Beyond veganism section).

Contradictions

Doesn't apply the rule to humans.

No one accepts killing a person because humanity is numerous.

Mourns the death of their own animal.

The existence of millions of dogs consoles no one who has lost theirs.

Ignores farming's impact on threatened species.

Concern for the species appears only for farmed species.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Concern for species and ecosystems is legitimate, and extinction is a real loss. Conserving biodiversity deserves attention, and there are cases where protecting populations requires difficult decisions.

But

But the question of what we owe each animal isn't settled by looking at the species. The one who suffers is always someone, not a category. The abundance of chickens doesn't make each one's life any less her own.
