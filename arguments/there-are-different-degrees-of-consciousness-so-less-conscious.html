A5

"There are different degrees of consciousness, so less conscious animals matter less"

The answer in one sentence

minds do differ, and that much is true. What doesn't follow is that a simpler mind has interests worth less. The argument runs three things together: what is at stake for each being, the intensity of what it feels, and the moral weight of an equal harm. The first varies with capacities and hardly anyone disputes it; the second is an open empirical question that doesn't track intelligence in any simple way; the third is the claim actually being defended here, and it is the weakest. On top of that, current science does not describe consciousness as a single scale along which species line up.

Note: this chapter is about the weight of an equal harm. Denying that animals are conscious at all is A1, and comparing how much each one suffers is A6

Short answer

That minds differ is not in dispute. The question is another one: why would an equal pain weigh less just because the mind feeling it is simpler? Applied to humans, that criterion would make babies and people with severe cognitive disabilities count for less, and almost no one accepts that. And even if the weight were smaller, it would still have to be compared with what sits on the other side, which is usually the taste of a dish.

Question back: "If moral weight tracks mental capacity, does the pain of a person with a severe cognitive disability count for less than yours?"

For children

It's true that animals are different from one another. But pain in a simpler head doesn't hurt less for whoever is feeling it: it hurts as much as that animal can feel, which is everything he or she has. And between hurting someone and picking something else for lunch, the choice doesn't get hard just because that head is different from yours.

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Not every animal is the same. A chimpanzee isn't a chicken, and a chicken isn't an earthworm. The simpler the mind, the less it weighs."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Consciousness isn't all or nothing: some beings have richer minds and some have poorer ones.
  2. P2The moral weight of a being's interests depends on the richness of its mind.
  3. P3The animals we exploit have poorer minds than ours, and some have very poor ones.
  4. CSo their interests weigh less, and can be sacrificed for much smaller human interests.

P1 is true, but not in the way the argument needs: the variation runs along several dimensions, not a single ruler. P3 is partly true and depends on the species. The central problem is P2 and the leap to C.

The argument is valid in form. Its soundness fails at P2, which no one applies to humans, and at the leap from "weighs less" to "may be sacrificed for the pleasure of a flavour".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    Three different claims travel together in this argument. Separating them settles much of the discussion.

    ClaimWhat it saysWhere it stands
    What's at stake variesA being with plans, memory and an anticipated future loses more by dyingHardly disputed; it isn't speciesism, it's a description of the harm
    Intensity variesMore complex minds would feel moreAn open empirical question, and it doesn't track intelligence in any simple way
    The weight of an equal harm variesA pain of the same intensity counts for less in a simpler beingThis is gradualism's own thesis, and the one that needs defending

    Whoever uses the argument almost always has the first claim in mind, which is reasonable, and concludes the third, which doesn't follow from it. One example makes the difference visible: if two people suffer identical burns, nobody asks which of them has more schooling before deciding who gets the painkiller.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. There is no single scale of consciousness.

    An influential review on the subject, by Jonathan Birch, Alexandra Schnell and Nicola Clayton, argues precisely against the image of a ruler: forcing conscious states onto one or two dimensions means missing important variation. The paper proposes five independent dimensions, among them perceptual richness, evaluative richness and self-consciousness. The upshot is that each species has its own profile rather than a place in a queue. An octopus is not a "worse dog": it is something else. Without the ruler, P3 loses the meaning the argument gives it.

    2. The criterion drags humans down with it.

    If weight tracks capacities, babies, people with advanced dementia and people with profound intellectual disabilities would weigh less than typical adults, and less than many adult animals. This is the argument from marginal cases, or from species overlap. There are ways out — potential, social ties, Shelly Kagan's "modal personhood" — but each is contested, and none is what the speaker had in mind when saying that a chicken doesn't think much.

    3. Less cognition can mean more suffering, not less.

    Understanding what is happening lets you know the pain will end, accept treatment, distract yourself. A being who doesn't understand has none of that. The direction of the effect isn't obvious, and treating it as obvious is the mistake.

    4. The conclusion actually drawn isn't "weighs less", it's "doesn't weigh".

    A smaller weight doesn't license disregard. If an animal's interest were worth a tenth of the equivalent human interest, killing him or her for a taste preference would still be wrong, because on the other side there is no strong human interest, only a few minutes of pleasure (J5). The practice the argument defends doesn't weigh anything: it ignores.

    5. The arithmetic of numbers runs the other way.

    Discounting the individual weight settles nothing when the victims number in the tens of billions a year. Anyone who takes gradualism seriously has to accept that a discount per individual can be outweighed by the number of individuals (N14).

    6. The scale people actually use isn't the cognitive one.

    In practice, the hierarchy that organises how animals are treated tracks familiarity, usefulness and likeability, not mental capacity. The pig, who does well on almost every cognitive measure compared with the dog, is the one on the plate. That suggests the scale is built afterwards, to justify what is already being done.

    7. Even the best-known gradualist doesn't reach the conclusion.

    Kagan defends an explicit hierarchy in which animals count for less than people. And he still writes that they count enough that there is no justification whatsoever for anything close to current practices.

  4. d

    The best reply: limited hierarchy

    The serious reply isn't the dinner-table one. Kagan holds that moral status comes in degrees that track psychological capacities, and answers the argument from marginal cases with the idea of modal personhood: a human baby is someone who, in the normal course of things, could have been a person, and that raises his or her status above that of psychological peers. On this view a pig counts, but counts less, and that would be enough to permit trade-offs we make today.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Kagan himself denies the conclusion.

    He writes that animals count enough that nothing close to current practices has any justification. Citing gradualism to defend industrial animal farming is citing against yourself.

    Modal personhood is contested.

    It grants status to a baby for what he or she could have been, while treating differently a human who never could have been one, and most people don't accept that consequence (A19).

    Degrees require a measure, and we don't have one.

    Without a single scale, saying "counts half" is an intuition dressed up as a number.

    A smaller weight still wins against the other side of the scale.

    The real comparison isn't between a human life and an animal life, but between an animal life and an eating habit that has substitutes.

    Equal consideration isn't equal treatment.

    Singer holds that similar interests should be given similar weight, which doesn't mean giving everyone the same rights or denying that death harms a being with plans more. Rejecting identical treatment is not an argument against equal consideration.

Contradictions

The scale is never applied to humans.

No one proposes weighting a person's pain by a cognitive test.

Eating the pig while protecting the dog.

The scale invoked is cognitive; the choice of dish is not.

Saying the animal counts a little and acting as if he or she counted zero.

A small weight would still weigh more than a flavour.

Treating insects as irrelevant and lobsters as a serious case, or the reverse.

The boundary shifts with local custom.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets right that minds are not all alike, and that this sometimes matters. A being with plans and an anticipated future loses more by dying than a being with none of that, and taking the difference seriously is part of thinking well about the subject. It also gets right that treating every case as identical hides hard questions, especially where the victims are enormously numerous and the information is thin.

But

But the difference between minds is one of profile, not of position on a ruler. No version of the criterion survives being applied to humans. And even granting that the weight varies, what sits on the other side of the scale is almost never an important human interest: it is the taste of a dish, and it has substitutes.
