N7

"Domestic animals are our responsibility, wild ones are not"

The answer in one sentence

it's reasonable to think we have special duties toward the animals we domesticated and that depend on us. The mistake is concluding that, toward wild ones, we have no duty at all. What obliges us to help someone is, first of all, the harm that person or animal would suffer without help, and that doesn't depend on where they live or who domesticated them. A debt or a special relationship can give priority, but it doesn't license indifference.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Reply

I agree that we have special duties toward those we domesticated, just as you do toward your family. But that doesn't mean you owe nothing to strangers: we send help to earthquake victims in other countries, with no relationship to them at all. What makes it wrong to let someone suffer is their suffering, not their address. A special relationship gives priority; it doesn't license indifference.

For children

Your dog is part of your family, and you look after it with special love. But if you saw a hurt little bird on the street, you'd want to help it too, wouldn't you? It feels pain just like your dog. Caring more for our own doesn't mean not caring about others.

Ask a question back

"Do you think we only have a duty to help people in our own city, and that flood victims in another country aren't our problem?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"We domesticated dogs and cats, so we have to look after them. Domestic animals, yes; wild animals belong to nature."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Duties to help arise from special relationships: dependence we created, historical debt, living together or belonging to a community.
  2. P2We have these relationships with domestic animals, but not with wild ones living far from us.
  3. CSo we have a duty to help domestic animals, but not wild ones.

This is a common position among animal protection advocates, and it has defenders in philosophy. Clare Palmer (2010) argues that, as a rule, we have no obligation to help wild animals, but we do toward those we've made dependent. Sue Donaldson and Will Kymlicka (2011) propose treating domestic animals as co-citizens and wild communities as sovereign. Aaron Simmons (2009) and J. Baird Callicott (1992) also draw this line. P2 is largely true. The problem is P1, when it says only these relationships generate duties.

The argument is valid, but P1, in its exclusive form, doesn't survive the human cases we all accept.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    There are several versions, and each picks a different relationship:

    VersionRelationship that would generate the dutyProblem
    TerritoryLiving in "domestic" spaceThe exceptions always favour humans
    Historical debtHaving domesticated the speciesWould give priority, not exclusion
    ProximityLiving together and emotional bondsWould also leave out distant humans
    ReciprocityBeing able to give backWould leave out humans who can't give back
    IndependenceWild animals "get by"Most victims are young animals, who don't get by
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Territory always favours humans.

    The idea that "natural" space shouldn't be interfered with is suspended when the victims are human: we vaccinate Indigenous peoples living in the forest and rescue tourists lost in the bush. "Domestic" space is, not by chance, the space where humans live (Cunha, 2022, pp. 111–112).

    2. The reason to help has no address.

    Why help a dog with cancer? Because the cancer makes it suffer and may kill it. That reason is equally present in a sick wild animal. Whoever adds a second condition, belonging to our territory, needs to explain why it matters, not just assume it does (Cunha, 2022, pp. 112–113).

    3. Historical debt gives priority, not exclusion.

    Even if we had a special debt to domesticated animals, it would tell us whom to help first, not that others don't deserve help. And one can also speak of a debt to wild animals: much of what we enjoy came from transforming nature in humans' favour, without ever considering the animals living in it (Cunha, 2022, pp. 123–124). Having caused a harm creates an extra responsibility, but not having caused it doesn't erase the reason to help, as N6 shows.

    4. Proximity can't be the criterion.

    If we only had duties toward those near us, we'd also have no duty to stop exploiting animals we've never seen, or to help distant humans. What explains why we should help those nearby, the harm they would suffer, is present in those far away too (Cunha, 2022, pp. 210–211).

    5. Reciprocity leaves humans out.

    If we only had duties toward those who can give back, we'd have no duties toward babies or people with severe cognitive disabilities. Since no one accepts this, reciprocity can't be the basis of duties to help (Cunha, 2022, p. 213).

    6. "They can fend for themselves" applies to few.

    Wild animals' independence is imagined from large, healthy adults. But most animals that suffer and die in nature are young. And being independent in general doesn't mean being able to get out of a concrete situation, like a human adult lost in the jungle (Cunha, 2022, p. 124; N4).

    7. Some already think about care at scale.

    The philosopher David Pearce proposes, in texts such as "A Welfare State for Elephants?", extending to wild animals something like the welfare state we offer humans: monitoring, medical care and fertility control, starting with large species such as elephants. It is a long-term and contested proposal, but it shows that the border between "ours" and "theirs" is a choice, not a technical limit.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: special obligations exist

    The most serious version points out that common morality is full of special obligations. Parents owe more to their children than to strangers, and no one thinks this unfair. Domesticating an animal is creating a being that depends on us, and so we have duties toward it that we don't have toward those who never depended on anyone. Palmer calls the widespread idea that we should leave wild animals alone the "laissez-faire intuition". And Donaldson and Kymlicka add that intervening in wild communities would be like one country governing another uninvited.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Special obligations add; they don't subtract.

    Parents owe more to their children, but that doesn't release them from saving a stranger's child they could easily save. Even accepting moderate partiality, what follows is that we have some special duties, not that we have no duty toward others (Cunha, 2022, p. 212).

    A widespread intuition isn't a justification.

    Many intuitions about who deserves consideration have turned out to be prejudices. Cunha recalls that preference for white-skinned people was once a strong and common feeling (Cunha, 2022, pp. 211–212). The intuition must pass the same test as any other.

    Sovereignty itself allows help.

    Donaldson and Kymlicka don't forbid all help: they accept interventions that don't undermine the autonomy of wild communities, like humanitarian aid between countries. That already admits that wild animals' suffering gives reasons to act.

    The debate continues.

    Palmer, Donaldson and Kymlicka, Simmons, Clement (2003) and Everett (2001) are serious defenders of the idea that duties toward wild animals are different. What's in dispute isn't whether domestic animals matter, but whether the absence of a relationship erases the duty toward others.

Contradictions

Donates to disaster victims in other countries.

With no special relationship to them.

Takes an injured wild bird they find to the vet.

Which is already helping an animal with whom they have no relationship.

Supports vaccinating wild animals against rabies.

When it protects humans and domestic animals.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that we have special duties toward the animals we domesticated and that depend on us, and that these duties can justify priority. It's also right that respecting wild animals' lives matters, and that helping shouldn't mean controlling everything.

But

But a special duty toward some doesn't erase the general duty toward others. A wild animal's suffering doesn't weigh less because it never lived in a house.
