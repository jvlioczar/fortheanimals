L24

"Vegans are racist, because they criticize Afro-Brazilian religions"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the objection starts from true facts. In Brazil, religions of African origin such as Candomblé and Umbanda, some of whose rites include the ritual slaughter of animals, are targets of what is called religious racism, and attacking that slaughter while ignoring the Christmas turkey, the barbecue and the slaughterhouses is a selective and unfair criticism. But the phrase generalizes. Consistent antispeciesism criticizes every avoidable killing of animals, in any tradition or in none, and puts the focus where almost all the scale is: industrial exploitation. Criticizing a practice is not attacking a people, as long as the criterion is applied to everyone alike.

Short answer

You're right about something important: a person who attacks slaughter in Afro-Brazilian temples and eats barbecue isn't defending animals, they're picking a target. And in Brazil that target has a color and a history. I don't make that choice. What I say applies to every animal killed unnecessarily, and nearly all of them die in slaughterhouses, for ordinary consumption, including that of my own culture. That's where the conversation starts.

For children

In Brazil, some religions that came from Africa were persecuted for a long time, and even today some people treat them badly. That is wrong. It's also wrong to complain only about the animals killed by those religions and forget the billions of animals killed for food every day. Anyone who wants to protect animals needs to look at all of them, and start with their own plate.

Ask a question back

"If the same criticism is made first of the slaughterhouse and the barbecue, and in the same way of every tradition, is it still racist?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"You only pick on Candomblé. Nobody says a word about the Christmas turkey or the barbecue. That's religious racism."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Religions of African origin are historical targets of persecution and racism in Brazil.
  2. P2Campaigns against ritual slaughter aim at these religions and spare ordinary slaughter, which is incomparably larger.
  3. P3A criticism that only hits the practice of a discriminated group works as discrimination.
  4. P4Vegans criticize ritual slaughter.
  5. CSo vegans are racist, and their criticism can be dismissed.

P1 and P2 are true. P3 is true of selective criticism. The problem lies in P4 and the conclusion: not every vegan criticizes selectively, and the antispeciesist criterion, applied consistently, falls first of all on what the majority consumes.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails because it generalizes in P4 and moves from "this criticism is selective" to "every criticism of killing animals in rituals is racist".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    Two very different criticisms need to be separated:

    Selective criticismConsistent criticism
    TargetRitual slaughter in Afro-Brazilian templesEvery avoidable killing of animals
    What it sparesSlaughterhouses, barbecue, Christmas dinnerNothing: it starts with what the majority consumes
    Who usually makes itMostly people who eat meatThose who apply the same criterion to their own plate
    EffectReinforces the persecution of a groupQuestions a practice shared by almost everyone

    The objection is exactly right about the first column and wrong to extend it to the second.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Religious racism is real, and so is the selectivity.

    Brazil's national human rights hotline (Disque 100) recorded 2,472 complaints of religious intolerance in 2024, up 66.8% on 2023. Among complaints where the victim's religion was given, Umbanda (151) and Candomblé (117) were the most affected; in 1,842 the religion was not specified. Hostility to these temples (terreiros) is far older than veganism and has other roots.

    2. Brazil's Supreme Court ruled on the issue, and it's worth knowing what it decided.

    On 28 March 2019, in Extraordinary Appeal 494601, the Supreme Federal Court (STF) upheld a law of the state of Rio Grande do Sul that exempted ritual sacrifice in religions of African origin from the prohibitions of the state animal protection code. The thesis it set says that an animal protection law which, in order to safeguard religious freedom, permits the ritual sacrifice of animals in those religions is constitutional. All the justices upheld the law; three dissented only in part, proposing a reading that would extend the rule to the rites of any religion and bar cruelty. Several opinions mentioned the prejudice and stigma these religions face. The ruling is about religious freedom and equality before the law; it doesn't say that killing animals is morally indifferent.

    3. There is an international parallel.

    In 1993, in Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah, the United States Supreme Court struck down ordinances of a Florida city that banned the animal sacrifices of Santería. The Court found that the ordinances had the suppression of that religious practice as their object, since many other killings of animals for nonreligious reasons were left untouched or expressly approved.

    4. The scale is elsewhere.

    In Brazil, according to the national statistics office (IBGE), about 39 million cattle, 58 million pigs and more than 6 billion chickens were slaughtered in 2024. At the STF hearing, the lawyer Hédio Silva Júnior, defending the religions of African origin, compared ritual slaughter with industrial-scale slaughter and asked whether the prosecutor himself wasn't wearing leather shoes. Anyone who starts defending animals at the temple has started in the wrong place.

    5. The consistent criterion has no religious address.

    To the animal, it makes no difference whether the person killing them is praying, celebrating or just working a shift. Antispeciesism criticizes every avoidable killing: in the slaughterhouse, in fishing, at Christmas dinner, in kosher and halal slaughter, in ritual. Applied to everyone, and to the majority first, the criterion doesn't single out peoples.

    6. The debate exists inside the religions themselves.

    In 2019 the Brazilian anthropologist Patrício Carneiro Araújo documented Candomblé houses in São Paulo whose priestesses abolished sacrifice: Mãe Solange Buonocore, in Guarulhos, and Iyá Senzarumban, in Embu das Artes. These are minority cases, contested by most practitioners, and the priestesses themselves say the change did not come from vegetarianism. They do show that traditions aren't monoliths (D2), and that this conversation belongs first to those who live the religion.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: in a racist country, even consistent criticism feeds persecution

    The most serious version says: even if you criticize all slaughter, the part that circulates and turns into bills is the part that hits the temples. In a context of persecution, talking about ritual sacrifice, even with good intentions, hands ammunition to those who want to shut them down.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It's right about care.

    Animal advocates shouldn't support laws or campaigns that target only the temples, nor join those who attack them. Criticism of ritual slaughter in isolation, in today's Brazil, serves prejudice more than it serves animals.

    It's about how and where to start speaking, not about the animals.

    Attention to context guides priorities and tone. It doesn't make an animal's death indifferent to the animal.

    Consistency is the best protection.

    Someone who starts with their own plate and with the industry, and only then speaks of any tradition, hands ammunition to no one: the same yardstick falls on the majority first (L7).

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The discussion about religious racism is necessary, and the charge of selectivity is fair for many people. But it is about the critic. The animal who dies doesn't choose the religion, the culture or the lack of religion of whoever kills them. The moral question is the same in every case and, going by the numbers, it is addressed above all to those who buy meat at the supermarket: what justifies killing someone who feels, when there is an alternative?

Biases

Stereotyping

Vegans are seen as a white, wealthy and hostile bloc

Linked to L1 and J2

Identity-protective cognition

Criticism of the practice is felt as an attack on the group, which has historical reasons for distrust

Linked to D1

Salience bias

Ritual slaughter catches the outsider's eye; industrial slaughter happens out of sight

Explains the selectivity the objection denounces

Scope insensitivity

Billions of ordinary slaughters weigh less than the few ritual ones

Linked to K1

Extrapolations

The principle being tested is: "criticizing a practice of a discriminated group is always discriminating against the group".

Contradictions

Denounces selectivity.

But the answer to selectivity is to apply the criterion to everyone, not to no one.

Accepts that practitioners discuss sacrifice within the religion.

Which shows that discussing the practice isn't, in itself, attacking the people.

Usually condemns cockfighting and bullfighting.

Which are also cultural practices involving animals (D2).

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets a great deal right. Religious racism is a fact, with numbers, a history and victims. Criticism of ritual slaughter has been and still is used as an acceptable language for persecuting Afro-Brazilian temples, almost always by people who eat animals every day. And some vegans join in, out of carelessness or prejudice, which is wrong and needs to be said within the movement itself.

But

But what that history condemns is the crooked yardstick, not the yardstick. A criterion that starts with one's own plate, falls first on the industry and holds for every tradition doesn't pick a people as its target. And the animal, on any altar or in any slaughterhouse, is still someone.
