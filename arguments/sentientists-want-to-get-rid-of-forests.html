N11

"Sentientists want to get rid of forests"

The answer in one sentence

it's a straw man. What the ethics of wild animal suffering proposes in practice is research (welfare biology), help in cases where we already know how to act (vaccination, rescue in disasters, contraception in place of culling) and caution. Destroying a habitat causes enormous, immediate suffering to those who live in it. There are philosophical texts that ask whether there is more suffering than wellbeing in nature, and at least one author has argued that habitat loss reduces suffering. But a theoretical question isn't a plan of action, and uncertainty about the answer is precisely the reason not to do anything irreversible.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

The organizations that work on this don't propose cutting down any forest. The real proposals are to study how wild animals live, to vaccinate, to rescue in floods and fires, to use contraception instead of killing. Destroying a habitat kills and injures millions of animals on the spot. There is philosophical discussion about how much suffering there is in nature, but recognizing a problem isn't wanting to blow up the place where it happens.

For children

When we find out that lots of people in a neighborhood are sick, no one thinks of knocking the neighborhood down. We send doctors, medicine and vaccines, and we study why people are getting sick. It's the same with the animals in the forest. Knowing that many of them suffer is a reason to study and to help, not to knock down their home.

Ask a question back

"When someone says there's a lot of suffering in a poor region, do you take them to mean they want to destroy the region, or to help the people who live there?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"You want to pave over nature to end suffering. That's ecocide with good intentions."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Sentientists claim that, in nature, most animals die shortly after being born, and that suffering therefore probably outweighs wellbeing.
  2. P2If the goal is to reduce suffering, and wild life contains more suffering than wellbeing, the most direct route is less wild life.
  3. P3There are authors in this field who have drawn exactly that conclusion.
  4. CSo sentientism, taken seriously, calls for the end of forests and ecosystems.

P1 describes a real thesis, defended among others by the Spanish philosopher Oscar Horta. P3 is true of at least one author. The problem lies in P2, which jumps from the diagnosis to the most drastic remedy, and in the conclusion, which attributes to the whole field what one essay argued.

The argument is invalid: from "there is a lot of suffering in a place" it doesn't follow that "the place should be destroyed".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument mixes three kinds of claim:

    KindExampleWho holds it
    DiagnosisThere is a great deal of suffering in nature, and it mattersThe whole field
    Theoretical questionIn certain habitats, does suffering outweigh wellbeing?Some philosophers, with different answers
    Practical proposalResearch, help where we already know how, act with cautionThe organizations and researchers in the area

    "Getting rid of forests" isn't in any of the three rows as a proposal. The argument gets there by attaching to the second row a remedy the field doesn't prescribe.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The real proposals are different.

    The organization Animal Ethics collects the forms of help already practiced: rescue in disasters, treatment of the injured, care of orphans and vaccination. Oral vaccination of foxes against rabies helped eliminate the disease from much of Europe (N7). Wild Animal Initiative says "we focus on generating scientific research" and names, as a promising area, more humane ways of controlling populations (contraception in place of culling is the most discussed example). Neither proposes eliminating habitats.

    2. Destroying habitats causes immense, immediate suffering.

    Fire, starvation, dehydration and displacement are terrible ways to die. WWF estimated that nearly 3 billion animals were killed or displaced by the Australian bushfires of 2019 and 2020. Anyone concerned with the suffering of wild animals has there a direct reason against destruction.

    3. A theoretical question isn't a practical proposal.

    In 2010 Oscar Horta argued that, since most species produce far more young than survive, suffering probably prevails in nature. His conclusion was that intervening to help animals may be justified, not destroying where they live. In 2016 the American essayist Brian Tomasik went further: he wrote that habitat loss in general reduces suffering and should be "encouraged rather than opposed". It's honest to acknowledge that this text exists. But the rest needs saying: it is a reply to Yew-Kwang Ng, the founder of welfare biology, who argued for environmental preservation as a measure in animals' favor; Tomasik himself admits that burning or paving an area "usually causes severe short-run suffering", lists exceptions and says that evaluating any policy "requires detailed analysis"; and the thesis isn't the program of any organization in the field.

    4. Uncertainty calls for caution.

    No one knows how to measure reliably the wellbeing of most wild animals, or how to predict the effects of large changes in an ecosystem. The philosophers Nicolas Delon and Duncan Purves go so far as to argue that, today, we have no way of predicting whether broad interventions would reduce or increase suffering. Faced with an irreversible action and deep uncertainty, the reasonable course is not to act that way and to study first. That is why the field's central proposal is research (N6).

    5. The human test.

    There are regions of the world with hunger, epidemics and very high infant mortality. Saying so isn't proposing to destroy those regions or to prevent people from being born there. The response we give is aid, vaccines, sanitation and research. Applying a different logic to animals (either accept the suffering or destroy everything) creates for them a dilemma we would never accept for humans (N5).

    6. The ones cutting down forests today are another industry.

    Cattle pasture was responsible for about 41% of tropical deforestation between 2005 and 2013 (H1). Sentientists are among those who most oppose that industry. The accusation points the wrong way.

    7. The philosopher David Pearce proposes the opposite of destroying nature.

    Pearce argues that life in forests, jungles and oceans is dominated by predation, hunger, disease and painful deaths of billions of animals, and that the romantic idea that "wild nature is perfect" ignores the enormous amount of physical and psychological pain they suffer there. The way out he imagines is not to wipe out habitats: it is that the Earth could keep recognizable forms of nature, including large animals, in reserves and parks managed with technology to eliminate cruelty, predation and agony. In Reprogramming Predators (2009), he argues that a biosphere without suffering is technically feasible, and discusses means such as immunocontraception and genetic engineering. These are speculative and controversial ideas, and worth discussing, but they show that the aim is to preserve the nature we know with less pain, not to tear it down.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the logic leads there

    The most serious version says: it doesn't matter what the organizations propose today. An ethics should be judged by what it implies. If all that counts is the balance of suffering, and nature's balance is negative, then less nature is better, and one day someone with power and technology will act accordingly. Tomasik's essay would show where the idea points.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The implication depends on premises no one knows to be true.

    We would have to know that most wild animals' lives are bad on balance, that there is no better way to help them and that destruction wouldn't cause even more harm. None of the three is established.

    Sentientism isn't a single theory.

    It says who matters, not how to calculate. There are sentientists who defend rights, and for them killing and displacing individuals is wrong even if the balance improves (E7). For those who think in terms of balance, the suffering caused by destruction goes into the sum.

    The same reasoning would work against any ethics that cares about suffering.

    Anyone who wants to reduce human suffering could also, "by the logic", be accused of wanting the end of humanity. No one accepts that caricature, because there is another path: helping. That path exists for animals too (N7).

Hasty generalization

Contradictions

Doesn't apply the reasoning to humans.

Someone who denounces a region's misery isn't accused of wanting to destroy it.

Worries about forests.

And often funds, on the plate, the main cause of tropical deforestation.

Accuses of recklessness.

A field whose central proposal is to study before acting.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that the idea of reducing suffering in nature, if applied hastily, simplistically and with power to spare, could cause disasters. It's right that there is at least one well-known text that argues for welcoming habitat loss, and that sentientists shouldn't pretend it doesn't exist. And it's right that the field needs to say clearly what it proposes and what it doesn't, because the subject is frightening.

But

But the limit lies between discussing and proposing. Asking how much suffering there is in nature is a legitimate question, and those who take it seriously have been the first to call for research, humility and small, reversible actions. Standing forests shelter beings who feel, and destroying them would make those beings suffer now, for certain, in exchange for a benefit no one knows would exist.
