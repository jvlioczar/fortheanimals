J21

"A party with only vegan food excludes people who eat meat"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's the other way round. People who eat meat also eat rice, beans, salad, pasta, fruit and sweets without animal ingredients; vegans don't eat meat. A vegan menu is the only one everyone present can eat, so it's the most inclusive. Nobody goes hungry because there's no meat, and nobody is forced into anything: the party simply doesn't offer one kind of food.

Short answer

People who eat meat can eat vegan food, but vegans can't eat meat. That's why a vegan menu is the only one everyone can eat: it's the most inclusive, not the one that excludes. Nobody goes without food, nobody is banned from anything outside the event, and the party simply didn't offer one kind of dish.

For children

At a party with egg-free chocolate cake, popcorn and veggie hot dogs, everyone can eat. If there were only food with meat, some friends would have nothing to eat. The party where everyone can eat is the one that includes the most people.

Ask a question back

"At the vegan party, who was left with nothing to eat?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Making the school party vegan-only is an imposition. People who eat meat are left out."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1At a party or group event, the menu should respect the preferences of the majority.
  2. P2Most people eat meat.
  3. P3A vegan-only menu ignores that preference.
  4. CSo a vegan-only menu excludes people who eat meat.

P1 is debatable: at a group event, the fairest criterion is that everyone can eat, not that the majority eats what they prefer. P2 is true. P3 is true, but ignoring a preference isn't excluding: people who eat meat can still eat everything that's served.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1 and in the step from "not having the preferred food" to "being excluded".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    MenuWho can eat everythingWho is left without options
    Meat onlyPeople who eat meatVegans, vegetarians, people with some religious or health restrictions
    MixedPeople who eat meatSometimes those who don't, for lack of dishes or cross-contamination
    Vegan onlyEveryoneNobody goes without food
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Not having your favorite dish isn't exclusion.

    Excluding means stopping someone from taking part. People who eat meat take part and eat normally at a vegan party.

    2. Vegan food is the most compatible.

    It also suits vegetarians, many people allergic to milk or eggs, and several religious restrictions, such as those of people who don't eat pork or beef.

    3. Nobody loses a right.

    Outside the party, everyone still eats what they want. The event just chose a menu, as it always does.

    4. Many everyday dishes are already vegan.

    Rice, beans, salads, pasta with tomato sauce, fruit, popcorn and many sweets have no animal ingredients.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    This guide, chapter J8Eating together matters for social lifeA vegan party keeps the social side, with everyone at the table
    This guide, chapter E2The freedom to eat what you want isn't affected by a menuThe discussion of imposition
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: a party is also about tradition and pleasure

    The most serious version says the barbecue or the meat dish is part of the party's tradition, and that removing it changes the meaning of the celebration.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Party traditions change all the time.

    Menus change with the times, the place and who is there.

    Some people's pleasure doesn't outweigh everyone's participation.

    At a group event, making sure everyone can eat matters more than serving one group's favorite dish.

    The moral question remains.

    For the animals, a party with meat isn't a neutral tradition.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question isn't whether the party became less traditional, but whether it's fair for animals to be killed for a celebration that can happen without that, and with everyone at the table.

Contradictions

Talks about inclusion.

And defends the menu that leaves some people with nothing to eat.

Talks about imposition.

And accepts a meat menu being imposed on those who don't eat it.

Says they'll go hungry.

And can eat everything that was served.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that food is part of the tradition and pleasure of parties, that menu changes can bother people, and that the choice should be talked through.

But

But a vegan menu leaves nobody without food, and it's the only one everyone can share. It includes rather than excludes.
