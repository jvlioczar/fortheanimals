G15

"My doctor told me to eat meat"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about health and nutrition, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on health grounds, anyone who manages to be healthy eating animals, and many people do, would have no reason to change. And if an animal product turned out to be demonstrably healthier, the same argument would start defending exploitation. The eater's health says nothing about whether what is done to the animal is just.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

medical advice deserves to be taken seriously and understood. If it's general rather than tied to your case, it carries less weight than institutional positions. The US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and the British Dietetic Association recognize that well-planned plant-based diets are adequate. Many doctors receive little training in nutrition. There may be specific clinical reasons for the advice, and they should be understood, but "eat meat" is rarely the only solution.

Short answer

It's worth understanding why. If there's a clinical reason, such as an iron or B12 deficiency, it almost always has a solution without meat, with supplements and adjustments. If the recommendation is general, it runs against the main nutrition bodies, which consider well-planned vegan diets adequate. Many doctors receive little training in nutrition. A second opinion, from a dietitian who knows plant-based diets, can help.

For children

Doctors and dietitians know a lot and help us stay well. But sometimes they each think differently. That's why it's good to ask: "Why do I need this? Is there another way?" Many dietitians know how to plan meals with only plants that keep us strong and healthy.

Ask a question back

"Did your doctor explain which nutrient in meat you need, and whether it can come from another source?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"My dietitian said I need it. No doctor recommends veganism."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1My doctor or dietitian knows my health and has examined me.
  2. P2They recommended that I eat meat.
  3. P3It's prudent to follow the advice of health professionals.
  4. CSo I should eat meat.

P1, P2 and P3 may be true. The problem is that the recommendation may have reasons that allow alternatives, or may reflect a personal opinion contrary to the positions of the professional bodies in the field.

The argument is valid. Its soundness depends on the case: it fails when the advice runs against the evidence without a specific clinical reason.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The recommendation may have different bases:

    Basis of the recommendationWhat to do
    A specific deficiency (B12, iron)Correct it with supplements and adjustments, with no need for meat
    A rare clinical condition that greatly limits possible foodsFollow the advice, with possible adaptations
    The professional's general opinion about meatCompare with institutional positions; seek a second opinion
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Following your doctor is reasonable, but it's worth asking why.

    Trusting the person who follows your case isn't a fallacy. The error lies in turning individual advice, or a professional's general opinion, into a rule for everyone: in that case, it weighs less than the positions of the bodies that gather the evidence in the field.

    2. Nutrition bodies consider the diet adequate.

    The US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics and the British Dietetic Association recognize that well-planned plant diets can support healthy living at all ages.

    3. Medical training in nutrition is limited.

    In the United States, a 2010 survey found an average of about 20 hours of nutrition across the whole of medical school, and only a quarter of schools met the recommended minimum of 25 hours. Dietitians are the professionals best placed to plan a diet.

    4. Almost every clinical reason has an alternative.

    Deficiencies of iron, B12 or protein can be corrected without meat.

    5. "No doctor recommends it" is false.

    Many doctors and dietitians recommend plant-based diets, and medical and nutrition bodies recognize them.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, 2016Well-planned vegan diets are appropriate at all stages of lifeOfficial position
    Adams and colleagues, 2010, *Academic Medicine*US medical schools offered, on average, about 20 hours of nutrition teachingA national survey
    British Dietetic Association, plant-based dietsWell-planned plant-based diets can support healthy living at every age and life stageThe UK association of dietitians
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the professional knows my case

    The most serious version says the professional who follows the person knows details that general recommendations don't consider, and that it would be unwise to ignore them.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Knowing the case matters, which is why it's worth asking the reason.

    If there's a specific reason, it may have another solution.

    A second opinion is common practice in medicine.

    It isn't disrespect; it's care.

    If there really is a need, it's an exception.

    Veganism already allows for what is "possible and practicable" (D7).

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    If the recommendation has a real clinical reason with no alternative, it's an exception, not a rule. For most people, the positions of nutrition bodies show that meat isn't necessary, and the moral question returns to the center.

Contradictions

Seeks second opinions on other matters.

Especially for important diagnoses.

Doesn't follow all of their doctor's advice.

Many people ignore recommendations about exercise or alcohol.

Trusts health bodies on other subjects.

But not on this one.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Following the advice of health professionals is generally prudent, and there are clinical cases where dietary restrictions make sense. No one should ignore a doctor or dietitian who looks after their health.

But

But it's possible to respect the professional and understand the reason for the advice. Almost always, what the body needs is a nutrient, not an animal, and there are professionals ready to help get it another way.
