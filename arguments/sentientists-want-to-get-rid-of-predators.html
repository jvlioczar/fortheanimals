N17

"Sentientists want to get rid of predators"

The answer in one sentence

nobody in this debate proposes going out and killing predators today. Some have considered, in theory, that it would be good for carnivorous species to disappear gradually: the philosopher Jeff McMahan, in a 2010 piece in The New York Times, on condition that this caused no more harm than it prevented. But eliminating predators under present conditions is exactly what the position rejects, because prey populations would explode and hunger and disease would rise — more suffering, not less. What exists is a discussion about the distant future, about whether it will one day be possible to reduce predation without causing harm, through things like contraception, alternative feeding and gene editing. The main site devoted to the subject calls the idea "a proposal to investigate, not a technique ready to deploy". What is actually advocated today is something else: vaccinating, treating the injured, rescuing animals from fires and floods, managing fertility where that is already done.

Note: this chapter is about predation. The idea that intervening is "playing God" is N10, and the fear of making things worse is N9

Reply

Killing predators today is what this position rejects, also for an ecological reason: without them, prey populations would explode and far more animals would die of hunger and disease. What is being discussed is whether, one day, predation could be reduced without causing harm, through contraception, alternative feeding or gene editing. That is long-term research, not a plan. What is advocated for today is vaccinating, treating the injured, and rescuing animals from fires and floods.

For children

Nobody wants to kill the lion. The lion isn't evil: it's just the only way he knows how to eat, and he feels pain and fear too. The question people are asking is a different and very hard one: might there one day be a way for the lion to live well without having to hurt anyone? Nobody knows the answer yet. For now, what can be done is caring for injured animals, vaccinating them and helping in fires.

Ask a question back

"Are you against vaccinating wild animals for rabies and rescuing them from fires? Because that is what this is about today."

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"These people want to get rid of lions. They want a world without predators, as if you could press a button and rewrite nature."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Anyone who takes sentience seriously has to consider the suffering caused by predation.
  2. P2Reducing that suffering would require eliminating or altering predators.
  3. P3Eliminating or altering predators would cause ecological collapse and even greater suffering.
  4. CSo the position leads to an absurd conclusion and should be rejected.

P1 is right, and it is accepted. P3 is right about elimination carried out today, which is exactly why nobody defends it as a plan; those who discuss it in theory, like McMahan, make it conditional on causing no more harm than it prevents. The mistake is P2, which treats "reducing the suffering of predation" and "eliminating predators" as the same thing, and treats a long-term research programme as a plan of action.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P2: the conclusion attributed to the position is precisely the one it rejects, for the reason P3 itself gives.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    Three different things are treated as one. Separating them takes the objection apart.

    What it isWho defends itWhere it stands
    Help already practised: vaccination, treating the injured, rescue from fires and floods, fertility managementPractically the whole field, and environmental agencies already do itBroad consensus, barely controversial
    Research on predation: investigating whether there are safe ways to reduce it in the futurePart of the field, as a research programmeOpen, and explicitly conditional
    Gradual extinction of carnivorous species as an ideal in theoryJeff McMahan (2010), on condition that it cause no more harm than it preventsA conditional philosophical hypothesis, with no proposal to act on present knowledge
    Eliminating predators nowNobodyRejected, for the ecological reason the objection gives

    The objection takes the last row, which has no defenders, and attributes it to people in the first ones.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Elimination is rejected by the criterion itself.

    If what matters is suffering, killing predators is counterproductive: prey would multiply to the limit of the food supply, and hunger and disease would kill more of them, and worse. Anyone who takes sentience seriously reaches the opposite conclusion to the one attributed to them. The honest caveat is that this holds under present conditions. Luciano Carlos Cunha notes that the risk of prey overpopulation assumes nobody would also look after the prey's birth rate, and proposes the human test: if humans were prey, with millions of young per pregnancy and almost all of them dying, nobody would argue for preserving the predators to keep births down; according to him, we would drive the predators extinct and look for harmless ways to reduce our own births (Razões para ajudar, 2022, p. 249). In other words, the rejection of elimination is empirical and conditional, not a matter of principle for every author in the field.

    2. The predator is someone too.

    He or she feels pain, hunger and fear, and goes hungry often. Not a villain to be punished, but another animal caught in a situation they did not choose. Any serious proposal has to include their welfare, which already rules out violent solutions.

    3. It is a research proposal, not a plan of action.

    The main site devoted to the subject, Herbivorize Predators, presents itself as "a proposal to investigate, not a technique ready to deploy", speaks of change happening gradually across generations, and states that no general solution has been demonstrated. David Pearce, author of the best-known proposal, writes about technologies that would be decades away and treats the matter as a long-term question.

    4. Pearce doesn't advocate exterminating predators.

    Reprogramming Predators discusses depot contraception, cultivated meat for carnivores and gene editing, not killing. He does say the case for selective extinction "isn't absurd", and that centuries from now lions may exist only in digital archives, but he adds that "prediction is not advocacy". Attributing an extermination plan to him swaps what was written for a caricature.

    5. We already intervene constantly, and nobody calls it madness.

    Oral-bait rabies vaccination of foxes, rescuing animals from fires and oil spills, treating the injured, controlling invasive species, fertility management in elephants and wild horses: all of it is intervention in nature, already done and widely accepted. What changes, in the position being criticised, is only the reason: the good of the animals themselves rather than of the landscape.

    6. The scale of the problem is what motivates the question.

    Suffering in nature is immense, above all because of the very high mortality of young in most species, who die shortly after birth of hunger, cold, disease and predation. Concluding that predation must be altered isn't obligatory. Not finding the question ridiculous is, for anyone who thinks animals matter.

    7. Caution is the field's own position, not an objection to it.

    Those who argue for helping wild animals usually insist on prior research, tested interventions, reversibility and small scale before anything large, precisely because of the risk of making things worse (N9). The objection presents as criticism what is already the internal rule.

    8. Someone did argue in theory for the end of carnivores, and it's worth knowing how.

    In 2010 the philosopher Jeff McMahan published the essay "The Meat Eaters" in The New York Times. In it he suggested that it would be good, in principle, for carnivorous species to be gradually driven extinct or genetically altered so that they no longer prey, provided this could be done without an ecological upheaval causing more harm than it prevented. He didn't propose acting on present knowledge, and he answered his critics in a second piece days later. The essay provoked a strong reaction, above all over the ecological risk. Saying that "nobody" thinks about this would be false; the accurate thing to say is that nobody proposes doing it now, and that those who discuss the idea make it conditional on not making things worse.

    9. We already accept intervening in predation, as long as the reason isn't the prey's good.

    We defend ourselves and other humans from predators, farmers protect their herds, and environmental agencies reintroduce predators or cull those that threaten rare species. Cunha calls this the "specific arrangement": rejection only appears when the predation is between wild animals and the aim is their own good (p. 158). Whoever finds intervening absurd has to explain why only this case would be.

    10. Not intervening can be the worst outcome.

    Oscar Horta proposed an example, reconstructed by Cunha (pp. 166–167): a fox is about to catch a rabbit, and we have two packets of plant food. Among the options, letting the predation happen and eating one packet produces a death with suffering; giving the second packet to the fox produces no death at all. The example shows that there is nothing wrong in itself with intervening. It is controversial: Horta also ranks options that involve killing and eating an animal, which most animal advocates would reject, and Cunha himself records that caveat. So it serves to show that intervention isn't absurd in principle, not as a recommendation.

    11. A "right to prey" becomes the law of the strongest.

    If the predator had a right to hunt, the prey couldn't defend themselves, and nobody could defend them. A right that only protects one side is arbitrary; a right to survival would protect both, and would call for research into alternative feeding (pp. 168–169). And those who hold rights theories can't appeal to long-term consequences, such as a prey explosion, to say intervening is absurd, since those theories don't decide by consequences (p. 169).

    12. Concern for the predator only appears when the victim isn't human.

    If it were humans being hunted, few would hesitate to stop the hunter. The philosopher Eze Paez (2015) observes that the abundance of defences of the predator when the victims are animals reveals speciesism. And feeding, sterilizing or genetically altering a predator needn't harm them, or harms them far less than predation harms the prey (Cunha, pp. 167–168).

    13. Defending third parties is accepted, even by critics.

    The philosopher Paul Taylor, who argued for respect for nature and against intervention, accepts the defence of third parties, but only when the victim is a moral agent. Cunha considers that restriction arbitrary (pp. 162–163).

    14. The aim isn't to reduce predation but total suffering.

    The "predation paradox" objection says that saving one prey animal leaves alive an animal who will prey on others, and so on. It assumes the goal is to count predations, when what one wants to reduce is suffering and deaths in general, including from starvation (pp. 249–250). And even those who reject intervening in ongoing cases can agree that it would be better to prevent new cases from arising (p. 171).

    15. There are no vacant niches: one more reason for caution.

    Discussing the flooding of a valley, Peter Singer notes in Practical Ethics that displaced animals don't easily find somewhere else to live, because a territory able to support an animal is usually already taken, and that most of them starve or drown (ch. 10). The transfer is this guide's: abrupt changes to an ecosystem, such as removing the predators from a region, don't leave the survivors in peace, and can lead many animals to starve when the population grows beyond the available food. It's one more reason, within sentientism itself, for the caution described in points 1 and 7.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the ecological risk

    The serious version isn't the caricature. It is the argument that ecosystems are webs of dependencies we understand poorly, that changes at one level produce cascading effects, and that the history of human intervention in nature is full of well-meant disasters. Given that, touching predation would be far too risky, today and perhaps always.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It is an argument about method, not about value.

    It grants that suffering matters and discusses how to act, which is exactly the discussion being proposed.

    It works against large interventions, not small ones.

    Vaccinating, treating and rescuing don't bring down ecosystems, and are already done.

    It recommends research, not silence.

    If the problem is not knowing enough, the answer is to study, which is what the proposal asks for.

    The risk of acting has to be compared with the risk of not acting.

    Not intervening has a cost too, paid in full by the animals, and it isn't zero just because it is what already happens.

Contradictions

It accepts control of invasive species and population management.

Which is direct intervention, and often lethal.

It vaccinates wild animals against rabies.

Mass intervention, accepted without controversy.

It rescues animals from fires and oil spills.

Interfering with the "natural" course of things.

It thinks killing predators is wrong.

Which is also the conclusion of the people being accused.

It accepts culling predators that threaten herds or rare species.

And only finds intervening absurd when the one helped would be the prey.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets several things right, and they are worth naming. Ecosystems are complex, cascading effects are real, and the history of human intervention in nature includes serious mistakes made with good intentions. It also gets right that the idea of reprogramming predators sounds strange, and that some present it with more confidence than the evidence allows. Pearce does entertain selective extinction as a discussable hypothesis, even while not advocating it, and McMahan suggested in 2010 that the gradual extinction of carnivores would be good in principle, if it caused no more harm; it is understandable that this alarms people.

But

But the objection draws the wrong conclusion. The position being criticised rejects killing predators for the very ecological reason the objection raises, counts the predator's welfare too, and treats predation as a research question rather than a plan. Meanwhile, what is actually advocated is a good deal less exotic: vaccinate, treat, rescue, and study before doing anything larger.
