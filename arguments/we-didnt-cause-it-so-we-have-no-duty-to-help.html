M3

"We didn't cause it, so we have no duty to help"

The answer in one sentence

the difference between causing harm and failing to prevent it may carry some moral weight, but it doesn't erase the duty to help when helping is possible and costs little. No one would accept this reasoning about a drowning child or flood victims. The feeling that omission is innocent is a known bias, omission bias, and it weighs more heavily when the victims are animals.

Short answer

No one threw the child into the pond, and yet everyone thinks whoever can save it without great risk should save it. Not having caused suffering doesn't release us from helping when we can. And for the victim, it makes no difference whether the harm came from someone or from nature.

For children

If you saw a kitten stuck in a drain, you weren't the one who put it there. Even so, you'd want to call someone to get it out, wouldn't you? Not having caused the problem doesn't mean we can't help. Sometimes helping is the most important thing.

Ask a question back

"If you saw a child drowning and could save them without risk, would you say it isn't your duty because you weren't the one who pushed them in?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I didn't do anything to these animals. If they suffer in nature, it's not my problem."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1We have a strong duty not to cause harm to others.
  2. P2The duty to help those who suffer from causes we didn't create is, at most, weak and optional.
  3. P3The suffering of wild animals wasn't caused by us.
  4. CSo we have no duty to help them.

P1 and P3 are acceptable. P2 has serious defenders in philosophy, under the name of the doing/allowing distinction. The problem is drawing from it the conclusion that there's no duty at all.

The argument is valid, but P2, in its strong form, doesn't survive the human cases we all accept.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument depends on turning a difference of degree into an all-or-nothing difference:

    PositionWhat it saysConsequence
    Doing and allowing weigh the sameFailing to save is as serious as killingEnormous duties to help
    Doing weighs more than allowingCausing harm is worse than failing to prevent it, but failing still countsReal duties to help, proportional to cost and benefit
    Allowing weighs nothingThere's only a duty not to causeNo one would need to save the child in the pond

    The argument needs the third position, which almost no one accepts when the victim is human.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The child in the pond.

    Peter Singer proposed in 1972: if you can save a child drowning in a shallow pond at the cost of muddying your clothes, you should save it. Almost everyone agrees, and no one caused the drowning.

    2. Omission seems lighter than it is.

    Psychology experiments show that people judge harm caused by omission as less serious than the same harm caused by action, even when outcome and intention are equal. This is omission bias. It explains the feeling of innocence, but doesn't justify it.

    3. The human test.

    Faced with a flood, an earthquake or an epidemic, we accept that governments, organizations and individuals should help, even though they didn't cause the disaster. No one says helping victims is optional because the rain is to blame. If we deny this reasoning to animals, the victims' species is doing the deciding.

    4. Even defenders of the distinction accept helping.

    Those who hold that duties not to harm are stronger usually accept there are reasons to help when the cost is small and the benefit large. The scale of suffering in nature makes those reasons strong.

    5. Helping doesn't require heroism.

    The duty to help can start with the simplest things: not opposing those who help, supporting research, and arguing that the wellbeing of wild animals be considered in decisions we already make.

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the duty to help has no limit

    The most serious version says: if failing to help counts, we'd be guilty of all the suffering we could have prevented in the world, human and animal. Such a morality would be impossible to fulfil. The doing/allowing distinction exists precisely to give our obligations a reasonable limit.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    A limit isn't zero.

    It's reasonable for the duty to help to have cost limits. That doesn't lead to the conclusion that there's no duty at all.

    We apply reasonable limits to humans.

    We don't require each person to give everything they have, but we expect them to help when it's easy. The same standard, applied to animals, already asks far more than we do today.

    The first step costs almost nothing.

    Taking the problem seriously, supporting research and not obstructing those who help are well within any reasonable limit.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

False dilemma
Either the duty to help equals the duty not to harm, or it doesn't exist

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Omission bias
Treats omission as innocent because it seems less serious

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Double standard
Accepts the duty of rescue for humans and denies it for animals
Focus on the agent
Asks only "who caused it?" and forgets "who suffers?"

Biases

Omission bias

Not acting seems less serious than acting

Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991

Intention bias

Harm only seems serious when someone meant to cause it

Tomasik, "Intention-based moral reactions distort intuitions about wild animals"

In-group bias

The duty of rescue feels strong only for humans

Caviola, Everett and Faber, 2019

Contradictions

Thinks the child in the pond should be saved.

Without having caused the drowning.

Gives to or supports disaster relief.

Without having caused the disaster.

Approves of rescuing animals in floods and fires.

Which already admits a duty to help animals one didn't harm.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that causing harm is usually worse than failing to prevent it, and that our duties to help need reasonable limits. It's also right that, for exploited animals, the first duty is to stop harming them.

But

But "worse" doesn't mean "the only one". When we can relieve enormous suffering at no great cost, not having been the cause isn't an answer, for humans or for animals.
