N3

"A balanced nature is good for animals"

The answer in one sentence

ecological balance is a measure of numbers and stability, not of wellbeing. In most species, balance is maintained by enormous mortality among the young. And environmental programmes kill thousands or millions of "invasive" animals in the name of that balance, for goals we would never accept if the victims were human. Protecting ecosystems can coincide with helping animals, but it isn't the same thing.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

Balance measures how many animals there are, not how they live. A stable population can be maintained by thousands of young dying of hunger and predation every year. And environmental programmes kill thousands of "invasive" animals to maintain certain balances. Sometimes protecting nature helps animals, but the criterion has to be their wellbeing, not balance in itself.

For children

Imagine a "balanced" pond: every year thousands of tadpoles hatch, and almost all are eaten or die before becoming frogs. The number of frogs stays exactly the same every year. The pond is in balance, but most of the tadpoles didn't have a good life. Balance doesn't mean each little animal is well.

Ask a question back

"If a region's balance depended on eliminating a group of people who'd arrived from elsewhere, would you accept it?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"If nature is in balance, the animals are fine. The problem is invasive species and human destruction."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Balanced, biodiverse ecosystems are stable and resilient.
  2. P2Stable ecosystems provide the conditions for animals to live.
  3. P3Invasive species and human action unbalance ecosystems.
  4. CSo protecting balance, including eliminating invasive species, is the best we can do for animals.

P1 and P3 are generally true. The problem lies in P2 and the conclusion: a stable ecosystem may be stable precisely because the vast majority of animals die young.

The argument is invalid as an argument about animals: from the premises follows something about ecosystems, not about individual wellbeing.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument uses the same word for different things:

    GoalWhat it measuresGood for whom
    Ecological balanceStability of populations and of relations between speciesThe ecosystem, as a system
    BiodiversityNumber and variety of speciesThe maintenance of species
    Animal wellbeingHow each animal lives and feelsEach sentient individual

    The three can coincide, but often don't. Environmentalism, as a moral view, values the first two; sentientism, the third.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Balance hides mortality.

    In most species, each female produces far more young than the environment can support. The population stays stable because almost all die young. Balance is the result of that mortality, not its solution (N1).

    2. Environmental programmes kill animals for non-sentient goals.

    Eradications of cats, rats, foxes, deer and wild boar are carried out in the name of protecting native species. New Zealand, for example, has a national goal of eliminating its most harmful introduced predators by 2050. The suffering of the animals killed rarely counts as a cost.

    3. "Native" and "invasive" aren't criteria of who feels.

    A rat doesn't suffer less for having arrived on a ship. Valuing an animal's origin above its life is using a criterion unrelated to the capacity to feel.

    4. The human test.

    No one would accept eliminating a human population because it came from elsewhere and altered a region's balance. Environmentalists, in fact, limit their goals when those goals would harm humans, but not when they harm animals. That's giving humans special weight without justification (A8).

    5. Sometimes the goals coincide, and that's good.

    Protecting habitats can reduce some suffering. But to know when this happens, we have to look at animals' wellbeing, not assume that balance already is wellbeing.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: without ecosystems, there are no animals

    The most serious version says: animals' wellbeing depends on healthy habitats. Without functioning ecosystems, there would be no animals living well or badly. Protecting balance is protecting the foundation of everything.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    A foundation isn't a goal.

    That animals depend on habitats doesn't mean every stable habitat is good for them, or that any price in lives is worth paying to keep it.

    The question is still about individuals.

    How an ecosystem is maintained matters: some ways increase and some reduce the suffering of those who live in it.

    The environment is a means, not an end.

    From a sentientist point of view, an ecosystem's value lies in what it means for the beings who feel.

Contradictions

Says they love animals.

And supports programmes that kill thousands of them.

Rejects eliminating humans to preserve ecosystems.

Which shows balance isn't the supreme value.

Is moved by the suffering of filmed wild animals.

But not by that of those who die to maintain balance.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that habitat destruction is a serious problem, that introduced species can cause much suffering and death, and that stable ecosystems can, in many cases, be better for animals than devastated ones.

But

But the sentientist question is different: how do the animals in this ecosystem live and feel? When the goals coincide, good. When they don't, the wellbeing of those who feel can't be sacrificed for an abstraction.
