I28

"Police and detection dogs are well cared for and save lives"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the usefulness of testing, clothing, entertainment, work and companion animals, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the defense of animals depended on showing that testing, clothing or entertainment with animals are useless, one useful use would be enough to justify them. But usefulness does not settle the question: no benefit justifies using people who did not consent, and the same holds for animals.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

police, military and detection dogs often have a strong bond with their handlers and save human lives. But they are put at risk without being able to choose, in situations none of them would understand: explosives, shootings, collapsed buildings. And the care depends on usefulness: until 2000, in the United States, retired military dogs could not be adopted, and many were put down; only a law passed that year opened the way to adoption. Recognizing their work doesn't answer the question of using them.

Short answer

Many police and detection dogs have a strong bond with their handlers and save human lives. But they are put at risk, explosives, shootings, collapsed buildings, without being able to choose or understand that risk. And the care depends on usefulness: until 2000, in the United States, retired military dogs could not be adopted, and many were put down; only a law passed that year opened the way to adoption. Recognizing their work doesn't answer whether it's fair to use them, and it certainly doesn't justify other uses of animals.

For children

Sniffer dogs have amazing noses and help find dangerous things. Many are loved by their human partners. But they don't know they're near a bomb or a shooting: they didn't choose to run that risk. You can admire the dog and still ask whether it's fair to put it there.

Ask a question back

"If it were a person unable to understand the risk, would sending them ahead to find the bomb be acceptable?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"A bomb-sniffing dog is a hero, treated as part of the team. Are you going to say that's exploitation?"

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Police and detection dogs save human lives.
  2. P2They are well cared for and bond with their handlers.
  3. P3If a use saves lives and the animal is treated well, it's acceptable.
  4. CSo using dogs in policing and detection is acceptable, and calling it exploitation is going too far.

P1 is true in many cases. So is P2. P3 ignores the risk imposed on someone who can't consent and what happens when the animal stops being useful.

The argument is valid. Its soundness depends on P3, which is debatable.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The objection lumps together different uses with different risks:

    UseWhat the dog faces
    Drug detection at airportsRoutine, transport, repetitive work; relatively low risk
    Explosives detectionBeing near bombs and mines
    Attack and apprehension dogsPhysical confrontation with people, risk of injury and of being shot
    Military dogsConflict zones, heat, injuries, stress
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The risk is imposed on someone who can't consent.

    The dog doesn't know what a bomb or a shooting is. It's trained for a task whose risk it doesn't understand.

    2. The care depends on usefulness.

    Until 2000, in the United States, retired military dogs could not be adopted, and many were put down. Robby's Law, signed in November that year, allowed them to be transferred to or adopted by former handlers, law enforcement agencies or families. This shows the care didn't always come from recognizing the dog's interests.

    3. Attack dogs also harm people.

    Using dogs to restrain suspects causes serious bites and is debated as a human rights issue in several countries.

    4. One useful use doesn't justify others.

    Even if explosives detection were fully justified, that would say nothing about breeding and killing animals for food when there are alternatives.

    5. It's a changing field.

    Adoption programs, age limits, rules of use and detection technologies can reduce dependence on dogs. The question of the best way forward remains open.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: better to risk a dog than a person

    The most serious version says that, faced with a bomb, someone has to take the risk, and it's less serious to risk a well-trained dog than a human being.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It assumes the dog's life is worth less.

    That's exactly what's under discussion, and it can't serve as a premise (A13).

    Even granting it, it only holds for some uses.

    It doesn't justify attack dogs, nor dogs discarded at the end of their service.

    It doesn't extend to the plate.

    Risking a dog to save lives is a tragic choice; breeding animals for food, when there are alternatives, isn't.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The case of working dogs is hard, and deserves to be treated as such. But the difficulty shows the criterion: real need and the animal's interests have to be part of the calculation. Applied to breeding for slaughter, the same criterion leaves no room.

Logical

Is the form valid?

Contradictions

Calls the dog a hero.

And accepted that it be put down on retiring.

Says it's part of the team.

But no human team member is sent in without knowing the risk.

Uses the extreme case as an argument.

And stretches it to uses where no life is at stake.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that detection dogs save lives, that many handlers love their dogs and that, today, most retired dogs can be adopted. It's also true that this is a hard case, with human lives at stake.

But

But a hard case, which calls for careful rules, doesn't license the cases where there's no life to save.
