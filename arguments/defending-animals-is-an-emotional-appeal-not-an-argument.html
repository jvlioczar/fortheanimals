L34

"Defending animals is an emotional appeal, not an argument"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about who defends veganism, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If the question depended on who defends animals, vegans being pleasant and consistent would make the argument valid, and being annoying and inconsistent would make it invalid. But the animals suffer just the same, whoever the messenger is.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

emotion and reason aren't on opposite sides of this debate. The main arguments for animals start from criteria and facts, such as the capacity to feel, not from a feeling: science today recognizes strong evidence of conscious experience in mammals and birds. Emotion often sets moral reflection in motion, and it's legitimate for a campaign to move people; but what decides is whether the reasons hold up. Besides, the defense of consumption also rests on emotions, such as attachment, pleasure and habit. If feeling something disqualified a position, it would disqualify both sides.

Short answer

I agree that emotion alone doesn't decide anything. That's why the argument isn't "I feel sad, so it's wrong". It's this: animals feel pain and fear, which science has well established, and causing that suffering without need is wrong for the same reasons it would be with a human. The images just show that it happens. And people who defend eating meat use emotion too: pleasure, tradition, the family barbecue. So let's discuss the reasons on both sides.

For children

Imagine you see a classmate push another one to the ground, and you feel sad and angry. Someone says: "That's just what you're feeling." But the push happened, and the one who fell got hurt. You felt sad precisely because you saw something wrong. The feeling didn't invent the hurt; it helped you notice it.

Ask a question back

"Leaving emotion aside, which part of the argument do you think is false: that animals feel, or that making them suffer without need is wrong?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"That's sentimentality. You show videos of poor little animals suffering, but you have no argument."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1The defense of animals relies mainly on shocking images, cute animals and outrage.
  2. P2Emotions are poor guides to deciding what is just: they vary from person to person, favor the pretty animals and can be manipulated.
  3. P3A position that depends on emotions needn't be taken seriously as an argument.
  4. CSo the defense of animals can be dismissed.

P2 has a true part: emotions on their own are unreliable guides, and many defenders of animals agree. The problem lies in P1, which describes the campaigns, not the arguments, and in P3, which confuses the presence of an emotion with the absence of reasons.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1, because the central arguments for animals start from criteria, and at P3, because a position can be moving and well grounded at the same time.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The sentence doesn't discuss the content of the defense of animals; it labels it as feeling in order to dismiss it. It's possible to reconstruct what it usually defends:

    What the sentence saysWhat it usually defends
    "That's sentimentality"That the opposing position is cool and rational, and the animals' side isn't
    "A video isn't an argument"That the images can be ignored without examining what they show
    "Facts don't care about your feelings"That the facts are on the meat eater's side
    "All you do is appeal to emotion"That there are no reasons behind the emotion
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The central arguments start from criteria, not feelings.

    In the preface to Animal Liberation, Peter Singer warns that some passages will stir emotions, but that the book appeals to no emotion that reason can't support. The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane shows that the same holds for the argument from marginal cases: it doesn't rest on the intuition that babies must be protected, but on a criterion that applies to babies and animals alike, sentience in Singer or being the subject of a life in Tom Regan (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 123–126).

    2. That animals feel is a fact, not an emotion.

    The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness of 2024, signed by hundreds of researchers, states that there is strong scientific support for conscious experience in mammals and birds, and at least a realistic possibility of it in all vertebrates and many invertebrates. The declaration isn't unanimous and doesn't close the research (A1, A2), but it shows the premise is empirical.

    3. Having an emotion isn't the same as appealing to emotion.

    The appeal-to-emotion fallacy consists of using a feeling in place of a reason. Feeling outrage at real suffering is something else: it's a reaction to a fact, and it can be fitting or not. Cochrane accepts that emotion is what sets morality in motion, but gives three reasons for reason to have the final say: it corrects prejudice, it breaks ties when equally compassionate people feel differently, and it allows the general rules a political community needs (Cochrane, 2010, p. 134). That's the role the arguments for animals give to reason.

    4. The other side feels too.

    The psychologist Jonathan Haidt proposed in 2001 that many moral judgments come from quick intuitions, and that reasoning often comes afterwards to justify them. The model is contested: the philosopher Joshua May, for example, argues that reasoning plays a larger role than Haidt allows. But in the case of meat there is specific evidence: Bastian and colleagues showed in 2012 that people attribute less mind to animals when they think of them as food and when they expect to eat them. Attachment, pleasure and habit weigh on both sides. If emotion disqualified, it would also disqualify the defense of consumption.

    5. Being moved has a legitimate place in persuasion.

    Cochrane notes that the images and descriptions in Animal Liberation inevitably move readers, and argues that stirring feelings is a legitimate part of the strategy of those who campaign for animals, as long as emotion doesn't on its own define the content of justice (Cochrane, 2010, p. 141). A true image from a farm is information. The relevant question is whether it shows something that actually happens (L27).

    6. The charge of sentimentality settles nothing.

    A strong feeling neither proves nor refutes a conclusion: what settles it is whether the reasons hold. Sometimes compassion is misplaced, as with someone who killed their own parents and asks for mercy for being an orphan; but the prey eaten alive or the animal on the farm did nothing to deserve what they suffer. And a conclusion that moves us may well coincide with the one an impartial judge would reach (Cunha, 2022, p. 160).

    7. Animal ethics also asks us to set aside feelings that choose badly.

    In a passage on another topic, whose conclusions this guide doesn't endorse, Peter Singer observes that valuing a being more because it is small and cute would be like thinking that a baby seal, with white fur and big eyes, deserves more protection than a gorilla (Practical Ethics, ch. 6). The criterion of sentience works as a correction of this kind of emotion: it gives the same weight to the pain of an animal who moves us and of one who doesn't, like a fish or a chicken. In practice, it is current protection that tends to follow affection: dogs and cats get care and laws of their own, while pigs and chickens, who feel in comparable ways, are left out.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: emotion picks its victims badly

    The most serious version says: campaigns for animals work because they show pigs, puppies and calves, not rats or insects. Being moved is selective and manipulable, and a position that grows this way isn't reliable. Care ethicists make a similar criticism from the other direction: they say rationalist philosophers themselves hide the emotion that drives them, and that the argument from marginal cases depends on the intuition that all humans must be protected (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 124–125).

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Selectivity is a flaw of emotion, which is why a criterion is asked for.

    That being moved favors pretty animals is exactly the reason to judge by the capacity to feel, which doesn't depend on looks. That criterion applies to the rat and the fish as much as to the calf (A2, L17).

    The arguments don't depend on intuition.

    Cochrane replies to care ethicists that Singer and Regan give reasons to protect babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities, and those same reasons reach animals (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 125–126).

    Manipulation has to be shown.

    If an image was staged or distorted, that can be pointed out and corrected (L27). If it shows what happens, calling it emotional doesn't make it false.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    Taking emotion out of the conversation doesn't change what's at stake. Animals used for food feel pain and fear, and about 88 billion land animals were slaughtered for meat worldwide in 2024, according to FAO data compiled by Our World in Data, not counting fish. The question remains whether it's just to cause this when we don't need to. It can be discussed as coolly as anyone likes; the answer doesn't get more comfortable for it.

Contradictions

Blames emotion, but uses emotion.

Defends meat with pleasure, tradition and attachment, which are feelings too.

Accepts outrage in other cases.

Anger at cruelty to a dog is usually seen as a fitting reaction, not as a lack of argument.

Says the images don't matter, but avoids seeing them.

If they were just emotion, there would be no reason to look away (E12).

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that emotion alone is a poor guide: it's selective, varies between people and can be exploited. Cochrane, who defends justice for animals, says the same, and that's why he insists reason should have the final say (Cochrane, 2010, p. 134). It's also right that campaigns can exaggerate, and those who defend animals should show only what is true and representative.

But

But the criticism hits a way of communicating, not the argument. Animals feel, and what is done to them can be assessed calmly. Once that assessment is made, the question remains their suffering and death.
