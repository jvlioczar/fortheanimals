B1

"There is humane slaughter"

The answer in one sentence

"humane slaughter" describes how to kill; it does not answer whether killing is justified. Even in the best case, a young, healthy animal loses the life it had ahead of it. And the best case is not what happens at scale: the rules in force in the European Union, the United States and Brazil allow methods that the EU's own scientific authority describes as painful, make exceptions to stunning and leave fish, who are most of the victims, with almost no protection.

Short answer

"Humane slaughter" is a name for the way of killing, not a reason to kill. Even if everything worked, a young, healthy animal would lose the life ahead of it, and we call that harm when it happens to a dog. And it doesn't always work: the rules in force allow birds to be hung upside down while conscious, pigs to be stunned with a gas they avoid when they can, and animals to be killed without stunning for religious reasons.

For children

Imagine someone says: "I'm going to take your dog, but I promise he won't feel anything." Would you be fine with that? No, because the problem isn't only pain. It's that he won't be alive anymore, playing, eating and running with you. It's the same with the cow and the pig. Taking the life of someone who wanted to keep living doesn't become kind just because it's quick.

Ask a question back

"Would you accept humane slaughter for your dog? And what would be a fair way to kill an innocent who does not want to die?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Slaughter is humane these days. The animal is stunned first and doesn't feel a thing."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1What makes using animals for food wrong is the suffering caused to them.
  2. P2Humane slaughter kills without pain or fear, because the animal loses consciousness before the cut.
  3. P3Farming can also be done without significant suffering.
  4. CSo eating meat from animals raised and slaughtered humanely is not wrong.

This is the position known as welfarism: the problem would be how animals are treated, not the fact that they are killed. It deserves to be taken seriously, because many people who hold it genuinely care about animal suffering.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1, which reduces harm to suffering and forgets death, and at P2, which describes an ideal rather than practice. P3 is dealt with in chapter B2.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument mixes two different questions and answers only one of them.

    QuestionWhat humane slaughter answers
    How to kill?With stunning, restraint and rules to reduce pain and fear
    Whether killing is acceptable?Nothing. The name assumes the answer

    The word "humane" does the work the argument doesn't. It is a persuasive definition: it borrows a term associated with compassion to describe the death of someone who does not want to die. No one would call it humane to painlessly kill a healthy person who was asleep.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It confuses the method with permission.

    A perfect slaughter is still the death of an individual who had an interest in going on living. Death deprives the animal of all the good experiences it would still have had, and farmed animals are killed very young, far from the natural end of their lives. That discussion is at the heart of chapter A16.

    2. The stunning the law allows can be painful.

    European Union rules on killing animals (Regulation 1099/2009) list carbon dioxide at high concentration among the permitted stunning methods for pigs, and Brazil's slaughter regulation also allows controlled-atmosphere stunning without banning it. In 2020, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) concluded that pigs find high-concentration CO2 aversive and avoid it when they can choose, that exposure causes pain, fear and respiratory distress before loss of consciousness, and that "there are no preventive or corrective measures" for this: the only way out is to use other gases.

    3. Birds are hung up conscious.

    EU and Brazilian rules both allow poultry to be stunned in an electrical water bath, in which the birds are shackled by their feet, upside down, before losing consciousness. In the United States, the federal humane slaughter law does not cover poultry at all. In 2019, EFSA identified 35 welfare hazards in the slaughter of poultry, most linked to stunning and bleeding, and noted that water-bath stunning does not guarantee that every bird is unconscious.

    4. The law itself makes exceptions.

    The EU regulation allows member states to exempt religious slaughter from prior stunning, the US law treats ritual slaughter as humane by definition, and Brazil's regulation also permits it. Brazil's regulation even sets rules for slaughtering females in late pregnancy, specifying that fetuses not used must be "left inside the closed uterus until they are dead".

    5. Fish are practically left out.

    Slaughter rules deal mainly with mammals and poultry. For fish, the norm worldwide is death without stunning: suffocation in air, on ice or in ice slurry, gill cutting or gutting. Tens of billions of fish are farmed each year, a number comparable to chickens, and more than a trillion wild fish are caught.

    6. Scale turns exceptions into crowds.

    In 2021, about 73.8 billion chickens, 1.4 billion pigs and 332 million cattle were slaughtered. A stunning failure rate of just 1% in chickens would mean more than 700 million birds killed while conscious every year.

    7. There is no fair way to kill someone who does not want to die.

    Imagine the question about an innocent person who does not want to die: what would be a fair way to kill them? No one answers "with the least suffering", because the problem is not in the method but in killing someone who did nothing and wants to go on living. The question shows that "humane" describes only the manner and does not resolve the injustice. An animal also has an interest in living, and shows it by fleeing, resisting and reacting to danger.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the problem is suffering, and suffering can be fixed

    The strongest version accepts everything said about practice and replies that these are failures of execution. If inspection improves, if CO2 is replaced by other gases and if each animal has a good life, nothing wrong is left. For animals without a sense of themselves over time, a painless death would not be a harm, as long as another animal with an equally good life takes its place. Peter Singer discussed this idea, called replaceability, in Practical Ethics.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It depends on death not being a harm.

    If losing years of good life is a loss for the one who loses them, the counter-reply falls. That premise is A16's, and it is not usually accepted in any other case.

    It proves too much.

    If killing painlessly and replacing were acceptable for beings without a sense of themselves over time, it would also hold for humans in that condition, such as newborns. Almost no one accepts this, so the counter-reply needs another criterion, which turns out to be species again (A10).

    The ideal scenario isn't what people buy.

    EFSA's own opinion says the CO2 problem cannot be fixed within the method, and it is still permitted. Defending the ideal while paying for the practice is defending something else.

    A good life rarely exists at today's scale.

    Most meat comes from intensive farming, as chapter B2 shows. The counter-reply would only work for a tiny fraction of what is eaten.

    Reducing suffering is progress, not a license.

    A less painful slaughter is better than a more painful one. It does not follow that it is acceptable, just as a less cruel prison does not make it just to imprison an innocent person.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Persuasive definition
"Humane" borrows the idea of compassion to describe a death
Changing the question
Answers "how to kill" when the question is "whether killing is acceptable"
False dichotomy
"Die at the slaughterhouse or old and sick", as if not breeding animals to kill were not an option

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Generalizing from the ideal case
Takes the perfect slaughter as a description of what happens to billions of animals
Trust in the label
Assumes that a rule existing guarantees it is followed and that it is enough

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Reducing harm to pain
Ignores the deprivation caused by death
Double standard
Accepts for pigs and chickens what it rejects for dogs and humans

Biases

Meat paradox

Eating meat leads people to attribute less mind and less moral status to the animal, which makes its death less troubling

Loughnan, Haslam and Bastian, 2010

Willful ignorance

People avoid learning how slaughter happens, and the label takes the place of information

Linked to E7 and B9

System justification

An official rule existing makes the practice seem legitimate

Linked to B8 and D7

Optimism bias

"The meat I buy is different"

Linked to B2, B2 and H8

Scope insensitivity

Billions of slaughters weigh no more than the image of one well-done slaughter

Linked to K1

Framing effect

The word "humane" presents the killing from its most favorable side and changes the judgment without changing the fact

Levin and Gaeth, 1988

Contradictions

Rejects humane slaughter of dogs.

The same person would be horrified by a dog slaughterhouse that followed every stunning rule.

Condemns killing a healthy animal for no reason.

Putting down one's own healthy cat for convenience is seen as wrong, even if painless.

Knows that killing someone in their sleep is wrong.

The absence of pain and fear has never been accepted as a justification among humans.

Doesn't know how the animal they ate was killed.

People who defend humane slaughter rarely check the method, the gas or the species, which shows the argument protects a habit, not a real requirement.

Admits that suffering matters.

If the animal's pain didn't matter, no one would need the word "humane".

What the argument gets right

What is true

There is something true here. Reducing suffering at slaughter matters a great deal: effective stunning is better than none, and replacing an aversive gas with a less painful one prevents real pain for billions of animals. People who work for these reforms do reduce suffering, and the wish that animals not suffer is exactly the right starting point.

But

But that wish, taken seriously, goes beyond the adjective. If the animal's pain matters because it is someone, its life also matters for the same reason. Humane slaughter improves the form of a practice, and the question it leaves unanswered is whether the practice should exist.
