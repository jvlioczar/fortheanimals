N8

"Using medicine and genetics to end suffering is playing God"

The answer in one sentence

we already use anaesthesia, painkillers, vaccines and antidepressants to reduce natural suffering, and no one calls that playing God. The idea of using biotechnology to reduce the suffering of humans and animals raises serious questions of safety, consent and justice, and they need answers. But "pain is natural" isn't one of them: if it were, anaesthesia would have been a mistake.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

Anaesthesia also tampered with something natural, and many people are grateful. Using science to reduce suffering isn't new; it's what medicine has done for centuries. The risks are real, which is why what's defended is careful, voluntary, regulated research, not imposition. What doesn't work as an objection is saying pain is natural.

For children

When you go to the dentist, they use a medicine so you don't feel pain. That's good, isn't it? Long ago that medicine didn't exist, and people suffered a lot. Some people thought it was wrong to invent medicine for pain, but today almost everyone is grateful. Using science carefully so that people and animals suffer less is a bit like that.

Ask a question back

"Would you refuse anaesthesia for surgery because pain is natural?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Messing with genes so no one suffers? That's playing God. Suffering is part of life."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Pain and suffering are products of evolution and serve important functions.
  2. P2Altering the biology of humans and animals to reduce them would have unpredictable consequences.
  3. P3Previous attempts to "improve" living beings through genetics, such as eugenics, led to terrible abuses.
  4. CSo we shouldn't use biotechnology to reduce suffering.

P1, P2 and P3 are true and need to be taken seriously. The problem is the conclusion: the risks call for caution, consent and rules, not a ban on any attempt.

The argument is invalid in its strong form: from the risks of a technology it doesn't follow that every use is wrong, as medicine itself shows.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument bundles very different objections:

    ObjectionTypeWeight
    "Pain is natural"Appeal to natureNone: we treat natural pain all the time
    "It could go wrong"RiskReal: calls for research, trials and caution
    "It's like eugenics"Historical abuseReal: calls for consent, freedom and justice
    "Pain has a function"BiologicalReal: pain protects; eliminating it entirely would be dangerous

    The last three are reasons to do it well, not reasons not to do it.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. We already do it.

    Anaesthesia, painkillers, vaccines, antidepressants and treatments for chronic pain are ways of altering biology to reduce suffering. When anaesthesia arrived in childbirth, in the mid-nineteenth century, there were religious objections to it; today almost no one defends them.

    2. Pain can be regulated without being eliminated.

    People born unable to feel pain face serious risks, which shows pain protects. But there are rare cases of people with greatly reduced pain and anxiety who live well, such as the Scottish woman studied by researchers in 2019, who carries a variant in the FAAH-OUT gene. The serious proposal is to reduce useless suffering while preserving pain's warning function.

    3. Eugenics is something else.

    Historical eugenics was coercive, discriminatory and based on hierarchies of value between people. A voluntary therapy to reduce the pain of those who choose it has none of these features. Past abuse calls for rules and rights, not a ban on all genetic medicine.

    4. The human test.

    If there were a safe, voluntary treatment that reduced the chronic pain of millions of people, no one would refuse it by saying "it's playing God". Refusing the same logic for animals, whose suffering is immense and who can't seek treatment, is using species as the criterion.

    5. Tools are already being tested.

    Gene drive techniques are being studied to reduce populations of malaria-carrying mosquitoes. This shows that genetic interventions in wild populations are already the subject of serious scientific debate, with all the caution they require.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: suffering gives life meaning

    The most serious version says: overcoming difficulties, feeling losses and facing pain are part of what makes life meaningful. A life without suffering would be shallow, and a humanity that eliminated pain would lose something valuable.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    No one proposes eliminating all challenges.

    The proposal is to reduce involuntary and extreme suffering, such as chronic pain, severe depression or the starvation of young animals, not to make life challenge-free.

    Meaning doesn't depend on extreme pain.

    We can find meaning in loving, learning and creating without needing agony.

    The argument doesn't apply to animals.

    A young animal that starves on its first day gains no meaning from it.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Appeal to nature
If pain is natural, it shouldn't be altered
Guilt by association
Equates voluntary therapy with coercive eugenics
False dilemma
Either we accept all suffering, or we eliminate all pain

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Double standard
Accepts reducing human pain with technology and refuses the same for animals

Biases

Appeal to nature

The natural seems sacred or untouchable

Linked to C1 and N2

Status quo bias

The current level of suffering seems normal

Samuelson and Zeckhauser, 1988

Omission bias

Not acting seems safer than acting

Spranca, Minsk and Baron, 1991

Ambiguity effect

The unknown outcome of a new technology is scarier than the known suffering of today

Ellsberg, 1961

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets a lot right: pain has vital functions, biotechnology carries enormous risks, and the history of eugenics shows the danger of using science to impose ideals. Any proposal in this area needs consent, freedom, justice and careful research. It's also legitimate that many people, including vegans, distrust these ideas.

But

But none of these concerns comes from the idea that pain is natural and therefore untouchable. They call for doing it well, not for not doing it. And if we accept reducing human pain with science, it remains to be explained why animals' pain wouldn't count.
