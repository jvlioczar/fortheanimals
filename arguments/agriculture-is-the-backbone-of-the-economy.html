J13

"Agriculture is the backbone of the economy"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

economic importance isn't a moral justification. Many profitable activities have been abandoned because they caused harm. Besides, the argument ignores the subsidies and the costs livestock farming passes on to society, such as deforestation, emissions, disease and water use. According to the UN, public support to agriculture worldwide, livestock included, amounts to about 540 billion dollars a year, and most of it distorts prices or harms nature and health. Crop farming, which grows food, also needs to be told apart from animal farming, which raises and kills animals. In 2024, about 88 billion land animals were slaughtered for meat worldwide, a scale of suffering and death sustained by consumer habits. If meat production were transformed, for example through cellular agriculture, still expensive and small-scale, it would in principle be possible to produce meat at scale without that suffering and killing, and to keep the sector's income in an ethical way.

Short answer

Being profitable doesn't make something right: many profitable activities have been changed because they caused harm. Farming also isn't only livestock: it includes grains, fruit, coffee and much more, which would carry on. And animal farming slaughters about 88 billion land animals a year worldwide, a suffering sustained by consumer habits; if it were transformed, for example through cellular agriculture, meat and income could still be produced without killing animals. And the argument ignores the costs livestock farming passes on to everyone: deforestation, emissions, disease and water use. According to the UN, public support to agriculture worldwide amounts to about 540 billion dollars a year, and most of it distorts prices or harms nature and health.

For children

Money is important, but it isn't everything. Some things that make a lot of money hurt people, animals or nature. And farming isn't only raising cattle: it's also growing beans, rice, fruit and many other foods, which can keep growing. The part that raises animals makes billions of them suffer and die every year. And there are scientists trying to make meat from cells, without having to kill any animal.

Ask a question back

"If an activity makes a profit, is that enough to say it's fair?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"Farming feeds the nation. The country lives off agribusiness. Veganism would wreck the economy."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Agriculture, including livestock farming, is an important part of the economy in many countries.
  2. P2It generates exports, jobs and income.
  3. P3Shrinking livestock farming would harm the economy.
  4. CSo veganism goes against the national interest.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3 and the conclusion: agriculture isn't only livestock, the hidden costs are large, and economic importance doesn't settle the moral question.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails because it leaves out costs and confuses the economic with the moral.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument looks only at the revenue side:

    What the argument considersWhat it leaves out
    Exports and GDPSubsidies and public credit
    JobsEnvironmental costs: deforestation, emissions, water
    Rural incomePublic health costs and the harm to animals
    Farming as one blockThe difference between crop farming, which grows food, and animal farming, which raises and kills billions of animals
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Profit isn't a moral justification.

    Profitable activities have been abandoned or restricted because they caused harm, such as child labor or the use of asbestos.

    2. Agriculture isn't only livestock.

    Grains, fruit, coffee, sugar cane and many other products would carry on, and demand for plant foods tends to grow.

    3. Animal farming causes suffering and death on an enormous scale.

    Crop farming grows plants; animal farming raises and kills animals. According to FAO data, about 88 billion land animals were slaughtered for meat worldwide in 2024, not counting fish. In Brazil alone, one of the largest meat exporters, about 6.46 billion chickens, 57.9 million pigs and 39.3 million cattle were slaughtered that year, according to the country's statistics office (IBGE). Broiler chickens, the vast majority, are slaughtered at about six weeks old (B2). This volume doesn't exist by chance: it is sustained by demand, that is, by the habits of those who buy.

    4. There are hidden costs.

    Livestock farming is the main driver of tropical deforestation (H1), a large source of methane (H8) and uses most farmland (H10). These costs are paid by society as a whole.

    5. There are large subsidies.

    A report by the FAO, UNDP and UNEP estimated public support to agricultural producers worldwide at about 540 billion dollars a year, and concluded that most of it distorts prices or harms nature and health.

    6. Economies change.

    Countries and regions have changed their economic base many times throughout history.

    7. Meat without slaughter is conceivable.

    If animal farming were transformed, for example through cellular agriculture, which grows animal cells in bioreactors, it would in principle be possible to produce meat at scale without raising and killing animals, and the sector would keep generating income, jobs and GDP. The technology is still small and expensive: cultivated meat has been approved for sale in only a few countries, such as Singapore, the United States, Israel and Australia, supply is limited, and Italy and some US states, such as Florida and Alabama, have banned its sale. Whether it will reach the price and scale of conventional meat isn't known. But the example shows that the sector's wealth doesn't depend, in principle, on the suffering and death of animals.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: some countries depend on meat exports

    The most serious version points out that countries such as Brazil, Australia and the United States are among the world's largest meat exporters, and that this revenue matters for their trade balance.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Dependence can be reduced.

    Through diversification and investment in other sectors.

    Global demand can change.

    Importing countries are also debating reduced consumption and deforestation.

    Revenue doesn't justify the harm.

    Neither to the animals nor to the environment.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The economic argument is about money, not about what happens to the animals. Even if livestock farming were indispensable to the economy, the question would remain: is it fair to raise and kill billions of animals for profit? And if the same income can, in principle, come from crops and from meat produced without slaughter, GDP stops being even an economic reason to keep the suffering going.

Contradictions

Criticizes subsidies in other sectors.

But not those for livestock.

Worries about deforestation.

Whose main driver is livestock farming.

Would accept limits on profitable activities that harmed them.

Profit isn't the criterion in those cases.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Agriculture is indeed important to many economies, and many families depend on it. Abrupt changes would be harmful, and any transition needs to consider its economic effects.

But

But economic importance isn't a moral justification, and the accounts for agriculture need to include the costs it passes on to everyone. A more plant-based farming sector, and meat production without slaughter, if the technology matures, can stay strong.
