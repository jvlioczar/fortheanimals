J9

"Agriculture is the backbone of the economy"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

economic importance isn't a moral justification. Many profitable activities have been abandoned because they caused harm. Besides, the argument ignores the subsidies and the costs livestock farming passes on to society, such as deforestation, emissions, disease and water use. According to the UN, public support to agriculture worldwide, livestock included, amounts to about 540 billion dollars a year, and most of it distorts prices or harms nature and health.

Short answer

Being profitable doesn't make something right: many profitable activities have been changed because they caused harm. Farming also isn't only livestock: it includes grains, fruit, coffee and much more, which would carry on. And the argument ignores the costs livestock farming passes on to everyone: deforestation, emissions, disease and water use. According to the UN, public support to agriculture worldwide amounts to about 540 billion dollars a year, and most of it distorts prices or harms nature and health.

For children

Money is important, but it isn't everything. Some things that make a lot of money hurt people, animals or nature. And farming isn't only raising cattle: it's also growing beans, rice, fruit and many other foods, which can keep growing.

Ask a question back

"If an activity makes a profit, is that enough to say it's fair?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Farming feeds the nation. The country lives off agribusiness. Veganism would wreck the economy."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Agriculture, including livestock farming, is an important part of the economy in many countries.
  2. P2It generates exports, jobs and income.
  3. P3Shrinking livestock farming would harm the economy.
  4. CSo veganism goes against the national interest.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3 and the conclusion: agriculture isn't only livestock, the hidden costs are large, and economic importance doesn't settle the moral question.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails because it leaves out costs and confuses the economic with the moral.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument looks only at the revenue side:

    What the argument considersWhat it leaves out
    Exports and GDPSubsidies and public credit
    JobsEnvironmental costs: deforestation, emissions, water
    Rural incomePublic health costs and the harm to animals
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Profit isn't a moral justification.

    Profitable activities have been abandoned or restricted because they caused harm, such as child labor or the use of asbestos.

    2. Agriculture isn't only livestock.

    Grains, fruit, coffee, sugar cane and many other products would carry on, and demand for plant foods tends to grow.

    3. There are hidden costs.

    Livestock farming is the main driver of tropical deforestation (H1), a large source of methane (H7) and uses most farmland (H9). These costs are paid by society as a whole.

    4. There are large subsidies.

    A report by the FAO, UNDP and UNEP estimated public support to agricultural producers worldwide at about 540 billion dollars a year, and concluded that most of it distorts prices or harms nature and health.

    5. Economies change.

    Countries and regions have changed their economic base many times throughout history.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    UNEP, on the report *A multi-billion-dollar opportunity* (2021)Public support to agricultural producers amounts to about 540 billion dollars a year; about 87% distorts prices or harms nature and healthA report by the FAO, UNDP and UNEP; checked from the summary
    Our World in Data, drivers of deforestationCattle pasture was responsible for about 41% of tropical deforestationAlso used in H1
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: some countries depend on meat exports

    The most serious version points out that countries such as Brazil, Australia and the United States are among the world's largest meat exporters, and that this revenue matters for their trade balance.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Dependence can be reduced.

    Through diversification and investment in other sectors.

    Global demand can change.

    Importing countries are also debating reduced consumption and deforestation.

    Revenue doesn't justify the harm.

    Neither to the animals nor to the environment.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The economic argument is about money, not about what happens to the animals. Even if livestock farming were indispensable to the economy, the question would remain: is it fair to raise and kill billions of animals for profit?

Contradictions

Criticizes subsidies in other sectors.

But not those for livestock.

Worries about deforestation.

Whose main driver is livestock farming.

Would accept limits on profitable activities that harmed them.

Profit isn't the criterion in those cases.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Agriculture is indeed important to many economies, and many families depend on it. Abrupt changes would be harmful, and any transition needs to consider its economic effects.

But

But economic importance isn't a moral justification, and the accounts for agriculture need to include the costs it passes on to everyone. A more plant-based farming sector can stay strong.
