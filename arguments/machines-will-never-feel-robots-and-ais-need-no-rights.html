N15

"Machines will never feel; robots and AIs need no rights"

The answer in one sentence

today's machines probably feel nothing. But if an artificial mind is ever capable of suffering, the material it's made of will matter no more than species does. The criterion is sentience, not biology. Since digital minds could be copied by the million, faceless and voiceless, the time to think about this is before creating them, not after.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

Today's machines probably feel nothing. But if an AI can one day suffer, being made of silicon rather than flesh won't make its suffering any less real, just as being a pig rather than a human doesn't make the pig's pain less real. That's why scientists and philosophers are already studying how to assess this, before we create minds that might suffer on an enormous scale.

For children

Today, computers don't feel anything, just like a calculator doesn't get sad. But imagine that one day someone invents a robot that really feels pain, like a dog. Then it would be wrong to hurt it, wouldn't it? What matters isn't whether someone is made of skin or metal. It's whether they can feel.

Ask a question back

"If it were proven that a machine feels pain, would you think it right to make it suffer just because it isn't made of cells?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Computers don't suffer. It's just a program. Talk of robot rights is science fiction."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Only sentient beings can be harmed.
  2. P2Sentience depends on a living organism with a biological nervous system.
  3. P3Machines and programs aren't living organisms.
  4. CSo no machine can ever be harmed, and there's nothing to discuss.

P1 is this guide's own criterion. P3 is true. The problem lies in P2: we don't know whether sentience requires biology, and the main scientific theories of consciousness don't say it does.

The argument is valid, but P2 is an assumption, not an established fact.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument mixes two different claims:

    ClaimStatus
    Today's AIs aren't sentientLikely, according to most researchers, though there's no certainty
    No machine can ever be sentientAn assumption without consensus; several theories of consciousness allow the possibility

    This guide holds that the moral criterion is sentience. If that's the criterion, the material a being is made of matters as little as species.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Substrate is like species.

    Saying only biological beings can matter repeats, at another level, the error of speciesism: choosing a trait unrelated to the capacity to suffer (A8, A11).

    2. We don't know whether consciousness requires biology.

    A 2023 report, by researchers in neuroscience, AI and philosophy, assessed AI systems in light of the main scientific theories of consciousness. It concluded that no current system is a strong candidate, but that there are no obvious technical barriers to building systems that satisfy those theories' indicators.

    3. The scale could be enormous.

    Digital minds, if sentient, could be copied by the million, run faster than ours and be used for repetitive tasks, with no one to notice. It's the kind of risk that is easier to prevent than to fix.

    4. The human test.

    If a person had parts of their brain gradually replaced by artificial components that do the same job, at what point would they stop being able to suffer? The question shows that what concerns us is experience, not material.

    5. Uncertainty calls for precaution.

    We don't need to claim machines feel in order to take the possibility seriously. It's enough to recognize that if we err on the side of indifference, the cost could be immense, as it was with animals.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: giving rights to machines is dangerous

    The most serious version says: attributing sentience to machines that don't feel would be a serious mistake. Companies could use it to dodge responsibility, people could become attached to systems that only imitate emotions, and resources would be diverted from beings who certainly suffer, such as animals.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The risk runs both ways.

    Attributing too much sentience is a mistake; denying it when it exists is another, potentially more serious one. The answer is to develop criteria, not to deny the question.

    Precaution isn't granting rights now.

    Taking the issue seriously today means researching, creating criteria and avoiding creating suffering through carelessness, not treating every program as a person.

    It doesn't compete with animals.

    The same principle, that sentience is what matters, protects both animals and possible artificial minds. Rejecting it in one case weakens it in the other.

Contradictions

Rejects speciesism.

But accepts a criterion of the same structure, based on material.

Is moved by artificial characters in films.

Recognizing, in fiction, that their suffering would matter if it were real.

Accepts precaution for other technological risks.

But not for this one.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that today's machines probably don't feel, that many people attribute emotions to systems that only imitate them, and that this confusion can be exploited. It's also right that animals are real sentient beings suffering now, in enormous numbers.

But

But the criterion that protects animals is the same one that would protect an artificial mind, if one existed. Thinking about it in advance is cheaper than repeating with machines the mistake we made with animals.
