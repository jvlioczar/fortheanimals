B17

"It's a fair trade: we care for them and feed them, and they give us meat"

The answer in one sentence

the idea of an agreement between humans and farmed animals, defended in books such as Stephen Budiansky's The Covenant of the Wild (1992), assumes a trade. But a trade requires that both parties can accept or refuse, and the animal can't. The same side that "protects" is the one that kills, and the price charged is life itself, in a fraction of the time the animal could have lived. The fact that the species multiplied through domestication doesn't benefit each individual born to be slaughtered.

Short answer

A fair trade requires that both sides can accept or refuse, and the animal can't. The one who "protects" is the same one who kills, and the price is life itself, charged long before its natural end. The species having multiplied through domestication doesn't help the individual born to be slaughtered. And the comparison with nature doesn't work: without farming, these animals wouldn't be out there suffering; they wouldn't have been bred.

For children

A trade is when both sides agree, like swapping stickers. The cow didn't agree to anything: nobody asked her, and she can't say no. And in the end, the one who cared for her is the one who takes her life. That isn't a trade, it's a decision made by only one side.

Ask a question back

"If it were a fair trade, could the animal say no?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"We give them food, shelter, protect them from predators and care for them when they're sick. In return, they give us meat. It's a fair deal, and nature is much worse."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Farmed animals receive food, shelter, protection from predators and veterinary care.
  2. P2In the wild, they would suffer from hunger, disease and predators.
  3. P3A relationship in which each side gives something and receives something is a fair trade.
  4. CSo breeding animals and killing them for food is a fair trade.

P1 is partly true, and depends a lot on the farming system. P2 compares with an alternative that isn't on the table: these animals wouldn't live in the wild, they simply wouldn't be bred. P3 is false when one side can neither consent nor refuse.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    What the argument calls a tradeWho decidesWhat the animal gives
    Food, shelter, protectionOnly the farmerTheir whole life, as well as freedom of movement and company
    Veterinary careOnly the farmer, according to economic valueTheir body, when slaughter pays more than further care
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. A trade requires consent.

    An agreement in which only one side sets the terms, and the other can neither accept nor refuse, isn't a trade. Between humans, we'd call it an imposition.

    2. The protector is the predator.

    The argument describes the farmer as the one who protects the animal, but the farmer is the one who kills them. Protecting someone in order to kill them later isn't protection; it's breeding for use.

    3. The price is disproportionate.

    What the animal "pays" is their life, usually long before its natural end. No care received beforehand makes up for losing everything that would have come after.

    4. The species isn't the individual.

    Domestication multiplied the numbers of cattle, pigs and chickens, but that doesn't benefit each animal who is born. It is the individual who feels, not the species (see A10).

    5. The alternative isn't the wild.

    Without farming, these animals wouldn't be roaming free and suffering from predators; they simply wouldn't be bred (see B7).

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Budiansky, The Covenant of the Wild (1992)Argues that domestication was an advantageous strategy for domesticated speciesThe best-known version of the argument
    Donaldson and Kymlicka, Zoopolis (2011)Criticize the idea of a domestication contract and discuss what duties we have to domesticated animalsPolitical philosophy of animal rights
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the animals chose to come close to humans

    The most serious version says that at the start of domestication, some animals approached humans of their own accord, because they gained food and protection, and that this shows a kind of consent.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Approaching food isn't agreeing to be killed.

    An animal scavenging for scraps isn't agreeing to the slaughter of their descendants thousands of years later.

    Consent isn't passed down the generations.

    Even if an ancestor had chosen something, that doesn't hold for the animal alive today.

    Today's domestication is imposed.

    Controlled breeding, genetic selection and slaughter aren't the animals' choices.

Contradictions

Talks about protecting the animal.

And is the one who kills them.

Talks about a deal.

And only one side sets the terms.

Says they care for the animal's health.

And the care lasts only while the animal is worth more alive.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that many farmers feed and care for their animals, that life in the wild involves a lot of suffering, and that domestication multiplied these species.

But

But a trade requires that both sides can refuse, and the price charged is the animal's life. Caring for someone doesn't give the right to kill them.
