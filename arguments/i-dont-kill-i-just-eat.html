K7

"I don't kill; I just eat"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about effectiveness and individual responsibility, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended only by each person's impact, anyone who proved their purchase changes nothing would be let off. But no one accepts this for other harms: taking part in an injustice is still wrong even when each person's share is small.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

it's outsourced morality. Whoever pays for an act takes part in it. Slaughter happens because someone buys the meat, and the slaughterhouse worker does, to order, what the consumer doesn't want to see. Among humans, no one accepts that commissioning a harm is innocent just because someone else carried it out.

Short answer

Whoever pays for an act takes part in it. Slaughter happens because someone buys the meat; the slaughterhouse worker does, to order, what the consumer doesn't want to see. Among humans, no one accepts that commissioning a harm is innocent just because someone else carried it out. And if you couldn't kill the animal yourself, perhaps that says something about the act you're paying for.

For children

Imagine someone pays another person to break a classmate's toy. The one who paid is to blame too, right? Meat is similar: whoever buys it pays for the animal to be killed, even without seeing it.

Ask a question back

"If you wouldn't kill the animal with your own hands, why pay for someone else to?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I just buy it. The butcher is the one who kills."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Those who kill the animals are the slaughterhouse workers.
  2. P2The consumer doesn't take part directly in the slaughter.
  3. P3Moral responsibility falls on whoever performs the act.
  4. CSo those who only eat meat aren't responsible for the animals' deaths.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3: moral responsibility isn't limited to whoever carries out the act; it includes whoever commissions and funds it.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument separates the act from the order:

    RoleWhat they doResponsibility
    Slaughterhouse workerCarries out the slaughterDirect, but under economic pressure
    CompanyOrganizes productionLarge
    ConsumerPays and creates the demandReal: they place the order
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Paying is taking part.

    Moral responsibility includes whoever commissions and funds an act, not only whoever carries it out.

    2. Slaughter exists because of the purchase.

    Without demand, there'd be no slaughter (K2).

    3. The worker does what the consumer doesn't want to do.

    Slaughterhouse work is dangerous and grueling, and it's delegated to others precisely because it's hard to face.

    4. Distance makes denial easier.

    Psychology shows that people dissociate meat from the animal and the slaughter to reduce discomfort.

    5. Among humans, outsourcing doesn't absolve.

    Commissioning a crime is a crime.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Benningstad and Kunst, 2020, *Appetite*People dissociate meat from its animal origin to reduce discomfortA systematic review
    Kagan, *Do I Make a Difference?* (2011)Each purchase has a real expected effect on productionAlso used in K1 and K2
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: one purchase has a minimal effect

    The most serious version grants that whoever buys takes part in some sense, but says the effect of an individual purchase is so small that the responsibility is practically nil.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    The expected effect is real.

    As chapter K1 shows.

    Responsibility doesn't depend only on effect.

    Taking part in a harmful practice counts too.

    Small contributions add up.

    The slaughter of billions of animals is made of individual purchases.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument tries to shift the responsibility onto others. But the animal dies because someone paid for it. The moral question is for whoever pays: what justifies commissioning the death of another being?

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that consumers aren't directly present at the slaughter, and that companies and the system bear great responsibility. It's also true that slaughterhouse workers face hard conditions and deserve attention.

But

But distance doesn't erase participation. Slaughter is done for those who buy, and those who buy are part of the chain that decides the animal's death.
