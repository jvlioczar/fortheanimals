J17

"People are dying in a war and you're worried about animals"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

in a war, animals are among the victims, not beside them. They are killed in the bombing, left trapped on abandoned farms, starved in zoos with no staff, abandoned during the flight. Helping them has a cost, as everything does: money donated for animal rescue could have gone to humanitarian relief. But the cost is far smaller than the sentence assumes, because almost nothing used is contested with human relief, and in disasters caring for animals is often what makes it possible to save people, since many will not leave without them. And a cost existing doesn't settle the question: every use of resources has one, and the objection appears only when the beneficiary is an animal. The rule is also applied to nothing else: nobody says it's inhuman to play football or go to the cinema during a war.

Note: the general version of this argument is J16; the accusation aimed at the person is L20

Short answer

Animals aren't standing next to the victims of a war: they are victims of the war. They die in the attacks, they're left trapped on farms with nobody there, they starve in shelters and zoos. There is some cost, yes: money is fungible, and a donation for animals could have gone to people. But the people rescuing animals wouldn't be operating on the wounded, and most of the effort comes from people and organisations who wouldn't be working in human relief. And in disasters, caring for animals is often what makes people agree to leave: in a survey after Hurricane Katrina, 44% of those who did not evacuate said they stayed because of their animals. Finally, a cost existing decides nothing: it exists in everything, and the objection is only made when the one being helped is an animal.

Question back: "Would you say the same to someone who, during the war, looks after a library, a football club or a veterinary hospital?"

For children

In a war, many people suffer, and that is terrible. The animals who live there suffer too: they run out of food, they lose their families and they get hurt in just the same way. Helping a lost dog takes help away from no one. And many people don't want to leave home without their animals, so whoever helps the animals is helping the people as well.

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"There are children dying under bombs and you're posting pictures of a dog. That's inhuman."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1In extreme situations, resources and attention are scarce and have to be prioritised.
  2. P2Human suffering in a war is the most urgent.
  3. P3Whoever devotes attention to animals takes attention away from the human victims.
  4. CSo worrying about animals in a war is wrong, or at least out of place.

P1 and P2 are reasonable. The whole argument rests on P3, which is empirical and false: the attention given to animals doesn't come out of a stock that would otherwise go to people, and animal rescues are normally carried out by people who wouldn't be doing human relief.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, and fails again at the leap from "it's less urgent" to "it's wrong to care".

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The sentence runs three different things together, and only the first is an argument.

    What is saidWhat it actually claimsWhere it stands
    "There are more urgent things"A comparison of prioritiesReasonable, and doesn't lead to the conclusion
    "You're taking from people"An empirical claim about resourcesOnly partly true, and much smaller than assumed
    "That's inhuman"A judgement about the character of the speakerNot an argument about the subject

    The third is usually what carries the sentence. It doesn't dispute whether animals suffer in war: it disputes who the person caring about it is. That is why this chapter sits here and not among the arguments about sentience: there is no thesis about animals to refute, there is an accusation to answer.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The animals are victims of the same war.

    Entire farms are left without water, feed or milking when people flee. Companion animals are abandoned or die with their families. Zoos and shelters are left without staff and without power. Working and draught animals are killed or used to exhaustion. None of this is a concern running parallel to the conflict: it is part of what the conflict causes.

    2. There is a cost, but not the one the sentence describes.

    Money is fungible: a donation for animal rescue could have gone to humanitarian relief, and that is worth acknowledging rather than pretending the effort comes from nowhere. What doesn't hold is the size and shape the objection gives that cost. The people organising animal rescue wouldn't be performing war surgery, don't occupy a hospital bed, don't consume feed no one else would eat; the logistical capacity involved is tiny next to the humanitarian operation; and much of the money donated for animals wouldn't exist without that specific cause, rather than being diverted from another.

    3. And a cost, by itself, decides nothing.

    Every use of resources has an alternative forgone: restoring a cathedral, keeping an orchestra, funding a club, researching a rare disease. In none of those cases does anyone conclude that doing it is wrong. For the cost to settle the question here, you would have to assume the animal side of the scale weighs zero, and that assumption is precisely what's in dispute (A8). The objection skips the argument and helps itself to the conclusion.

    4. Helping animals often saves people.

    This is the best-documented point in the chapter. In a survey conducted after Hurricane Katrina, 44% of the people who did not evacuate said they stayed because they wouldn't leave their animals behind. That is why, in 2006, the United States passed a law requiring state evacuation plans to include pets and service animals. Planning for animals is planning for the people who won't leave without them.

    5. The criterion is applied to nothing else.

    During wars people go on playing football, making music, restoring historic buildings, looking after libraries and getting married. Nobody calls that inhuman. The rule "while humans are suffering, nothing else may be cared for" is invoked only when the object of care is an animal, which suggests the problem isn't the priority but the animal.

    6. The demand is impossible to meet.

    There is always, somewhere, a human tragedy under way. If it cancelled every other kind of care, there would never be a legitimate moment to attend to animals, which turns the objection into a permanent ban dressed up as a call for common sense.

    7. Whoever makes the accusation is rarely helping anyone.

    In practice the sentence tends to come from people not involved in any relief effort, and it serves to end the conversation rather than redirect effort. It is the "what about that?" pattern: using a cause you don't defend to disqualify another.

    8. The assumption about compassion is never defended.

    The sentence presupposes that care is a resource that runs out, so that spending it on animals leaves less for people. Nobody who says it offers a reason, and the same presupposition is applied to nothing else: no one thinks that being moved by victims of a distant earthquake leaves you with less compassion for your own neighbourhood.

  4. d

    The best reply: triage

    The serious version isn't the accusation. It is the principle of triage: with scarce resources, you attend first to those at greatest risk and those who can benefit most, and that usually means prioritising human beings. While people are in immediate danger, available effort should go to them.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Triage sets the order; it doesn't remove anyone from the queue.

    Attending first isn't never attending. Its conclusion is "later", not "never".

    It presupposes a shared pool, which exists only in part.

    Triage applies within one hospital, with the same teams and the same beds. Between a human relief team and a group of volunteers feeding abandoned animals, what is actually contested is donated money, and not the rest.

    In disasters, triage often recommends including the animals.

    For the reason in point 4 above: without it, some people don't leave.

    The reply doesn't support the tone.

    At most it would justify "do it later". It doesn't justify calling the person inhuman.

Contradictions

It doesn't complain about football, cinema or weddings during a war.

Only about care for animals.

It praises the soldier who saves a dog.

The same act becomes heroism when one of "ours" does it.

It accepts spending on animals in peacetime.

Although humans are in severe suffering then too.

It calls for organised evacuation.

Which, to work, has to account for families' animals.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets two things right, and they deserve saying. The first is that human pain in a war is immense and urgent, and that speaking about anything else calls for care with tone and timing. The second is that there is a way of defending animals that really does sound cold: comparing victims, arguing over who suffers more, using someone else's tragedy as an argument. That exists, and it doesn't help (see Stop saying this).

It gets a third thing right, which has to be conceded: donated resources are finite, and what goes one way doesn't go the other. That is true, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

But

But none of that makes it wrong to care for the animals of a war zone. The real cost is small next to what the sentence suggests, most of the effort isn't contested with human relief, and in many cases it is the opposite: people only agree to leave when the animals can come along. Above all, a cost existing doesn't close the question. It exists in every use of resources, and only becomes an objection when the one being helped is an animal.
