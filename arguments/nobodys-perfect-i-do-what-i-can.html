E10

"Nobody's perfect; I do what I can"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the phrase is true in general, and often used to avoid doing what is easy. No one has to be perfect, and veganism doesn't ask for that. But for people with access to stores, changing what one eats is one of the most accessible and impactful changes anyone can make, for animals and for the environment too. Not doing everything doesn't justify not doing what's simple. "I do what I can" only works as an answer if the person is actually doing what they can.

Reply

You don't have to be perfect, and veganism doesn't ask for that. But "I do what I can" only holds if you really are doing what you can, which often isn't the case. With access to stores, changing what you eat is one of the most accessible and impactful changes there is. Recycling is good, but it doesn't make up for the animals who die for what's on your plate. Not doing everything doesn't justify not doing what's simple.

For children

No one has to be perfect, and everyone makes mistakes. But imagine a friend who says: "I can't stop pushing you; nobody's perfect." Actually, he can: stopping is easy! When something is possible and helps a lot, "nobody's perfect" isn't an excuse.

Ask a question back

"Is giving up animals beyond what you can do, or just something you haven't wanted to do yet?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"I do my part. Everyone at their own pace. At least I recycle."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1No one can live without causing any harm.
  2. P2Demanding moral perfection is unrealistic and paralyzing.
  3. P3Each person has different limits, priorities and circumstances.
  4. CSo no one can be required to stop eating animals; it's enough for each person to do what they can.

P1, P2 and P3 are true. The problem lies in the conclusion: not being able to demand everything doesn't mean nothing can be asked, and giving up eating animals is usually among what a person can do.

The argument is valid if "what I can" is understood honestly. It fails when the phrase is used to avoid doing what the person could in fact do.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The phrase can be used in two ways:

    UseWhat it meansLegitimate?
    Honest"I have real limits and I do what's within my reach"Yes
    Evasive"Since I can't do everything, I don't need to do what I can"No

    The test is simple: is the person not giving up animals for lack of means or for lack of will?

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. No one is asking for perfection.

    The classic definition of veganism speaks of avoiding exploitation "as far as is possible and practicable" (D7). What's asked is reasonable effort, not purity.

    2. Changing one's diet is accessible.

    For most people with access to stores, swapping meat for beans, lentils, chickpeas, tofu and other foods is possible day to day (J1).

    3. The impact is large.

    Someone who stops eating animals stops paying, year after year, for animals to be bred and killed for their own consumption. The effect goes beyond the animals: one of the most comprehensive analyses to date of the environmental impact of food (Poore and Nemecek, 2018) estimated that removing animal products from diets would cut food's land use by about 76% and food's greenhouse gas emissions by about 49%. Few individual changes within reach of people with access to stores have a comparable effect (K1).

    4. Other good deeds don't compensate.

    Recycling, donating or caring for a dog are good deeds, but they don't undo the harm done to other animals. It isn't a ledger of credits and debits.

    5. "Everyone at their own pace" is reasonable when there is a pace.

    If change is under way, the pace matters. If there's no change, the phrase describes a decision not to change (E15).

    6. Imperfection in some areas doesn't license harm in others.

    That it's impossible to avoid all harm doesn't make easily avoidable harm acceptable.

    7. Excusable isn't the same as exempt.

    When something is very hard, failing can be excusable: we blame less someone who tried and couldn't. But that concerns the guilt of the person acting, not what ought to be done. And there's a practical difference: if an action is required but hard, there's a duty to get ready to manage it, by seeking information, recipes, support and practice. "I do what I can" works as an excuse for someone trying to improve what they can, not as a permanent exemption (Cunha, 2022, pp. 223–224).

    8. Assessing the act isn't the same as blaming the person.

    Peter Singer, in Practical Ethics, separates two kinds of assessment: praise and blame concern the agent; right and wrong concern the act. Discussing donations against poverty, he notes that blaming people who already give something for not giving more may be counterproductive, since they may feel the demand is too much and give up altogether; and he recalls that between an ethic for saints and an ethic that demands nothing there are intermediate positions. But he adds that this says nothing in favour of those who make no effort at all (ch. 8). Singer is dealing with human poverty; the transfer is this guide's. "I do what I can" may be a good reason not to blame someone who is changing (point 7), but it doesn't change what the act is, and it doesn't apply to someone who is doing nothing.

    9. Doing what one can starts with the minimum.

    Not exploiting other individuals for one's own benefit isn't perfection, it's the minimum of justice, and so it comes before extra good deeds. No one would say they do what they can for other people while still exploiting someone when they could avoid it. Veganism doesn't demand perfection; it demands that minimum, as far as is possible and practicable.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: people have different priorities

    The most serious version says each person chooses their causes: one devotes themselves to the environment, another to poverty, another to animals. Demanding the same cause of everyone would be unfair.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Not causing harm isn't a cause; it's a limit.

    No one can choose another cause as an excuse to harm someone.

    Choosing causes applies to helping, not to not harming.

    No one is obliged to devote themselves to animals; paying for them to be killed is something else.

    Causes don't compete.

    Changing one's diet doesn't prevent devoting oneself to other causes.

Contradictions

Doesn't accept "nobody's perfect" for harm done to them.

No one accepts being harmed by someone who "does what they can".

Makes hard changes in other areas.

Exercise, work and study take effort.

Recognizes they could eat less meat.

Which shows the change is within reach.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that nobody's perfect, that demanding purity drives people away, and that everyone has real limits. Some people face genuine difficulties in changing their diet, and they deserve understanding. Celebrating each step also helps more than condemning.

But

But "I do what I can" is a promise, not an excuse. It's only kept when the person actually does what they can, and for most people that includes keeping animals off the plate.
