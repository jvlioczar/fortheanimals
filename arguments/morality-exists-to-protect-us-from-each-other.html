A23

"Morality exists to protect us from each other"

The answer in one sentence

if morality applied only to those who can threaten or repay us, it would also leave out babies, children and incapacitated people, and no one accepts that. Besides, explaining how a practice arose doesn't show it's right: the first astronomers got things wrong too. And it's false that people only respect those they fear: many people protect precisely those who can't defend themselves.

Reply

If morality applied only to those who can threaten us, babies and incapacitated people would be left out too. Is that the morality you stand for? And even if morality began as an agreement for protection, the origin of an idea doesn't prove it's right. Astronomy also began full of mistakes. In the end, the question is whether it's fair to make suffer those who can't defend themselves.

For children

A baby can't hit anyone or make promises. Even so, everyone knows it's very wrong to hurt a baby. So we're not good to others only because they can hit back. We look after those who are weak precisely because they can't defend themselves. Animals can't either.

Ask a question back

"Do you think you only owe respect to those who can hit back?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"Morality is an agreement between people so no one harms anyone. Animals aren't part of the deal, because they can't threaten us."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Morality arose and exists as an agreement of mutual protection between individuals who can harm one another.
  2. P2It only makes sense to include in the agreement those who can keep it or break it.
  3. P3Animals can't take part in the agreement or threaten us in any relevant way.
  4. CSo we have no direct moral duties toward animals.

The idea has a long tradition, from Hobbes to contemporary versions of contractualism, such as Peter Carruthers's. It's related to A21 ("No duties, no rights"), but it rests on the origin and function of morality rather than on reciprocity of duties.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1, which confuses origin with justification, and at P2, which would exclude many humans.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument can be read in three ways:

    ReadingWhat it claimsProblem
    DescriptivePeople only respect those they fearFalse as a generalization
    About originMorality was born of self-interestOrigin isn't justification
    NormativeWe should only respect those who can threaten usExcludes vulnerable humans
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It excludes vulnerable humans.

    Babies, small children and people with certain illnesses or disabilities have no power to threaten us or take part in agreements. By the argument, we could do to them what we do to animals.

    2. The description is false.

    Some people only respect those they fear, but many others protect precisely those who are defenseless. Saying "that's what humanity is like" is usually a generalization from oneself.

    3. The origin is probably not that.

    Studies of animal behavior suggest that morality has roots in altruistic and caring behaviors far older than any self-interested agreement.

    4. Origin isn't justification.

    As an explanation of where morality came from, the argument has something plausible about it: moral norms seem to have arisen from group life, from the complexity of relationships and from the need to cooperate and settle conflicts. But these are two different questions. One is about origin: how did we come to have morality? The other is about justification: whom should it apply to now, and in the best way? Answering the first doesn't answer the second. Writing, as far as the oldest records from Mesopotamia indicate, arose mainly for bookkeeping, and no one concludes that today it should only serve to count sacks of grain. Likewise, even if morality had been born of fear, that wouldn't show this conception is the correct one, nor that its scope must stay confined to the group where it began. It's like trying to prove a calculation is right by showing that's how people calculated thousands of years ago.

    5. Being a beneficiary doesn't require being an agent.

    Someone treated by a doctor doesn't need to be a doctor. Someone protected by morality doesn't need to be capable of acting morally (A21).

    6. Moral agents are the instruments of morality, not its only recipients.

    For the philosopher Oscar Horta, morality depends on agents able to act for reasons, just as journeys depend on vehicles; but journeys don't exist to serve cars, and morality doesn't exist only to serve those who practise it. Caring for the vulnerable is in fact one of the clearest uses of moral capacity (Cunha, 2022, p. 59).

    7. A morality only among those who can reciprocate would exclude humans too.

    If morality were a pact of mutual protection, those who can neither threaten nor reciprocate, such as a baby or a person with a severe disability, would only count indirectly. A pact made without knowing who we'd be, human or animal, strong or weak, would reject that rule (Cunha, 2022, p. 213; A21).

    8. The contract would also leave out the enslaved, poor nations and future generations.

    The idea that morality is a pact to avoid suffering injustice is old: Plato puts it in Glaucon's mouth, in the Republic, so that Socrates can refute it. Peter Singer, in Practical Ethics, sees echoes of it in John Rawls and David Gauthier, and, like point 4, separates explaining the origin of ethics from justifying it: however self-interested its origins, reason isn't bound by them (ch. 3). He points to consequences that go beyond animals. Slave traders had no self-interested reason to treat the enslaved better, since enslaved Africans couldn't retaliate; an abolitionist who was only a contractualist would have had no reply. By the same criterion, rich countries would owe nothing to poor ones that can't reciprocate. And we would owe nothing to future generations, who can do nothing for us: it would be enough to store nuclear waste, which stays dangerous far longer, in containers that last a hundred years. Some reply that the capacity for reciprocity is enough, even if it is never exercised. Singer notes that this version has already abandoned reciprocity, since future generations can never reciprocate, and then no reason is left to limit morality to those who would have that capacity. The contractualists he cites soften the reach of the exclusion in different ways, and some versions of the contract include animals (A21, point 9).

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the contract protects the vulnerable indirectly

    The most serious version, Carruthers's, admits that babies don't make contracts. But it says the contractors have an interest in protecting them, because they love them and because allowing abuse against them would threaten social stability. So vulnerable humans would be protected indirectly, and animals wouldn't.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Protection becomes dependent on other people's affection.

    A baby with no one to love them would lose protection. Almost no one accepts that.

    The same would hold for animals.

    Many people love animals and are disturbed by cruelty. By the same logic, animals should also be protected indirectly.

    It puts the wrong in the wrong place.

    If hurting a baby is wrong only because it upsets adults, the victim isn't the baby. Most people feel the victim is the baby.

    Excluding animals can also have costs for humans.

    Carruthers follows John Rawls, for whom excluding humans without moral capacity would be a risk to just institutions, and says excluding animals carries no such risk. The political philosopher Alasdair Cochrane replies that a society that accepts inflicting grave and unnecessary suffering on animals may become more tolerant of suffering inflicted on humans (Cochrane, 2010, pp. 57–59). It's an empirical hypothesis, not an established fact: some studies find associations, such as that of Fitzgerald, Kalof and Dietz (2009), which linked slaughterhouse employment to higher crime rates in US counties, but these are correlations that don't prove causation.

    Some animals may meet the criteria.

    The argument assumes no animal can be party to the agreement. Cochrane points out that zoologists and philosophers debate whether great apes and cetaceans have capacities sufficient to be seen as moral and autonomous beings, and that ignoring that debate isn't justified (Cochrane, 2010, p. 59; see A20).

    Stability doesn't prove justice.

    Almost every society in history has excluded groups of humans from justice and still remained stable. That a rule provokes no revolt shows it's tolerated, not that it's just (Cochrane, 2010, p. 59).

    Excluding animals entirely would also provoke resistance.

    Many people would oppose a justice that said nothing about the limits of what we do to animals (Cochrane, 2010, p. 59). In the 2023 Eurobarometer survey, 84% of respondents across the 27 European Union countries said the welfare of farmed animals should be better protected than it is now in their country. By the stability criterion itself, animals would have to be included.

Contradictions

Who can neither threaten nor make agreements.

Condemns cruelty to dogs.

Who aren't party to any contract either.

But admires those who sacrifice themselves for those who can't repay them.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Cooperation and reciprocity are an important part of moral life, and many social rules work as agreements. It's also true that only agents capable of understanding rules can be held responsible for them. And, as a hypothesis about the origin of morality, the idea that it was born of the relationships and conflicts of group life is a strong one.

But

But a good explanation of where morality came from doesn't say whom it should apply to now. And being held responsible and being protected are different things. The morality we know protects many who never signed any agreement.
