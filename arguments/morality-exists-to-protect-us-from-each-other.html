A23

"Morality exists to protect us from each other"

The answer in one sentence

if morality applied only to those who can threaten or repay us, it would also leave out babies, children and incapacitated people, and no one accepts that. Besides, explaining how a practice arose doesn't show it's right: the first astronomers got things wrong too. And it's false that people only respect those they fear: many people protect precisely those who can't defend themselves.

Short answer

If morality applied only to those who can threaten us, babies and incapacitated people would be left out too, and no one accepts that. And even if morality began as an agreement for protection, the origin of an idea doesn't prove it's right. Astronomy also began full of mistakes. The question is whether it's fair to make suffer those who can't defend themselves.

For children

A baby can't hit anyone or make promises. Even so, everyone knows it's very wrong to hurt a baby. So we're not good to others only because they can hit back. We look after those who are weak precisely because they can't defend themselves. Animals can't either.

Ask a question back

"Do you think you only owe respect to those who can hit back?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Morality is an agreement between people so no one harms anyone. Animals aren't part of the deal, because they can't threaten us."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Morality arose and exists as an agreement of mutual protection between individuals who can harm one another.
  2. P2It only makes sense to include in the agreement those who can keep it or break it.
  3. P3Animals can't take part in the agreement or threaten us in any relevant way.
  4. CSo we have no direct moral duties toward animals.

The idea has a long tradition, from Hobbes to contemporary versions of contractualism, such as Peter Carruthers's. It's related to A7 ("No duties, no rights"), but it rests on the origin and function of morality rather than on reciprocity of duties.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P1, which confuses origin with justification, and at P2, which would exclude many humans.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument can be read in three ways:

    ReadingWhat it claimsProblem
    DescriptivePeople only respect those they fearFalse as a generalization
    About originMorality was born of self-interestOrigin isn't justification
    NormativeWe should only respect those who can threaten usExcludes vulnerable humans
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. It excludes vulnerable humans.

    Babies, small children and people with certain illnesses or disabilities have no power to threaten us or take part in agreements. By the argument, we could do to them what we do to animals.

    2. The description is false.

    Some people only respect those they fear, but many others protect precisely those who are defenseless. Saying "that's what humanity is like" is usually a generalization from oneself.

    3. The origin is probably not that.

    Studies of animal behavior suggest that morality has roots in altruistic and caring behaviors far older than any self-interested agreement.

    4. Origin isn't justification.

    Even if morality had been born of fear, that wouldn't show this conception is the correct one. It's like trying to prove a calculation is right by showing that's how people calculated thousands of years ago.

    5. Being a beneficiary doesn't require being an agent.

    Someone treated by a doctor doesn't need to be a doctor. Someone protected by morality doesn't need to be capable of acting morally (A7).

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the contract protects the vulnerable indirectly

    The most serious version, Carruthers's, admits that babies don't make contracts. But it says the contractors have an interest in protecting them, because they love them and because allowing abuse against them would threaten social stability. So vulnerable humans would be protected indirectly, and animals wouldn't.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Protection becomes dependent on other people's affection.

    A baby with no one to love them would lose protection. Almost no one accepts that.

    The same would hold for animals.

    Many people love animals and are disturbed by cruelty. By the same logic, animals should also be protected indirectly.

    It puts the wrong in the wrong place.

    If hurting a baby is wrong only because it upsets adults, the victim isn't the baby. Most people feel the victim is the baby.

Contradictions

Protects babies and incapacitated people.

Who can neither threaten nor make agreements.

Condemns cruelty to dogs.

Who aren't party to any contract either.

Says morality is just self-protection.

But admires those who sacrifice themselves for those who can't repay them.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Cooperation and reciprocity are an important part of moral life, and many social rules work as agreements. It's also true that only agents capable of understanding rules can be held responsible for them.

But

But being held responsible and being protected are different things. The morality we know protects many who never signed any agreement.
