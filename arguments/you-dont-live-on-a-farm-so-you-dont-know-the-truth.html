B16

"You don't live on a farm, so you don't know the truth"

The answer in one sentence

people who live in the countryside know many things city people don't, and they are worth listening to. But the practices under discussion — slaughter age, separating calves, breeding for fast growth, stocking density in sheds — aren't secrets known only to those who live on farms: they are described by the industry itself, in technical manuals, regulations and scientific assessments. And the argument has a bigger problem: it doesn't say anything in the critique is wrong; it only says the critic has no right to speak. Many people who lived and worked on farms reached the same conclusion as vegans.

Note: this chapter is about the appeal to experience. The appeal to technical authority is B9, and the claim that the footage shows exceptions is B15

Short answer

What is being criticised isn't a secret of country life. Slaughter age, separating calves, shed stocking density and breeding for fast growth are in the technical manuals, the regulations and the scientific reports of the industry itself. And the argument doesn't say the critique is wrong: it says whoever makes it can't speak. If that held, nobody could have an opinion on anything they don't do every day. Besides, many people who lived and worked on farms reached the same conclusion.

Question back: "What exactly did I say that's wrong? If it's that, show me, and I'll change my mind."

For children

People who live in the countryside know lots about plants and animals, and it's good to learn from them. But you don't need to live there to know that a calf cries when it's taken from its mother, or that a chicken lives only a few weeks: the people who raise the animals write that in their own books. And many people who lived in the countryside also thought this was wrong.

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Easy to talk from a flat in the city. Come live on a farm, see what it's really like, and then we'll talk."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Knowledge of a practice comes largely from direct experience of it.
  2. P2People who criticise animal farming have, as a rule, never lived on a farm.
  3. P3Images and accounts from outside pick the worst cases and don't show everyday life.
  4. CSo the critique comes from people who don't know the reality, and shouldn't be taken seriously.

P1 is partly true, and P3 points to a real risk in any exposé. P2 is a generalisation that is often false. The central problem is the leap to C, which swaps assessing what is said for assessing who says it.

The argument is invalid: even if P1 to P3 were true, the critique could be correct. Knowing whether a claim is true requires examining it, not examining the address of whoever made it.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The sentence can mean three different things, and only the first deserves an answer on the merits.

    What is meantIs it an argument?Answer
    "That fact you cited is wrong"YesCheck the fact, and correct it if need be
    "You don't know what I know"PartlySay what it is, so it can be examined
    "You have no right to speak"NoWho says something doesn't change whether it's true

    The argument almost always arrives in the form of the third row, leaning on the legitimacy of the first.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The practices are documented by the industry itself.

    Nobody needs privileged access to know at what age broiler chickens are slaughtered, how calf separation works in dairy, or what density is allowed in a shed. That information is in technical manuals, regulations and scientific assessments, such as the European food safety authority's 2023 opinion on broiler chickens. The critique usually starts from what the industry itself says it does.

    2. Attacking the source doesn't refute the content.

    Saying "you live in the city" doesn't show that any claim is false. It is the genetic fallacy, or an attack on the person: who speaks is assessed instead of what is said.

    3. The criterion, applied consistently, silences everyone.

    If only those who live a practice could have an opinion on it, nobody could discuss prisons without having been imprisoned, nor child labour without having worked as a child, nor industrial policy without having been a factory worker. And the criterion silences its users too: most people who eat meat have never lived on a farm.

    4. Proximity brings knowledge, but also habit.

    Someone who grows up with a practice learns a great deal about it and, at the same time, learns to see it as normal. Familiarity isn't impartiality, and an outside perspective is exactly what allows asking what, from inside, doesn't even look like a question.

    5. Many people who lived on farms reached the same conclusion.

    Howard Lyman, a fourth-generation cattle rancher in the United States, went vegan and became a critic of animal agriculture. Initiatives such as the Transfarmation Project, in the United States, support farmers who want to leave intensive animal farming. Living on a farm doesn't, by itself, lead to one conclusion.

    6. Experience can be brought into the conversation.

    If people who live in the countryside know something that contradicts the critique, the way to use that knowledge is to say it. The argument doesn't do that: it just declares that the other person doesn't know, without saying what.

  4. d

    The best reply: situated knowledge

    The serious version isn't the attack. It is the observation that the technical, economic and cultural knowledge of people who live off the land is often dismissed in urban debates, and that proposals made without listening to farmers tend to fail and to push the cost onto those with the least.

  5. e

    Why the reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It asks to include a voice, not to exclude another.

    Listening to people who live off the land is necessary; silencing critics doesn't follow from it.

    It applies to the transition, not to the moral question.

    Knowing how to change without harming rural families is knowledge farmers have. Knowing whether animals should be killed doesn't depend on where you live (J12).

    Used well, it strengthens the critique.

    Many of the strongest accounts of what happens on farms come from people who worked on them.

Contradictions

The person saying it often doesn't live on a farm either.

And has opinions about farming with no trouble.

They have opinions on many things they don't live.

Politics, football, public health, wars.

They accept the account of someone who worked on a farm when it agrees with them.

And reject it when it doesn't.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It gets something important right: people who live in the countryside have knowledge that urban debates tend to ignore, and critiques made without it often get things wrong. Some animal advocates repeat false or exaggerated claims about animal farming, and that weakens the cause (see Stop saying this). It also gets right that changes made without listening to rural families tend to fail and to push the cost onto those with the least.

But

But what is being criticised doesn't depend on living on a farm to be known, and who says something doesn't change whether it's true. The way to use the experience of people who live in the countryside is to bring it into the conversation, not to use it to end it.
