G9

"Soy causes female hormones and cancer"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about health and nutrition, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on health grounds, anyone who manages to be healthy eating animals, and many people do, would have no reason to change. And if an animal product turned out to be demonstrably healthier, the same argument would start defending exploitation. The eater's health says nothing about whether what is done to the animal is just.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

soy isoflavones are phytoestrogens, not estrogen, and they act differently in the body. A 2021 meta-analysis of 41 studies concluded that neither soy nor isoflavones alter testosterone or estrogen in men. As for breast cancer, studies in humans link soy consumption to the same or lower risk.

Short answer

Soy isoflavones are phytoestrogens, which aren't estrogen and act differently. A 2021 meta-analysis of 41 studies concluded that neither soy nor isoflavones alter testosterone or estrogen in men. Studies in humans link eating soy to the same or lower risk of breast cancer. The scares came from rodent studies at high doses, which don't carry over well to people.

For children

Soy has some substances that look a little bit like hormones, but they aren't the same and don't do the same things. Scientists have studied this a lot and found that eating soy doesn't change people's hormones. In Asia, people have eaten tofu and other soy foods for a very long time.

Ask a question back

"If studies in people show no effect on hormones, why trust the scare that came from studies in rats more?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Soy feminizes men. Soy causes breast cancer. Soy is GMO poison."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Soy contains isoflavones, which can bind to estrogen receptors.
  2. P2Animal studies, using high doses, have shown hormonal effects.
  3. P3Hormonal effects can alter fertility and increase the risk of hormone-sensitive cancers.
  4. CSo eating soy is risky.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3 as applied to humans: studies in people, eating normal amounts of food, don't confirm these risks.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails because it carries results from rodent studies at high doses over to normal human consumption.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses three things:

    ClaimWhat the evidence says
    Isoflavones are estrogenNo: they're phytoestrogens, with different and much weaker effects
    Soy alters male hormonesNo, according to a meta-analysis of 41 studies in men
    Soy increases breast cancerNo; in humans, eating soy foods is linked to the same or lower risk
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Phytoestrogens aren't estrogen.

    Isoflavones bind to estrogen receptors selectively, and in some tissues they can even block the effect of stronger estrogen.

    2. There's no effect on male hormones.

    In 2021, a meta-analysis of 41 studies concluded that, regardless of dose and duration, neither soy protein nor isoflavones altered total testosterone, free testosterone, estradiol or estrone in men.

    3. Soy doesn't increase breast cancer.

    The American Cancer Society explains that rodent studies don't carry over well to humans, and that in people, soy foods are linked to the same or lower risk of cancer.

    4. Studies of those who eat the most soy don't show the alleged risk.

    In Asian populations, where tofu, miso and tempeh are common, soy consumption is linked to the same or lower risk of breast cancer. Tradition alone wouldn't prove safety; what carries weight is these studies.

    5. Most soy goes to animals.

    According to Our World in Data, about 77% of the world's soy is used as animal feed, and only a small share becomes direct human food, such as tofu and soy drinks. This says nothing about hormones, since meat eaters don't take in the isoflavones in animal feed; it says something about where the demand for soy, and the deforestation linked to it, comes from (H1).

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    Reed et al., 2021, *Reproductive Toxicology*Neither soy nor isoflavones affect male reproductive hormonesMeta-analysis of 41 studies
    American Cancer Society, soy and cancer riskSoy foods are linked to the same or lower cancer risk; rodent studies don't apply well to humansOfficial guidance
    Our World in Data, soyAbout 77% of soy is used as animal feedGlobal data
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: isoflavone supplements are something else

    The most serious version points out that concentrated isoflavone supplements have much higher doses than foods, and that the American Cancer Society itself doesn't recommend them.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It applies to supplements, not foods.

    Tofu, soy milk and tempeh aren't concentrated supplements.

    A vegan diet doesn't require soy.

    There are many other legumes.

    Caution about supplements doesn't change the conclusion about foods.

    The American Cancer Society, which doesn't recommend the supplements, considers soy foods safe.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    If soy doesn't carry the risks attributed to it, and a vegan diet doesn't even need it, the argument loses its force. The moral question remains: does fear of a food justify raising and killing animals?

Contradictions

Eats meat from soy-fed animals.

Soy is at the base of livestock farming.

Doesn't fear other foods with phytoestrogens.

They're in many vegetables, grains and beer.

Trusts rodent studies only in this case.

On other subjects, demands studies in humans.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that isoflavones interact with estrogen receptors, and that concentrated supplements deserve caution. Concern about hormonal health is legitimate, and the subject was studied precisely because of it.

But

But studies in humans eating soy foods don't confirm the fears. Soy is safe, a vegan diet doesn't even depend on it, and most of the world's soy feeds the animals who become meat.
