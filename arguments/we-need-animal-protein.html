G2

"We need animal protein"

Before answering: an indirect argumentThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about health and nutrition, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on health grounds, anyone who manages to be healthy eating animals, and many people do, would have no reason to change. And if an animal product turned out to be demonstrably healthier, the same argument would start defending exploitation. The eater's health says nothing about whether what is done to the animal is just.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

we need protein, not animal protein. Legumes such as beans, lentils, chickpeas and soy, along with grains, nuts and seeds, provide all the protein and all the essential amino acids an adult needs, as long as the diet is varied and supplies enough energy. Nutrition bodies in several countries, such as the US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, the British Dietetic Association and Dietitians of Canada, recognize that well-planned plant-based diets can be adequate at all stages of life; others, such as the German Nutrition Society, don't yet take a position for pregnant women, children and older people, and call for professional supervision.

Short answer

We need protein, not animal protein. Beans, lentils, chickpeas, soy, tofu, nuts and grains provide all the protein and all the amino acids we need, as long as we eat a varied diet over the day and in sufficient quantity. There's no need to combine foods in the same meal. The US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, the British Dietetic Association and Dietitians of Canada recognize that well-planned plant-based diets can be adequate at all stages of life. Rice and beans, a staple from Latin America to the Caribbean and West Africa, is already an excellent source of protein.

For children

Protein helps the body grow and get strong. And it isn't only in meat! Beans, lentils, chickpeas, peanuts and soy have lots of protein. Rice and beans, for example, is a great combination. And lots of people, even athletes, don't eat meat and get all the protein they need.

Ask a question back

"If beans, lentils and soy provide all the protein we need, is the need for protein or for meat?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Where do you get your protein? Without meat you'll be weak. You need quality protein."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Human beings need protein, with all the essential amino acids.
  2. P2Animal foods are concentrated, complete sources of protein.
  3. P3It's hard to get enough complete protein from plants alone.
  4. CSo we need to eat animal products.

P1 and P2 are true. The problem lies in P3: the evidence shows that varied plant diets provide enough complete protein for adults.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses the nutrient with the source:

    What the body needsWhere it can come from
    ProteinMeat, eggs, milk, but also beans, lentils, chickpeas, soy, tofu, nuts, grains
    Essential amino acidsPlant foods contain all nine essential amino acids, but in different proportions: cereals are low in lysine, which legumes make up for. Variety over the day solves it
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Plant protein is enough.

    A 2019 review concluded that protein-rich foods such as legumes, nuts and seeds are enough to ensure protein adequacy for adults on vegetarian and vegan diets.

    2. The amino acids are in plants.

    The idea that foods must be combined in the same meal is an old myth. Eating a varied diet over the day provides all the essential amino acids (G3).

    3. Nutrition bodies recognize adequacy.

    The US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics states that well-planned vegan diets are healthy, nutritionally adequate and appropriate for all stages of life, including pregnancy, childhood and for athletes. The British Dietetic Association says that carefully planned plant-based diets can support healthy living at every age and life stage, and notes that vitamin B12 has to come from fortified foods or a supplement. In Canada, a joint position of Dietitians of Canada with the American Dietetic Association reached the same conclusion about all stages of the life cycle. It isn't unanimous: the German Nutrition Society considers a vegan diet adequate for healthy adults but, for lack of data, neither recommends nor advises against it for pregnant women, children and older people, and calls for professional supervision. None of these bodies says animal protein is necessary.

    4. Traditional plates are already rich in protein.

    Rice and beans, dal and rice, hummus and bread: traditional combinations around the world provide good-quality protein.

    5. Animal protein comes from plants.

    Chickens and pigs get their protein from soy and corn feed; cattle, from pasture and grain, with the help of rumen microorganisms that the human digestive system lacks. So it doesn't work to say "if the ox gets strong eating grass, so would we": the digestion is different. The point is another: eating the animal means getting, by a longer route and with more losses, nutrients that came from plants, and humans get enough protein by eating legumes, grains and seeds directly.

    Data and sources

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: some groups need more attention

    The most serious version grants that healthy adults get enough protein from plants, but points out that older people, children and athletes have greater needs, and that some vegans eat less protein than they should, especially those who eat little overall. Mariotti and Gardner's review acknowledges these risks and adds that the digestibility of plant protein is only a few percentage points lower.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Needing attention isn't needing meat.

    Groups with greater needs can meet them with more legumes, soy and planning.

    Every diet requires attention.

    Many people who eat meat also have inadequate diets.

    The solution is planning, not exploitation.

    Guidance from a dietitian solves most cases.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    This is an indirect argument: it doesn't say animals don't matter, but that we need them. Since the need doesn't hold for those with access to a varied diet, the question returns to its starting point: if we don't need it, what justifies making animals suffer and die?

What the argument gets right

What is true

Concern about nutrition is legitimate. Protein is essential, and any diet, vegan or not, needs to be planned to provide enough. Some groups, such as older people and children, deserve special attention.

But

But the need is for protein, not for animals. With beans, lentils, soy and variety, protein is covered, and the moral question becomes the main one again: if we don't need it, why carry on?
