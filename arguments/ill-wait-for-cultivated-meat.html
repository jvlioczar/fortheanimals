K5

"I'll wait for cultivated meat"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about effectiveness and individual responsibility, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended only by each person's impact, anyone who proved their purchase changes nothing would be let off. But no one accepts this for other harms: taking part in an injustice is still wrong even when each person's share is small.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

the argument postpones the obligation until it's painless. Cultivated meat may one day help a great deal, but it still faces major challenges of cost and scale: a 2021 analysis concluded that, under the conditions studied, it would be hard to make it affordable as food, and other analyses are more optimistic. Meanwhile, billions of animals keep being killed. Waiting for a future solution doesn't justify causing harm in the present, when alternatives already exist.

Short answer

Cultivated meat could help a lot in the future, but it still faces major challenges of cost and scale, and no one knows when it will be widely available. Meanwhile, billions of animals keep being killed. Waiting for a future solution doesn't justify causing harm in the present, when alternatives exist today: beans, lentils, tofu and many other foods.

For children

Some people are trying to make meat in a lab, without needing to kill animals. That could be really good in the future. But it will still take a while, and meanwhile many animals keep suffering. Today there are already lots of tasty foods that don't need animals.

Ask a question back

"If cultivated meat takes 20 years, how many animals will have died while you waited?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"In ten years technology will fix it. When it's cheap, I'll switch."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Cultivated meat can offer meat without raising and killing animals.
  2. P2When it's available and cheap, many people will switch effortlessly.
  3. P3It's more effective to wait for a technological solution than to demand individual sacrifices.
  4. CSo I can keep eating meat until then.

P1 is true in principle. P2 is plausible. The problem lies in the conclusion: the date is uncertain, and the harm continues while we wait.

The argument is valid if waiting had no cost. Its soundness fails because the cost is the lives of the animals killed in the meantime.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument postpones change to an uncertain future:

    MomentSituation
    TodayCheap plant alternatives are available
    Uncertain futureCultivated meat may become affordable
    MeanwhileAnimals keep being raised and killed
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The date is uncertain.

    Cultivated meat was approved for sale in Singapore in 2020 and in the United States in 2023, but it's still produced on a small scale, and there's serious debate about when, or whether, it will be cheap.

    2. There are challenges of cost and scale.

    A 2021 techno-economic analysis by David Humbird concluded that production costs, under the conditions studied, would hardly allow an affordable food. Other analyses, from the industry itself, are more optimistic.

    3. The harm continues while we wait.

    Each year of waiting means billions of animals killed.

    4. Alternatives already exist.

    No waiting is needed to stop eating animals.

    5. It postpones the obligation until it's painless.

    If causing the harm is wrong, future convenience doesn't make it acceptable in the present.

    6. "It isn't natural" isn't a moral objection.

    Being natural doesn't tell us whether something is good (C1). What matters is whether cultivated meat harms animals. The real problems, such as the use of fetal bovine serum in early processes, are technical and are being replaced by animal-free growth media. Whether cultivated meat is vegan is an open debate; see The definition of veganism, debated.

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: technology is what changes the world

    The most serious version says mass change rarely comes from individual moral choices, but from technologies that make the alternative cheaper and more convenient.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    That may be true at the level of society.

    But it doesn't excuse the individual who can already change.

    Demand for alternatives speeds up the technology.

    Those who change today help create tomorrow's market.

    The duty doesn't wait.

    The animal who dies today doesn't benefit from future technology.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The argument admits that killing animals is a problem, since it waits for a solution. If it's a problem, the question is why keep causing it while the alternatives already exist.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Indefinite postponement

Epistemological

Technological optimism
Assumes the solution will come soon and cheap

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Convenience as a condition

Biases

Present bias

The cost of changing today weighs more than the future benefit

General literature on temporal discounting

Optimism bias

The technological solution seems near and certain

General literature on optimism bias

Pro-innovation bias

Cultivated meat is treated as a guaranteed solution, without counting its limits of cost, scale and timing

What the argument gets right

What is true

Cultivated meat and other technologies could profoundly transform food production, and it's reasonable to bet on them. For many people, change will be easier when there are identical, cheap alternatives.

But

But future technology doesn't change what happens today. Until it arrives, plant alternatives already exist, and every animal spared now matters.
