G4

"You'll lack iron, zinc and other micronutrients"

Before answering: an indirect argument

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about health and nutrition, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended on health grounds, anyone who manages to be healthy eating animals, and many people do, would have no reason to change. And if an animal product turned out to be demonstrably healthier, the same argument would start defending exploitation. The eater's health says nothing about whether what is done to the animal is just.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

The answer in one sentence

iron, zinc and other micronutrients can be obtained from plants with a varied diet and some planning. Iron deficiency is the most common nutritional deficiency in the world, and eating meat doesn't prevent it: about 30% of women aged 15 to 49 have anemia, in a population where most people eat meat. Vegetarians and vegans tend to have lower iron stores, which calls for attention and blood tests, not a return to meat.

Short answer

Iron from plants is absorbed at a lower rate, but there's plenty of iron in beans, lentils, chickpeas, tofu and dark leafy greens, and vitamin C in the meal increases absorption. Cooking, soaking and sprouting reduce the phytates that get in the way of zinc. And anemia is common on every diet: it affects about 30% of women aged 15 to 49 worldwide, and eating meat doesn't prevent it. Lower iron stores call for attention and blood tests, not meat.

For children

Iron helps the blood carry oxygen around the body. It's in beans, lentils, dark green leaves and lots of other foods. A trick: eating a fruit with vitamin C, like an orange, with your beans helps your body make better use of the iron.

Ask a question back

"If eating meat doesn't prevent anemia, is the solution meat or a well-planned diet?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"Vegans are always anemic. Iron from plants isn't absorbed."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Iron from plants (non-heme) is absorbed at a lower rate than iron from meat (heme).
  2. P2Zinc from plants is also less bioavailable, because of phytates.
  3. P3So vegans are at greater risk of deficiency in these minerals.
  4. CSo a meat-free diet is risky for health.

P1 and P2 are true. P3 is partly true: studies show vegetarians have, on average, lower iron stores. The problem lies in the conclusion: a risk that can be managed with planning doesn't make the diet inadequate.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at the conclusion: "greater risk" becomes "too risky", when the risk can be managed.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument turns a point of attention into an obstacle:

    NutrientPlant sourcesWhat improves absorption
    IronBeans, lentils, chickpeas, tofu, dark leafy greens, seedsVitamin C in the same meal; avoiding coffee and tea alongside
    ZincLegumes, nuts, seeds, whole grainsSoaking, sprouting, fermenting
    IodineIodized saltUsing iodized salt
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. There are many plant sources.

    Iron and zinc are found in everyday foods such as beans, lentils and grains.

    2. Absorption can be improved.

    Vitamin C increases the absorption of non-heme iron. Simple techniques, such as soaking legumes, reduce phytates.

    3. Eating meat doesn't prevent deficiency.

    According to the WHO, about 30% of non-pregnant women aged 15 to 49 and about 40% of young children worldwide have anemia, and iron deficiency is the main nutritional cause. The figure doesn't prove that meat causes anemia, since most people eat meat; it shows that eating meat isn't enough to prevent it.

    4. Nutrition bodies consider the diet adequate.

    The US Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics recognizes that well-planned vegan diets are adequate, with attention to these nutrients.

    5. Attention isn't impossibility.

    Every diet has points of attention. Diets with meat carry risks linked to saturated fat and processed meat (G13).

  3. c

    Data and sources

    SourceWhat it saysNote
    WHO, anemia fact sheetAbout 30% of non-pregnant women aged 15 to 49 and 40% of children aged 6 to 59 months have anemia; iron deficiency is the main nutritional causeGlobal data from 2019
    Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, 2016Well-planned vegan diets are adequate; vegetarians take in as much iron as omnivores, but tend to have lower storesOfficial position
  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: iron stores are lower

    The most serious version points out that, on average, vegetarians and vegans have lower iron stores (ferritin), and that this can be a risk for vulnerable groups, such as women of childbearing age.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Lower stores aren't deficiency.

    Lower ferritin within the normal range isn't anemia; what matters is monitoring.

    The risk can be monitored.

    Regular blood tests and, when needed, supplementation solve it.

    Vulnerable groups need attention with meat too.

    Anemia in women is common on every diet.

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    If iron and zinc can be obtained from plants with planning, the need to eat animals disappears. What remains is the moral question: does looking after one's own nutrition justify others being killed, when there are alternatives?

Contradictions

Doesn't worry about anemia in meat eaters.

The concern only comes up with vegans.

Eats beans.

One of the main sources of iron in many diets.

Accepts other points of attention in their own diet.

Every diet has risks that require care.

What the argument gets right

What is true

Iron and zinc deserve attention in vegan diets, and it's true that iron stores tend to be lower. Information about sources, combinations and blood tests is part of a responsible transition.

But

But attention isn't impossibility. With a varied diet, deficiency is preventable, and anemia remains a common problem among meat eaters too.
