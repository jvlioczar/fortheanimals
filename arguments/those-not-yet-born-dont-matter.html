N16

"Those not yet born don't matter"

The answer in one sentence

those not yet born feel nothing today, but they will feel what we leave them. An example: burying toxic waste today that will contaminate the water and poison children fifty years from now is wrong, even though those children don't exist yet. The harm is in the future, but it is real. When suffering happens doesn't change how much it hurts, and decisions made now, about technology, institutions and who counts morally, will affect immense numbers of beings, human and animal.

Note: this family takes the sentience criterion beyond exploitation. Many vegans disagree with parts of it, and the debate is legitimate. The test proposed in each chapter is simple: apply the same scenario to human beings and see whether the answer would be accepted; if it would not, that shows speciesism.

Short answer

Those not yet born feel nothing today, but they'll feel the consequences of what we do now. For example, burying toxic waste today that will contaminate the water and poison children decades from now is wrong: the harm is in the future, but it is real. The same goes for animals: today's decisions about farming, technology and who counts morally will affect billions of beings yet to be born.

For children

If you plant a tree today, the person who sits in its shade may be a child who hasn't even been born yet. And if you throw rubbish in the river today, the one who suffers may be someone who hasn't been born yet either. What we do now reaches those who come later, people and animals.

Ask a question back

"If we bury toxic waste today that will poison children fifty years from now, is that wrong even though they don't exist yet?"

Como pode aparecer

The strongest version

How it is said

"I care about the people who exist now. Let the future sort itself out."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Only beings that exist can have interests.
  2. P2Future beings don't exist yet; they're only possibilities.
  3. P3Possibilities can't be harmed.
  4. CSo we have no duties toward future beings.

P2 is true. The problem lies in P1 and P3: future beings, when they exist, will have real interests, and our actions today can harm them.

The argument is valid in form, but not sound: it confuses not existing yet with never being affected.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument confuses two things:

    ClaimStatus
    Future beings feel nothing todayTrue
    Future beings can't be harmed by what we do todayFalse: when they exist, they'll suffer the consequences

    Temporal impartiality is the idea that suffering doesn't matter less because it happens a hundred years from now, just as it doesn't matter less because it happens on another continent.

  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. The toxic waste case.

    Almost everyone agrees it's wrong to bury waste today that will contaminate the water and make sick whoever lives there in the future. The harm only happens later, but no one thinks it stops mattering for that reason. If future beings didn't matter, there'd be nothing wrong with it.

    2. Veganism already thinks about the future.

    Much of the impact of no longer buying animal products concerns animals not yet born, who will no longer be bred to suffer. Whoever accepts this already accepts that future beings matter.

    3. The human test.

    Climate policy, pensions, forest preservation and pandemic prevention are decisions taken today with people yet to be born in mind. No one considers them absurd. Applying the same reasoning to animals and other sentient beings is simply being consistent.

    4. Future numbers could be immense.

    If civilization lasts a long time, the number of sentient beings affected by today's decisions, humans, animals and perhaps artificial minds, could far exceed that of everyone who has ever lived.

    5. Thinking about the future doesn't require ignoring the present.

    Many actions that reduce future suffering, such as widening the moral circle and fighting speciesism, also help now.

    6. "Prioritise those who are here now" is a different claim, and it doesn't hold either.

    This version grants that future beings matter and says present ones come first. Two possible reasons are worth separating. The first is practical: the present is more certain, better known and easier to affect. That reason is a good one, but it is about effectiveness rather than value, and it doesn't justify ignoring a foreseeable, large future harm, such as the toxic waste. The second is pure time discounting: suffering would be worth less for being distant. That one fails the test of distance in space, which no one accepts — someone's pain ten thousand kilometres away is no smaller — and it leads to conclusions its own defenders reject, such as treating the warming of 2100 as nearly irrelevant. In practice the phrase arrives with the first reason and is used to conclude the second.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the future is too uncertain

    The most serious version says: even if future beings matter, we don't know what will happen or which actions today will have an effect. Focusing on the distant future would trade certain, present suffering for uncertain bets.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    It changes the subject.

    It moves from "do future beings matter?" to "can we help them?". The answer to the first is already yes.

    Some actions are worthwhile in almost every scenario.

    Spreading the idea that all sentient beings matter, strengthening institutions and preventing technologies that cause mass suffering are good bets in almost any future.

    Uncertainty isn't zero.

    We also act under uncertainty on climate and public health, and we don't stop planning because of it.

Reasoning errors

Logical

Is the form valid?

Equivocation
Confuses "doesn't exist now" with "won't be affected"

Moral

Is the criterion coherent?

Temporal partiality
Discounts suffering just because it's in the future
Double standard
Accepts thinking about the human future (climate, pensions) but not the animals'

Epistemological

Does the evidence support it?

Hyperbolic discounting
Gives exaggerated weight to the present because it's closer

Biases

Temporal discounting

What's far away in time seems worth less

Identifiable victim

Astronomical numbers stop moving us

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's right that present suffering is certain and urgent, that the future is uncertain, and that there's a risk of using the distant future as an excuse to ignore those suffering today. It's also right that there are hard philosophical debates about beings who may or may not come to exist.

But

But none of this makes future beings irrelevant. Suffering doesn't matter less because it happens a hundred years from now, and many of the decisions that will determine it are being made now.
