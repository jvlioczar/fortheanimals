I25

"Racehorses and sporting dogs live better than many people"

The answer in one sentence

expensive care is not the same as a good life for the animal, and the animal can't consent to being used as an athlete. A human athlete chooses the effort, which is very demanding, and can stop; the horse and the greyhound don't choose and may suffer even more. Racehorses and greyhounds are well fed and looked after while they earn, because they are investments; when they get injured or stop winning, the care ends. In Australia, the Coalition for the Protection of Racehorses recorded 168 horses killed on racetracks in the 2022/23 season, one every two days; in the United Kingdom, official greyhound track data recorded 123 deaths and 3,809 injuries in 2024. And comparing them with human poverty justifies nothing: poverty is an injustice to fix, not a yardstick for what may be done to animals.

Reply

That expensive care lasts while the animal earns: it's an investment. And a human athlete chooses a grueling effort and can stop; the horse and the greyhound don't consent and can't quit. Do you know how many die? In Australia, a group that monitors the industry recorded 168 horses killed in races in a single season, one every two days; on British greyhound tracks, there were 123 deaths and 3,809 injuries in 2024, according to official data. When they get injured or stop winning, many are discarded. And human poverty is an injustice to be corrected, not a yardstick for what may be done to animals. Is it fair to use an animal while it turns a profit and discard it afterwards?

For children

A racehorse gets good food and a doctor while it runs fast. But it didn't choose to race. A human athlete trains hard because they want to, and can quit whenever they like; the horse can't say no. It can get badly hurt on the track, and when it stops winning there's often no one left to look after it. Being treated well only while you're useful isn't the same as being loved.

Ask a question back

"If the care is out of love for the animal, what happens to it when it stops winning?"

How it can show up

The strongest version

How it is said

"A racehorse lives better than plenty of people: vets, good food, a clean stable. Nobody mistreats an animal worth millions."

Strongest version (steelman):

  1. P1Sporting horses and dogs get high-quality food, veterinary care and shelter.
  2. P2Many humans don't get that level of care.
  3. P3Whoever gets that care has a good life.
  4. CSo using animals in sport does them no harm; they live better than many people.

P1 is true while the animal earns. So is P2. P3 fails: the care serves performance, and the conclusion ignores the risks, the injuries and what happens after the career.

The argument is valid. Its soundness fails at P3, and the comparison in P2 is irrelevant to the conclusion.

Full answer

  1. a

    Reconstruction and diagnosis

    The argument only looks at the period when the animal is useful:

    StageWhat the animal lives through
    BreedingSelective breeding for speed; many animals are born and few get to race
    CareerIntense training, transport, competition, risk of fractures and other injuries
    Serious injuryOften euthanasia on the track itself
    End of careerSome are adopted; others are sold, used for breeding or slaughtered
  2. b

    Where the argument breaks

    1. Care is proportional to value.

    A valuable animal is well cared for for the same reason a racing car is well maintained: it earns. The care doesn't come from recognizing the animal's interests, and it ends when the earnings end.

    2. Racing kills.

    The Coalition for the Protection of Racehorses, which monitors Australian tracks in the absence of published official data, recorded 168 horse deaths in races in the 2022/23 season, the highest since the count began in 2014. The figures don't include deaths in training or the fate of retired horses.

    3. Official data confirm the risk.

    In the United States and Canada, the industry's own injury database (The Jockey Club's Equine Injury Database) recorded 1.11 deaths per thousand starts in 2024, the lowest rate since 2009; the rate refers to races, not training. In the United Kingdom, official data from the greyhound racing authority (GBGB) recorded 123 dogs killed on the tracks and 3,809 injured in 2024, about a quarter of the roughly 15,000 greyhounds that raced. The industry stresses that rates are falling; even so, every race imposes a risk the animal never accepted.

    4. Governments recognize the problem.

    New Zealand passed, with support from several parties, the end of greyhound racing, citing animal welfare; the last commercial race was held in July 2026.

    5. The comparison with humans is irrelevant.

    That many people live in poverty is an injustice to be fought, not a standard for what may be done to others. "Others have it worse" has never justified a harm (J20).

    6. The animal didn't choose, and can't stop.

    Even a well-treated animal was bred, trained and put at risk for other people's profit and entertainment. The comparison with human athletes shows the difference: an elite athlete's effort is very demanding and often painful, but they choose it, understand the risks, negotiate contracts, can turn down a race and can retire. The horse and the greyhound don't know why they run, can't weigh the risk or say no, and so may suffer even more: they bear the same strain without any of the protections that consent provides.

  4. d

    The strongest counter-reply: the industry has welfare rules and adoption programs

    The most serious version acknowledges the risks and points out that there are rules on whip use, veterinary checks and programs to rehabilitate and rehome retired animals, which improve every year.

  5. e

    Why the counter-reply doesn't reach the conclusion

    Improving isn't making acceptable.

    The rules reduce part of the harm, but the activity still requires animals to take risks for entertainment and betting.

    The programs don't reach all of them.

    The number of animals bred to compete exceeds the capacity for adoption.

    The question remains.

    Why breed animals to run that risk, if the goal is entertainment and betting?

  6. f

    Back to the moral question

    The question isn't whether the animal eats well while it races, but whether it's fair to breed it, risk its life and discard it for entertainment and profit. Good care during use doesn't change the fact that it is being used.

Logical

Is the form valid?

Irrelevant comparison

Epistemological

Selective sample
It looks only at animals at their peak and ignores injuries and discarding

Moral

Contradictions

Says the animal is treated like royalty.

And accepts that it's put down on the track when injured.

Uses human poverty as an argument.

Without proposing anything to fix it.

Would reject the same with people.

A well-paid athlete discarded after an injury would be a victim of abuse.

What the argument gets right

What is true

It's true that many sporting horses and dogs get high-quality care, and that many people who work with them like the animals. It's also true that some people live in worse conditions, and that this is unacceptable.

But

But the two facts don't lead to the conclusion: the care depends on earnings, the risk is real, and human poverty calls for justice for people, not a license to use animals.
