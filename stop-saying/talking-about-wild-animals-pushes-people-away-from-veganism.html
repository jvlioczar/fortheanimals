V31

"Talking about wild animals pushes people away from veganism."

Objection in disguise

They look like concern for strategy or for the animals, but become a ready excuse to change nothing.

Why it is weak

It looks like strategic calculation, but it is a prediction about facts, and those facts have not been measured. No study shows that talking about the suffering of wild animals reduces uptake of veganism or sympathy for animals. The philosopher Luciano Carlos Cunha, who recommends care in how the subject is presented, acknowledges that there are no data on this effect.

The research that exists points the other way. In a Rethink Priorities study with 830 people in the United Kingdom, 90% said they care about wild animals in pain, whether from human or natural causes, and support for helping was high for measures such as providing water and food, vaccinating and treating sick animals. Another study, with 1,371 people in the United States, showed that how the subject is framed changes support, in different directions depending on the question. That calls for care with framing, not silence.

Even as strategy, the calculation has another side: silence has a cost too, and that cost is certain. Suffering from natural processes stays untouched while no one argues for reducing it, and prejudices questioned early lose their force sooner. Supporting this help also asks little of each person, because interventions would fall to professionals.

And there is an ethical reason: the sentience that grounds veganism doesn't stop mattering when the harm comes from nature rather than from us. The same test as for the slavery comparison applies: if it were shown that talking about the subject helps animals, would you start supporting it? If the answer is no, the objection was never strategic.

How to say it better

Separate framing from content: choose where to start and whom to talk to, begin with examples already accepted, such as rescuing animals from fires or vaccinating against rabies, and measure the effect. If the concern is strategic, show the evidence; if it is moral, argue it as moral.

The concern about framing is legitimate, and Cunha himself recommends not opening the conversation with the most controversial points. The literature on the radical flank effect finds both harms and gains for moderate groups. What is missing is a basis for claiming that talking about the subject pushes people away, not a reason to think about how to talk about it.
