V5

"Superbugs come from the antibiotics given to animals."

Borrowed reason

They talk about health, hunger or the planet instead of the animals. When the borrowed reason falls, the motivation falls with it.

Why it is weak

It has a real basis. According to an estimate published in Science in 2019, about 73% of the antimicrobials sold worldwide are used in animals raised for food, often healthy ones, to prevent disease in crowded conditions or speed up growth. This favours resistant bacteria, which can reach people through food, contact and the environment. The mcr-1 gene, which confers resistance to colistin, a last-resort antibiotic, was identified in 2015 in pigs and chickens in China.

But the phrase exaggerates. The main driver of resistance in human infections is antibiotic use in medicine itself, often unnecessary, and no one knows precisely how much resistance comes from animals. Blaming it all on animal farming costs credibility with people who know the subject.

And it's a borrowed reason: animal farming can cut antibiotic use sharply without ceasing to exploit animals. The European Union banned their use as growth promoters in 2006, the Netherlands cut sales for animals by about 70% since 2009, and meat is already sold as "raised without antibiotics". The animals remain confined and killed.

How to say it better

Say that animal farming is the world's largest consumer of antimicrobials and contributes to antimicrobial resistance, which the World Health Organization considers one of the greatest threats to public health and which was directly responsible for about 1.27 million deaths in 2019. The WHO recommends ending antibiotic use in healthy animals. It's one more reason against intensive farming, but the main one is still what happens to the animals.

Preventive antibiotic use also reveals something about farming: to a large extent, it is only needed because the animals live in conditions that make them sick. There is a true public health prevention point here: reducing antibiotic use in farming, and intensive farming itself, lowers the pressure that selects for resistant bacteria. That is what the WHO's 2017 guidelines and the One Health approach recommend, and it is a strong argument for public policy, as long as it doesn't take the place of the main reason, which is the animals.
