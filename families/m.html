M. Ex-vegans: why I gave up

10 arguments in Part IND.

Underlying question: Does having given up change what is right?
Dominant error: Logical and moral (from personal experience to a moral conclusion)

Family of indirect argumentsThe answer to this argument is indirect. Tap to see why it isn't enough, and how to use it better.

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about the experience of someone who tried to be vegan and gave up, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism depended on being easy for everyone, one person's difficulty would be enough to make it stop counting. But someone's difficulty, which deserves to be heard and helped, doesn't change what happens to the animals: it calls for practical support, not a new moral conclusion.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

Arguments in this family

M1

I stopped being vegan because I started feeling unwell

The discomfort is real but rarely investigated: it usually comes from eating too little, lack of B12 or poor planning, and can be solved without animal products

Full chapterM2

I was vegan for my health, and I changed my mind

Changing your mind about health does not answer the reason concerning animals, which may have existed and lost strength, or never been examined

Full chapterM3

Being vegan limited me when eating at my relatives' homes

The social cost is real, but there are ways through: talking beforehand, bringing a dish to share, eating what is already vegan on the table

Full chapterM4

Being vegan is hard if you travel a lot

The difficulty of some meals justifies, at most, a genuine exception, not giving up veganism at home

Full chapterM5

I stopped being vegan because I'm at a different stage of life

A stage of life changes what is easy, not what happens to the animals; a hard stage calls for adjustments, not for giving up

Full chapterM6

Vegans are annoying and bossy; I don't want to be part of that group

Confuses the principle with the group: you can leave hostile people without ceasing to spare animals

Full chapterM7

I don't want to be labeled; the label brings pressure

Confuses the label with the practice: the name is optional, and what reaches the animals is what one does

Full chapterM8

It became an obsession; I lived with guilt

False dichotomy between total purity and giving up: veganism asks for what is possible and practicable, not perfection

Full chapterM9

I was vegan and started getting too radical, too rigid

The rigidity comes from how veganism is lived, not from the principle; the way can change without giving up the reason concerning animals

Full chapterM10

I used to be vegan; I know what I'm talking about

Having been vegan gives experience of how it was for that person, not authority on the moral question
