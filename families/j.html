J. Practicality, economy and society

22 arguments in Part IND.

Underlying question: Is it feasible and fair to ask this?
Dominant error: Moral (priority) and empirical

Family of indirect arguments

This is an indirect argument, and the answer to it is indirect too. It is about cost, practicality and the economy, not about whether animals deserve respect. The underlying question in the defense of animals runs in parallel to this one: it is sentience, the capacity animals have to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics).

Many activists question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what matters. Other movements are not defended this way: no one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy. The harm done to the victims is already the reason.

Why it is not good to use it, or why always to pair it with an ethical argument: If veganism were defended for being cheap, practical or good for the economy, any situation in which exploitation were cheaper or created more jobs would justify it. No one accepts this reasoning when the victims are human: exploiting people was once profitable too.

Indirect arguments also do not lead to veganism on their own. Often they support, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

So discuss the facts as facts: correct what is wrong and acknowledge what is true. But do not treat the answer as a moral foundation, because what is does not, on its own, get you to what ought to be: even a real benefit would not show it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. To really answer, show that the moral debate is a different one: speciesism and what happens to animals as individuals. Learn why

Veganism is expensive

Poor people have no access

A real barrier for some, which doesn't apply to the person objecting; and many cultures have old vegetarian traditions

Farmers and slaughterhouse workers would lose their jobs

An argument used against every moral reform; transitions can be planned

Full chapterJ4

Agriculture is the backbone of the economy

Economic importance is not a moral justification; it ignores subsidies and external costs

Full chapterJ5

What would we do with the animals if everyone went vegan?

A gradual change reduces births; the problem is created by breeding itself

Full chapterJ6

There are more important human problems

Priority fallacy: eating plants doesn't take time from any other cause

Full chapterJ7

Veganism is a political ideology or a globalist agenda

Guilt by association and conspiracy theory; it doesn't assess the argument

Full chapterJ9

It's hard; I don't have time

Initial difficulty isn't impossibility; inconvenience doesn't justify harm

Full chapterJ10

Vegan food is bland

A generalization from unfamiliarity; most spices and many dishes are already plant-based

Full chapterJ11

Meat tastes good

Taste as a justification for killing: no other context accepts this

Full chapterJ12

I eat little meat; that's enough

Reducing is better than nothing, but it doesn't justify what is left

Full chapterJ13

Eating out, traveling and parties become impossible

Exaggerates the social cost; options keep growing and adapting is quick

Full chapterJ14

Fishers and ranchers would lose their identity

Identity doesn't justify harm to others; it calls for a just transition

Full chapterJ15

A vegan world is utopian

Confuses full, immediate feasibility with a present individual duty

Full chapterJ16

My food beats yours

A taunt, not an argument: the pleasure of taste doesn't weigh against someone's life, and much vegan food is the same food as always

Full chapterJ17

Look what you're missing

Showing the plate doesn't answer the question about who became the plate; temptation doesn't make the act just

Full chapterJ18

This is my living; it feeds my family

Economic dependence explains why change is hard, but it does not make the activity just; the way out is a planned, supported transition

Full chapterJ19

Vegans are against farmers

Treats farming as a single block: veganism opposes breeding and killing animals, not crop growing, which is where vegan food comes from

Full chapterJ20

I don't want to feel left out; eating together is how you belong

Belonging doesn't require eating the same dish, and the social discomfort of refusing doesn't compare with the cost to the animal

Full chapterJ21

Veganism has become big business

Genetic fallacy: a moral position doesn't become false because someone profits from it, and the business of exploiting animals is far bigger

Full chapterJ22

Billions of people depend on fish for protein

The FAO figure refers to 20% of animal protein, not of protein needs, and the dependence of some doesn't justify consumption by those who have a choice

Full chapterJ23

People are dying in a war and you're worried about animals

The animals are victims of the same war; the cost of helping them is far smaller than the sentence assumes, and a cost existing decides nothing, since that holds for every use of resources
