This guide gathers the arguments used to justify animal exploitation or reject veganism, organized so you can find the answer you need without reading everything.

The underlying criterion

If a being is sentient, that is, can be harmed or benefited by what happens to it, its interests count morally. A justification for ignoring those interests is only valid if it survives three tests: logical coherence, empirical support and consistency when applied to equivalent human cases.

Five commitments

  1. Intellectual honesty: present the strongest version of each argument, acknowledge what it gets right and make no empirical claim without a source.
  2. Rigor on three levels: logical (is the argument valid?), epistemological (do the premises have evidential support?) and moral (is the criterion coherent and relevant?).
  3. Sentientism: sentience is the founding criterion of moral inclusion. Species, intelligence, usefulness and human affection are not.
  4. Antispeciesism and justice: similar interests deserve similar consideration, whatever the species of whoever has them.
  5. Parsimony: prefer the simplest criterion that explains our firmest moral judgments, without exceptions tailored to protect habits.

Knocking down straw men would weaken the guide and make it what it criticizes. That is why the strongest version of each argument comes before the answer.

How to use it

  • To debate: find the objection by its code or with the search and follow the map of connections to anticipate the next one. The letter is the family and the number is its position within it.
  • To recognize: the "How it can show up" block shows the other ways the argument is usually said or written.
  • To teach: use the answer for children and the short one. The technical layers are for those who want to go deeper. A class exercise: list acts that are clearly wrong, clearly permissible and doubtful, ask what makes those on the first list wrong, and only then ask whether it would be permissible to treat humans the way we treat animals on farms, in laboratories or in the fur industry (suggested by Nathan Nobis in Animals & Ethics 101, pp. 13–14).
  • To debate someone who starts from a political theory: the political objections show, theory by theory, why excluding animals contradicts each one's principle.
  • To research: the fallacies and biases indexes let you cross the catalogue by type of error.

Direct and indirect arguments

An argument is direct (DIR) when one of its premises is about the good or harm of animals, or about whether it is just to harm them: whether they feel, whether the individual counts, whether using or killing them is wrong. It is also direct when it invokes tradition, nature or freedom to justify the harm. It is indirect (IND) when its premises are about something else: it claims exploitation is necessary for health, that veganism is unfeasible, expensive, bad for the environment or ineffective, or that those who defend it deserve no credit. The classification applies to each argument, and some families bring together both kinds.

Even if an indirect argument were true, it would justify only exceptions, never exploitation in general. That is why Part DIR comes first.

Why indirect arguments are not enough

Indirect arguments do not discuss whether it is right to exploit animals. They discuss health, the environment, the economy, practicality or the credibility of those who defend veganism. Many activists and animal rights philosophers question using this kind of argument to defend animals, because it sidesteps what is important: respect for the animals themselves.

Other social movements do not rely on indirect arguments to defend humans. No one makes the case for anti-racism by saying it is good for the environment, for the health of white people or for the economy, and people fighting for the rights of minorities and of those who are harmed do not need to prove any of that: the fact that they are harmed is reason enough. Even so, veganism is often defended with health or environmental arguments, as if the end of animal exploitation were not reason enough in itself.

An indirect argument runs in parallel to the underlying question, which is sentience: the capacity of animals to be harmed and benefited (see what supports animal ethics). That is why it does not lead to veganism on its own either. Often it supports, at most, vegetarianism, eating less or environmentalism, which accept the uses of animals seen as healthy, cheap or low-impact.

Indirect arguments are useful on the level of facts: they show that a practical objection is false, that vegan living is healthy and possible, that animal farming causes harm. But on their own they do not justify the morality of respecting animals. And they can even pull the other way: "sustainable" leather, "sustainable" hunting, organic meat, insect farming and genetically modified animals are defended in the name of the environment and keep or increase exploitation (H15). That is why each chapter in Part IND answers on the argument's own ground, corrects the facts and brings the conversation back to the moral question.

When health, protein or the environment are used to justify eating animals, the reasoning usually fails on two levels. On the level of facts, because the premises are often false: a well-planned plant-based diet meets our needs and causes less environmental harm. On the moral level, because it leaps from what is to what ought to be, the move philosophers call the is-ought fallacy: even if eating animals brought some benefit, that would not show it is just to make them suffer and die to obtain it. Showing the facts is always necessary; stopping there is never enough. See also the difference between direct and indirect arguments and answers.

Arguments and answers: both can be direct or indirect

Direct and indirect describe what an argument claims, not how it is answered. The answer can also be direct, when it is about what happens to the animals and whether that is just, or indirect, when it talks about health, the environment, the economy or practicality. That gives four situations:

  • Direct argument, direct answer. This is the main path. To "we have always done this", the answer is that tradition does not make a harm just, as the history of so many abandoned practices shows (D1).
  • Direct argument, indirect answer. This changes the subject. Answering "eating meat is natural" with "red meat is bad for you" leaves the objection standing and suggests that, if meat were healthy, eating animals would be acceptable.
  • Indirect argument, indirect answer. This corrects the facts: to "we need animal protein", the answer is that a well-planned plant-based diet meets our needs (G2). It is useful, but not enough.
  • Indirect argument, direct answer. This completes the previous one: even if there were a benefit, it would not show that it is just to make someone suffer and die to obtain it. That is why every chapter on an indirect argument ends by bringing the conversation back to the moral question.

Indirect answers, used as a defense of veganism, are rejected by many activists and animal rights philosophers, because they set aside respect for the animals themselves and can lead to opposite conclusions, such as "sustainable" meat or insect farming. The guide uses them only to correct facts, and always alongside a direct answer. The Stop saying this page gathers examples of indirect answers that tend to backfire, such as "go vegan for the planet".

How to analyze an argument

Before answering, reconstruct the argument: write out the premises and the conclusion so that the conclusion follows from them. Three rules help:

  1. Pin down the quantity. Is the claim about all, some, or which ones? If "animals don't feel pain" is about all of them, a single counterexample brings it down; if it is only about some, it says nothing about the rest.
  2. Clarify ambiguous words. "Natural", "necessary", "person" and "right" tend to change meaning halfway through a conversation. Fix one meaning and keep it to the end (otherwise it is equivocation).
  3. Write out the hidden premise. The everyday phrase almost always leaves out the principle that leads to the conclusion. Writing it down shows where the disagreement lies, and the "Extrapolations" block of each chapter tests that principle. To practice, use the "Find the hidden premise" mode in Practice answers.

Describing is not evaluating

It is common to hear phrases like "from a strictly biological point of view" or "from an ecological point of view", followed by a conclusion about what is right. But biology and ecology have no moral point of view, just as mathematics doesn't. They describe and explain: that the lion hunts, that a population grows when it loses its predators, that the human body digests meat and plants. Saying that something is good, bad, permitted or wrong is attributing a value, and that step requires a moral premise, which does not come from the description. This is the distinction the philosopher David Hume drew between what is and what ought to be; jumping from one to the other is the is-ought fallacy, also called Hume’s law, and, when the fact invoked is nature, the appeal to nature (see C1 and C5).

This does not diminish science. Facts matter, and they often settle the discussion, such as whether an animal feels pain. But science informs ethics; it does not replace it. It is also worth noting that some terms used in science already carry a value, such as "pest", "invasive species" or "ecosystem health": they describe an effect from the standpoint of some human or environmental interest, and say nothing, by themselves, about what is owed to the animals who live there.

Necessary and sufficient conditions

A condition is sufficient when it is enough: if X, then Y. It is necessary when without it there is no Y: only if X, then Y. Those who defend animals usually propose a sufficient condition for someone to count morally, such as being sentient. Those who defend animal use usually propose demanding necessary conditions, such as reason, language or duties, and then have to account for the humans who lack them, such as babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities (see A15 and A18). A sufficient condition need not be the only one: saying that sentience is enough does not deny that other things count too.

Permissible, obligatory, forbidden and beyond duty

"Right" is an ambiguous word. An action is permissible when it is not wrong; obligatory when not doing it is wrong; forbidden when doing it is wrong; and supererogatory when it is good and admirable but goes beyond duty. It is worth asking which one is meant: "eating meat isn't wrong" says it is permissible; "I admire vegans, but it isn't for everyone" treats veganism as something beyond duty. The guide holds that ceasing to fund avoidable harm is a duty, not a heroic gesture (see E10 and E14).

Testing a principle with counterexamples

A general principle, such as "only those with duties have rights", falls to a single case that meets its condition and contradicts its conclusion: a counterexample. The method has three steps: identify the principle the argument uses, look for a case in which it gives a result the person themselves rejects, and ask them to revise the principle or point out the difference between the cases. That is what the "Extrapolations" block of each chapter does: it applies the principle to equivalent human cases, such as those of babies and people with profound cognitive disabilities.

Starting from the firmest judgments

Another route starts from moral judgments on which almost everyone agrees, such as that racism and sexism are wrong, or that the Tuskegee study, in which the U.S. Public Health Service followed Black men with syphilis from 1932 to 1972 without treating them, even after penicillin became available, was a crime. It then asks which principle best explains those judgments. This is an inference to the best explanation applied to morality. Peter Singer reaches equal consideration of interests this way, and Tom Regan reaches the rights of those who are subjects of a life. If the principle that best explains the human cases also applies to many animals, three consistent options remain: accept the consequence for animals, revise the judgment about humans, or point out a relevant difference between the cases. That is what the commitment to parsimony asks for.

When the conclusion becomes a premise

Some objections start from the point in dispute: "experimenting on animals is permissible; if Regan's theory were true, it would be wrong; so the theory is false". The argument is valid, but the first premise is exactly what is being debated. Used without reasons, it assumes what it should prove, as in begging the question. The answer is to ask: "why is that premise true?". The same goes for "we have a right to eat animals", which, without some further reason, only repeats that eating animals is not wrong. And every valid argument can be turned around: someone who rejects the conclusion can reject one of the premises. Between the two sides, whoever has the better-supported premises wins (see A8 and C10).

The three rules and the distinctions in this section follow Nathan Nobis, Animals & Ethics 101 (Open Philosophy Press, 2016), pp. 7–12, 29–31, 33, 39 and 40.

The 11 blocks of a full chapter

  1. Formulation: the everyday phrase and the strongest version, with numbered premises
  2. How it can show up: other forms of the same argument
  3. Answer for children: language for ages 8 to 10, without guilt or shock
  4. Reply: what to say to the person, with the central flaw and a question to ask back
  5. Full answer: validity, evidence, the strongest counter-reply and why it fails too
  6. Connections: what usually comes before and after
  7. Reasoning errors: logical, epistemological and moral
  8. Contradictions: where the argument clashes with the person's other beliefs
  9. Biases: the psychological mechanisms that make it convincing
  10. Extrapolations: what would follow if the principle applied to humans, a test by counterexample
  11. What the argument gets right: the honest concessions

The "Extrapolations" block is a test by counterexample: it shows what the argument's principle would allow us to do to humans (see how to test a principle).
