Political and ethical objections

What each ethical or political theory usually says to leave animals out, and why that exclusion contradicts the theory's own principle.

Every ethical or political theory starts from a principle: protect individual freedom, end exploitation, reduce suffering, treat like cases alike, care for the vulnerable. For most of history, these theories applied their principle only to humans. This section gathers the objections that usually come from each of them and shows why excluding animals does not follow from the principle, but from a cut made before it. The point is not that every theory says the same thing, but that none of them offers, on its own terms, a reason to leave out those who can be harmed.

The theories

Liberalism

"Freedom is a right of rational individuals; animals aren't subjects of liberal rights."

Protect individuals against harm caused by others and ensure each can live their own life. For the English philosopher John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), one of the leading names of liberalism, the only legitimate reason to limit someone's freedom is to prevent harm to others. This is the harm principle, which he sets out in On Liberty (1859).

Libertarianism and anarcho-capitalism

"What's mine is mine: animals are property, and the freedom to use property is sacred."

Self-ownership and the non-aggression principle: no one may initiate violence against another individual.

Conservatism

"Eating meat is tradition, and conservatives preserve what has worked; animal rights are a modern invention."

Preserve the institutions, customs and bonds that have proved valuable over time, change with prudence and cultivate duties toward those who depend on us.

Marxism, socialism and communism

"Exploitation is a relation between human classes. Animals aren't workers, and the struggle is a different one."

End exploitation and the turning of beings and lives into commodities.

Anarchism

"Anarchism fights domination between people; animals are not part of that."

Reject hierarchies and forms of domination that cannot be justified.

Utilitarianism

"Human pleasures are worth more: better to be a human dissatisfied than a pig satisfied."

Maximize well-being and minimize suffering for everyone affected, counting each one's interest equally.

Contractualism and Rawlsian justice

"Justice holds among those who have a sense of justice and can make agreements."

Choose the rules of a society impartially, as if we did not know what position we would occupy in it.

Deontology and Kantianism

"Only rational beings are ends in themselves; toward animals we have, at most, indirect duties."

Treat beings who have value of their own as ends, and never merely as means.

Communitarianism

"Duties arise from the community and its traditions, and animals are not members of it."

Morality is built in the bonds and practices of a community.

Egalitarianism and prioritarianism

"Equality is among human citizens; animals belong to another category."

Reduce unjust inequalities and give priority to those who are worst off.

Ethics of care

"Care applies to close relationships, not to unknown animals."

Respond to the needs of the vulnerable and the dependent with attention and responsibility.

Virtue ethics

"Virtue is about human character; treating animals well matters only because it makes us better."

Cultivate virtues like compassion, justice and temperance, and avoid vices like cruelty.

Feminism

"Feminism is about women; talking about cows and hens is changing the subject, or even offensive."

End oppression linked to sex and control over the body and reproduction.

Anti-racism and anti-ableism

"Comparing speciesism to racism or ableism is offensive; they are different things."

Do not give less weight to someone's interests because of a trait that does not change how much they can be harmed, such as race, disability, origin or appearance.

The progressive left and multiculturalism

"The animal cause is a white middle-class concern that ends up attacking the cultural practices of minorities."

Defend vulnerable groups, fight domination and respect cultural diversity.

Pacifism and nonviolence

"Nonviolence is a commitment between people, not to animals."

Refuse violence as a means of resolving conflicts and reaching goals.

Effective altruism

"Resources for doing good are limited, and should go to humans first."

Use evidence and reason to do the most good possible with the resources available.

Environmentalism

"What matters is saving the planet, ecosystems and species, not each individual animal."

Protect the environment and the planet: conserve ecosystems, biodiversity and natural resources, and secure living conditions for present and future generations.

Pragmatism

"Politics is the art of the possible: the animal cause is not viable, wins no votes, and there are more practical problems to solve."

Judge ideas and policies by their practical consequences, not by loyalty to a doctrine; test, look at the results and correct course.

Stoicism

"Animals lack reason, so there is no justice between us and them; the Stoic tends to their own virtue."

Virtue is the only good: live in accordance with reason and nature, with justice, courage, temperance and wisdom, and accept what does not depend on us.

Natural law and Thomism

"In the natural order, animals exist for human use; killing them for food is legitimate."

Do good and avoid evil, knowing through reason the basic goods of human nature, such as life, knowledge and community, and never attacking them directly.

Religious ethics and divine command

"God gave humans dominion over animals; if He allowed it, there is nothing wrong with eating them."

What is right is what God commands, or what agrees with God's good nature, known through scripture and tradition.

Discourse ethics

"A moral norm is valid if everyone accepts it in rational dialogue; animals cannot take part in dialogue, so they are left out."

A norm is valid only if everyone affected by it could accept it in a free dialogue, in which only the force of the better argument counts. This is the proposal of the German philosophers Jürgen Habermas and Karl-Otto Apel.

Ethical egoism and Objectivism

"Everyone should pursue their own interest; I owe nothing to animals if I gain nothing from it."

Each person should act in their own rational interest. In Ayn Rand's Objectivism, one's own life is the standard of value, and rights protect the freedom of action of rational beings.

Moral relativism and subjectivism

"Morality is relative: each culture and each person decides what is right, and no one can say that eating meat is wrong."

There are no universal moral truths: right and wrong depend on culture (cultural relativism), on each person's attitudes (subjectivism) or on emotions and preferences (emotivism).

Humanism and anthropocentrism

"I'm a humanist: human dignity comes first, and putting animals on the same level demeans humans."

Value human dignity, reason and well-being, and base ethics on reason, empathy and evidence, not on dogma.

From left to right

It is common to see people on the right dismiss veganism as a left-wing thing, and people on the left call veganism classist and elitist. Both accusations can't be right at once, and neither answers the question about the animals. You don't need to change political sides to be vegan. You only need to take seriously the values each side already holds. The values below are the ones usually associated with each camp, and none of them belongs to one side alone.

If you identify with the left

ValueWhy it leads to considering animals
EqualityIt asks that similar interests get similar weight. Excluding those who feel just because of their species creates an unjustified inequality. See the theory →
Fighting exploitationAnimal farming uses the bodies and lives of those who can neither resist nor bargain, on a scale of billions a year. See the theory →
Protecting the vulnerableAnimals are the extreme case of vulnerability: they don't vote, speak or organize. See the theory →
Critique of dominationIf no hierarchy is natural just because it exists, the hierarchy between species also has to justify itself. See the theory →

If you identify with the right

ValueWhy it leads to considering animals
Individual libertyOne person's liberty ends where harm to another begins. An animal is an individual with interests, and cannot consent to being used. See the theory →
Non-aggression and legitimate propertyWhoever holds that no one may be attacked or taken as property without consent has reasons to extend this to those who feel. See the theory →
Personal responsibilityVeganism is each person's consumer choice and doesn't depend on the state: it's the person taking responsibility for the consequences of what they buy. See the theory →
Tradition, family and characterMany traditions value compassion toward animals, and caring for those who depend on us is a virtue, not a fad. See the theory →
Free markets and innovationThe market already offers plant-based alternatives and invests in cultivated meat. Choosing them is using market freedom in favor of animals. See the theory →

The two sides may disagree about the means, such as laws, markets or cultural change, and evidence helps choose what works best. But on each side's own terms, there is no good reason to leave animals out.

The section draws on the book An Introduction to Animals and Political Theory, by Alasdair Cochrane, the article Animal Rights, Multiculturalism, and the Left, by Will Kymlicka and Sue Donaldson, and two essays by Julio Cesar Prava, revised: the claims were checked and the analogies adjusted so as not to say more than the evidence allows.
